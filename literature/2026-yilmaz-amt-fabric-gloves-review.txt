ABSTRACT 
Fabrics have evolved beyond passive materials into dynamic, programmable structures that function as artificial skins, 
driving advancements in wearable soft robotics. Fabric-based robotic gloves mimic the biomechanics of the human hand by 
combining softness, tactile adaptability, multiaxial flexibility, and shape-morphing capability, offering a compliant alternative 
to traditional rigid systems. While these systems show significant potential for assistive mobility and home-based therapy, 
their transition to real-world applications necessitates addressing complex interdisciplinary challenges. This review provides a 
comprehensive analysis of the current landscape and future outlook of fabric-based robotic exoskeleton gloves, critically examining 
technological advancements across multiple dimensions, including design and fabrication techniques, actuation mechanisms, 
sensor integration, control architectures, and power and energy requirements. Performance is evaluated through biomechanical 
testing, torque and grip assessments, as well as comfort-related studies. Key issues, such as durability, home-based usability, and 
material sustainability, are explored alongside the challenges of safety and ethical deployment. By identifying current limitations 
and research gaps, this review proposes a roadmap to advance fabric-based exoskeleton gloves toward intelligent, scalable, and 
materials-driven textile robotic systems designed for real-world rehabilitation and assistive use. 
1 Introduction 
The human hand functions as a complex biomechanical system. 
It enables intricate cognitive tasks such as communication, 
expression, and environmental perception, in addition to grasp- 
ing and manipulating objects [ 1–4 ]. Seemingly routine tasks 
in daily life, such as writing, eating, or carrying an object, 
are facilitated by the coordinated mechanics of this anatomical 
structure [ 5, 6 ]. The functional integrity of the hand is therefore 
directly linked to personal autonomy and overall quality of 
life. However, neurological or musculoskeletal disorders such as 
stroke, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injuries, and 
peripheral nerve damage severely limit this functionality, causing 
loss of mobility, psychosocial isolation, and dependency in daily 
living skills [ 7, 8 ]. Data from the World Health Organization 
and UNICEF project that 2.5 billion people worldwide need 
assistive technology, a number expected to reach 3.5 billion by 
2050 [ 9 ]. The rapid increase in this number shows that the field 
of materials science has a social responsibility to find a solution 
to this problem. However, the solution to the problem includes 
more than just external rigid mechanical support. The solution 
lies in how this support is integrated into the body, how it 
adapts to the tissue, and most importantly, how it is perceived by 
the user. Among the various soft robotic exoskeleton solutions,

Exoskeleton systems are biomechanical structures worn on the 
dorsal surface of the hand and fingers to provide external 
movement support [ 10–13 ]. The actuators (motion-enabling com- 
ponents) within the system transmit force not directly to the 
muscles or bones, but first to the skin and soft tissue, and then 
to the joints [ 14, 15 ]. Therefore, the actuator material must be 
able to both effectively transmit force and work in harmony 
with the skin and tissue. The bulky and rigid plastic or metal 
components utilized in current support systems compromise 
biomechanical compatibility in favor of functionality [ 16 ]. This is 
because natural hand movements, such as finger flexion and the 
micro-deformations of the palm during grasping, rely not only 
on force but also on flexible mechanical behaviors that permit 
multiaxial and multidirectional deformations [ 17, 18 ]. To address 
these requirements, soft materials such as elastomers [ 19–23 ], 
liquid crystal elastomers [ 24, 25 ], shape memory alloys (SMAs) 
[ 26, 27 ], and hydrogels have been used in conjunction with 
various actuation mechanisms, including pneumatic, thermal, 
electrostatic, and magnetic ones [ 28, 29 ]. Among the materials 
studied, elastomers have been the most extensively researched. 
However, these materials exhibit limitations such as unidirec- 
tional or isotropic deformation behavior, thermal instability, 
oxidation propensity, and a lack of long-term comfort. Therefore, 
softness alone is insufficient for determining the most suitable 
material. 
Textile-based materials offer distinct advantages that address the 
limitations of other soft materials [ 30 ]. Once restricted to passive 
substrate roles in wearable robotic exoskeletons [ 31 ], textiles 
have evolved into active functional components, valued for their 
breathability, light weight, skin-like mechanical behavior, and 
multidirectional deformability [ 32 ]. Among fibers, yarns, and 
fabrics [ 33–35 ], fabric-based gloves have emerged as the predomi- 
nant exoskeleton platform due to their mechanical and structural 
advantages. Accordingly, recent studies have predominantly 
focused on this format, where deformation at the fiber and yarn 
scale is transmitted through the fabric architecture to the overall 
actuator system [ 36–38 ]. They offer a broad range of flexibility 
and multidirectional stretchability, enabling the generation of the 
hand’s complex movements. The term “fabric-based exoskeleton 
glove” is used because the fundamental movement and support 
mechanism of these exoskeleton gloves is based on fabric-based 
actuators, since fabric-based actuators are widely featured in the 
literature as the main component of such soft robotic systems. 
Fabrics produced using knitted, woven, or nonwoven techniques 
enable not only the transmission of force but also its direction 
and regional modulation through features such as anisotropic 
deformation, layered structure design, and local stiffness adjust- 
ment [ 39–41 ]. For example, the yarn orientation and geometry 
of a knitted fabric, while allowing precise finger movements 
with low modulus initial flexibility, can limit movement by 
creating resistance with increasing deformation. Fabric-based 
materials can offer multiple functionalities in a single substrate by 
integrating actuators, sensors, and energy transmission lines into 
their fiber structure [ 1, 42 ]. In this context, textiles are not just 
structural materials; they are also positioned as active-material 
systems that can be shaped according to finger biomechanics 
and enable programmable motions. Therefore, the advancement 
sought in exoskeleton technologies is not merely “softness” but 
also adaptive fabric architectures engineered with mechanical 
intelligence.

2 The Human Hand: Anatomy and 
Biomechanics as Design Inspiration 
Understanding the biomechanical function of the fingers and 
the fundamental anatomy of the human hand is essential for 
designing robotic glove systems that are effective, safe, and 
ergonomic [ 185 ]. A healthy human hand is expected to fulfill 
the functions of mobility, stability, and strength. Any movement 
limitations in the shoulder, elbow, forearm, or wrist can directly 
affect the hand’s position in space, grip strength, and daily living 
activities [ 205 ]. However, this article focuses on the anatomy 
and biomechanics of the hand, rather than on the adjacent 
upper limb. The human hand consists of four fingers and a 
thumb. The finger joints, excluding the thumb, comprise the 
metacarpophalangeal (MCP), proximal interphalangeal (PIP), 
and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints, which together enable 
coordinated flexion–extension movements. In the thumb, the 
carpometacarpal (CMC) joint is the most proximal joint and 
is capable of multiaxial movement and opposition. The MCP 
in the thumb is the middle joint, controlling the thumb’s 
orientation and its basic flexion–extension movements. The 
interphalangeal (IP) joint is the distal joint of the thumb, which 
enables precise grasping and fine motor skills, as illustrated in 
Figure 2a [ 182, 206 ]. 
To ensure that the designed actuator moves the finger along 
its natural axis and avoids straining the joints in unnatural 
directions, it is crucial to consider the specific muscle groups 
responsible for each joint’s flexion. As shown in Figure 2b , the 
flexor digitorum profundus muscle, starting at the tip joint (DIP) 
of the finger, pulls the entire finger into the palm, providing 
fine motor control. The flexor digitorum superficialis muscle, 
on the other hand, flexes the middle joint (PIP) and moves the 
middle portion of the finger. The primary muscle responsible for 
straightening (extension) the fingers is the extensor digitorum, 
located on the dorsal (upper) side of the hand. This muscle runs 
along the dorsal surface, enabling coordinated finger extension. 
Through the coordinated activation of these muscle groups, 
the fingers can perform various movements, including flexion, 
extension, and hyperextension, facilitating tasks such as writing, 
playing instruments, or performing delicate surgery. The little 
finger and thumb contain antagonistic muscle pairs similar to 
the flexor muscles. In the thumb, flexion, extension, abduction, 
adduction, and opposition movements play an essential role in 
enabling humans to grasp objects securely (Figure 2d ) [ 207 ]. 
Applying force beyond normal anatomical limits to a joint can 
lead to serious injury. The maximum flexion angles of the hand 
joints range from 70◦ to 95◦, PIP: ∼ 90◦, and DIP: ∼ 110◦. In the 
thumb, the abduction angle ranges from 40◦ to 60◦, and the MCP 
flexion angle ranges from 30◦ to 45◦ [ 208 ]. However, these data 
exhibit significant inter-individual variability [ 209 ]; even within 
individuals with the same neurological disease or spasticity, dis- 
tinct variations in range of motion have been observed [ 210, 211 ]. 
This biomechanical variability highlights the need for custom- 
designed soft actuators. The inability of standard robotic gloves or 
rigid mechanical systems to fully adapt to each individual’s hand 
structure increases the importance of adaptive and flexible fabric- 
based actuators. Accurate modeling of thumb-opposition motion 
is particularly crucial for the effective performance of wearable 
robotic gloves. 
3 Design and Material Considerations for 
Fabric-Based Exoskeleton Gloves 
3.1 Systematic Design Framework 
Fabric-based soft robotic gloves are complex, multidisciplinary 
systems that integrate materials science, textile engineering, 
sensing technology, actuation technology, control engineering, 
human factors, and healthcare. Studies generally address one 
or two of these disciplines at a time. However, the develop- 
ment of a successful and high-performance system requires that 
all components be systematically integrated within a unified 
design framework. This framework follows an iterative process 
organized as a stepwise chain, as illustrated in Figure 3 . 
The first stage involves identifying design goals and user needs. 
The target user profile, the motor activities to be supported, and 
the user environment are defined at this stage. This information 
ultimately determines important engineering parameters such 
as force, range of motion, ergonomic criteria, and duration of 
activity. This stage is extremely important, as it has a direct 
impact on subsequent decisions regarding the entire system 
architecture (including material types and various control algo- 
rithms). The second stage focuses on selecting the material 
structure and manufacturing methods for fabric-based actuators. 
In this stage, emphasis is placed on the textile structure that 
determines the actuator deformation and its mechanical response 
mode. The confluence of the material properties and the ability 
to utilize manufacturing techniques, which will be valuable 
for the actuator performance, longevity, energy efficiency, and 
mass production, directly impacts the mechanical and energy 
performance of the actuator, making it possible to define the 
limits for efficient operation of the system. The integration of

Stage six is when the first prototype is constructed, and laboratory

comfort of the user’s operating experience. This stage marks 
the first technical validation of the design, transitioning from 
theoretical development to practical demonstration. Stage seven 
consists of clinical testing and functional evaluations. In this 
phase, the intended use is directed toward the target user

the therapeutic effect it provides, effectiveness, ergonomics, and 
reliability. These validated data support the laboratory data under 
actual use conditions. Stage eight is design optimization. At this 
stage, aspects of the actuator structure, manufacturing method, 
and system layout, including the control components, may be 
adapted according to the intended use and informed by validated 
laboratory and clinical data. The focus here is on optimizing 
performance while ensuring that the system is prepared for long- 
term and repeated use under varying conditions. Ultimately, stage 
nine is productization and large-scale production. This validation 
phase is nonclinical and verifies that the design is validated for 
daily use conditions and that the execution scheme is compliant 
with regulatory standards, quality control requirements, and cost 
constraints. This phase demonstrates a reliable and sustainable 
system that has transitioned from the laboratory setting to 
real-world application. The systematic approach described here 
provides an overall framework for the effective, reliable, and 
user-driven integration of fabric-based actuators in wearable 
exoskeleton gloves, further establishing the purpose of each 
stage, how they fit together in an overall system, and the 
impact on design decisions. However, published fabric-based 
glove systems show that most implementations do not progress 
through all nine stages to reach full productization (Table 6 ). For 
example, braided actuators have demonstrated strong mechanical 
validation and laboratory optimization, yet have not advanced 
to clinical testing or large-scale deployment. Sewing-based and 
hybrid actuators that have reached pilot patient studies (Stage 
7), but long-term validation and large-scale application would be 
required for product-level maturity. Knitted actuators align well 
with scalable production concepts (Stage 9), but most current 
works remain concentrated in laboratory validation and system 
refinement stages. This uneven progression does not necessarily 
indicate technical limitations of specific actuator classes, but 
may instead reflect structural factors such as short-term funding 
cycles, research groups focusing on particular subsystems (e.g., 
materials, actuation, or control), and limited long-term interdis- 
ciplinary coordination required to bridge prototype development, 
clinical validation, and regulatory productization. 
3.2 Motion Design 
The human hand provides a number of degrees of freedom 
(DOF) and complex combinations of motions, both anatomically 
and biomechanically. However, transferring this potential to 
fabric-based soft exoskeleton gloves requires a careful motion 
design process where the materials and structural architectures 
are combined at the appropriate scale. In existing studies, the 
basic modes of motion include contraction [ 212 ], bending [ 213 ], 
twisting [ 213 ], extension [ 214 ], and expansion [ 215 ] (Figure 4a ) 
[ 46, 216 ]. In addition, actuator designs based on biomimetic 
approaches that produce hook-shaped [ 217 ], clover-shaped [ 218 ], 
snake-like [ 213 ], and blooming [ 219 ] movements have also been 
reported (Figure 4b ). Other reported examples include worm-like 
[ 220 ] and crawling [ 221 ] motions. The type of motion produced 
largely depends on the engineered anisotropy imparted to the 
actuator structure. Recently, programmable shape-morphing has 
been demonstrated by applying Turing-inspired surface pat- 
terns onto fabrics, enabling complex and controlled anisotropic 
deformations. For such programmable morphing, automated 
design methods that reduce reliance on manual expertise and 
trial-and-error approaches are essential [ 222 ]. Extension actu- 
ators generally possess a symmetric cross-sectional structure; 
expansion actuators create linear extension by inflating the 
flexible structure, while contracting actuators incorporate fiber- 
like elements that limit strain, allowing length reduction during 
inflation. Twisting motion is achieved through boundary con- 
ditions created by spirally wound fibers, while bending motion 
is achieved through asymmetric cross-sectional structures with 
varying stiffness zones [ 223 ]. Fabric selection is a decisive factor 
in both achieving the desired motion and expanding the range of 
achievable motions. Therefore, before proceeding to the design 
phase, the fabric is characterized through force–extension tests. 
Beyond these various motions, the actuator glove design aims to 
provide specific movements that support the user’s daily living 
activities and enhance the rehabilitation process. Anatomically, 
finger flexion (bending) and extension (opening) are fundamental 
to functional grasping, and the literature has predominantly 
focused on these movements. After neurological damage, open- 
ing the fingers becomes particularly difficult, and a pathological 
condition known as “clenched fist deformity”can develop [ 224 ]. 
Therefore, it is critical that the designed system supports both 
grasping and opening movements. Furthermore, thumb abduc- 
tion plays a decisive role in overall hand functionality [ 208 ], 
as the thumb works in coordination with the other fingers 
during grasping and manipulation, enabling fine motor skills. 
While traditional rigid exoskeleton systems support thumb move- 
ments to a limited extent, fabric-based actuators can enable 
these movements more naturally due to fiber orientation and 
programmable elasticity. Therefore, the fundamental movements 
that should be optimized in fabric-based soft gloves include 
finger flexion and extension, which are essential for grasping and 
releasing, and thumb abduction. In addition, the development of 
gloves capable of performing the full range of hand movements 
is considered necessary for these systems to become genuine 
assistive technologies for daily life and functional tasks.

4.1 Design and Fabrication Strategies 
Various fabric-based actuator designs have been developed based 
on the opportunities offered by different production techniques. 
Figure 5 illustrates the chronological evolution of these technolo- 
gies with respect to their production methods. Regarding braided 
actuators, Al-Fahaam et al. [ 225 ] introduced the asymmetric 
braiding actuator in 2018, followed by Takahashi et al. [ 226 ] in 
2019 with antagonistic McKibben actuators, and Koizumi et al. 
[ 227 ] in 2020 with a three-point bending McKibben muscle, as 
shown in Figure 5a . Using an alternative approach, sewing-based 
actuators rapidly expanded, with Nassour et al. [ 228 ] presenting 
the enfolded sewing actuator in 2019, Chen et al. [ 229 ] introducing 
a directional knit joint sewing actuator in 2020, and Correia et al. 
[ 230 ] developing a gathered sewing actuator in 2020 (Figure 5b ). 
Further advances include the elastic band sewing actuator by 
Suulker et al. [ 231 ] in 2022, segmented sewing actuators by Yılmaz 
et al. [ 232 ] in 2022, pleated sewing actuators by Maldonado 
et al. [ 233 ] in 2023, and, most recently, a knit anisotropic sewing 
actuator by Yılmaz et al. [ 213 ] in 2024. Welded and laminated 
actuators provided bladder-free designs, such as ultrasonically 
welded actuators by Yap et al. [ 234 ] in 2017, articulated lamination 
actuators by Connolly et al. [ 235 ] in 2019, welded honeycombed 
actuators by Lai et al. [ 236 ] in 2023, and a folded welding actuator 
by Dalaee et al. [ 237 ] in 2024, as shown in Figure 5c . Hybrid 
fabrication approaches integrated multiple methods, with Yap 
et al. [ 238 ] developing an accordion actuator in 2017, Nguyen 
et al. [ 239 ] introducing a fabric-reinforced actuator in 2019, 
Nuckols et al. [ 240 ] reporting a hybrid segmented actuator in 
2021, Feng et al. [ 241 ] demonstrating a high-force actuator in 
2021, and Li et al. [ 242 ] developing a sensing weaving actuator 
in 2024 (Figure 5d ). Knitted actuators have emerged as a scalable 
and programmable solution, beginning with a machine-knitted 
seamless actuator by Atalay et al. [ 243 ] in 2021, pressure and 
capacitive sensorized knitted actuators by Luo et al. [ 244 ] in 
2022, a caterpillar-inspired knitted actuator by Yang et al. [ 245 ] 
in 2023, shape memory alloy knitted actuators by Lee et al. [ 246, 
247 ] in 2023 and 2024. Furthermore, thermally driven resistive 
sensorized knitted actuators were introduced by Atalay et al. 
[ 217 ] and Ahmed et al. [ 248 ] in 2024 and 2025, respectively 
(Figure 5e ). 
Manufacturing processes directly determine design diversity 
and kinematic potential, thereby driving progress beyond per- 
formance optimization. Different production methods, such 
as braiding, sewing, lamination/welding, weaving, and knit- 
ting, have been able to reproduce similar primary modes 
of motion with different geometric configurations and mate- 
rial anisotropies. This emphasizes the relationship between 
motion, geometry, and the fabrication process, demonstrating 
that increased force or displacement is not necessarily the 
sole source of innovation. Recognizing this relationship helps 
avoid unnecessary duplication and enables research efforts to 
be directed toward areas with greater scientific and practical 
impact. Traditional textile manufacturing technologies, through 
the combination of textile mechanics and three-dimensional

4.1.1 Braided Actuators 
Standard braided actuators consist of a fiber mesh sheath 
wrapped around an inner bladder made of elastomeric materials 
(e.g., silicone or latex). When liquid or gas is injected into 
them, they undergo radial expansion and simultaneous axial 
contraction, similar to the contraction mechanism of biological 
muscles, driven by the Poisson effect [ 249, 250 ]. The braiding 
technique involves threads placed on spools mounted on specially 
designed machines and passing diagonally through each other 
(Figure 6a ). In this process, one set of thread carriers moves 
clockwise, while the other set moves counterclockwise. This 
reciprocal motion allows the threads to wrap around a mandrel, 
forming a braid. Different functional elements, such as sensors 
and conductive fibers, can be easily integrated into the structure 
[ 251, 252 ]. Because the threads are arranged helically within 
the structures, tensile force is distributed across all threads 
and their intersections when a load is applied. This reduces 
stress concentration on individual threads and increases the 
structure’s resistance to breakage and abrasion. These structures 
can easily bend, stretch, and move. Furthermore, it is possible to 
strengthen the mechanical properties of the material in certain 
directions or to add flexibility by altering the weave angle and yarn 
type [ 253 ]. 
These braided structures, originally used in pressure-resistant 
hoses, have been widely adopted in soft robotics, particu- 
larly in McKibben-type actuators inspired by biological muscle 
contraction [ 254–256 ]. Increasing the distance between yarn 
intersections makes the braid looser and more flexible, while 
decreasing this distance renders the structure tighter and more 
rigid. As the weave angle increases (the yarns are wound more 
perpendicularly to the longitudinal axis), the axial extension 
capacity of the actuator increases significantly, whereas its 
radial expansion capacity decreases. On the other hand, smaller 
angles (the yarns are wound more horizontally to the axis) 
increase the ability to expand in the circumferential direction. 
Furthermore, increasing the number of intersection lines in 
the braid enhances mechanical strength and structural rigidity, 
while braids with fewer intersection lines provide advantages 
in terms of flexibility and shape adaptability [ 257 ]. By carefully 
optimizing these parameters, the mechanical performance and 
motion characteristics of exoskeleton actuators can be designed 
and precisely tuned for specific application needs. The trend 
in the literature regarding exoskeleton gloves over the years 
has been toward the development of small-diameter braided 
actuators. 
Al-Fahaam et al. addressed the inherent linear contraction 
of conventional braided pneumatic actuators by introducing 
asymmetric reinforcement, stitching a high-strength (500 N) 
inextensible thread onto one side of the braided sheath to induce 
controlled bending (Figure 6b ). The resulting 4-DOF glove (120 g 
10–23 mm diameter, 16 cm length) generated ∼ 25 N per finger, 
demonstrating biomimetic flexion capability [ 225 ]. In contrast, 
Takahashi et al. developed a biomimetic soft exoskeleton inspired 
by the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus muscles, 
positioning thin braided actuators directly on the hand rather 
than on a conventional glove platform (Figure 6c ). Flexor–
extensor actuator pairs were integrated for each finger, enabling 
up to 20 DOFs and replicating the complex kinematics of the 
human hand. The miniaturized actuators ( ≈ 2.5 mm diameter, 
< 2 g; 2.5 × 27 mm optimized for finger joints) reduced bulk

4.1.2 Sewing-Based Fabric Actuators 
Sewing provides an accessible and scalable method of producing 
fabric-based actuators at low cost, making use of existing apparel 
manufacturing infrastructure and offering a simpler alternative 
to braiding and weaving techniques [ 204, 213, 222 ]. The litera- 
ture has shown that linear contraction, bending, or multiaxial 
deformations can be controlled by simply altering the stitch 
patterns, making the method highly flexible and customizable 
[ 213 ]. Furthermore, new approaches that simplify the process 
of achieving complex movements in bio-inspired robots hold 
promise for significantly improving production. Therefore, an 
innovative encoded sewing constraint (ESC) method has been 
developed for the production of soft textile robots [ 258 ]. This 
method enables the fabrication of three-dimensional textile shells 
with programmed strain constraints by encoding heterogeneous 
mechanical properties through stitch placement. Two stitch types 
(“0” and “1”) with distinct tensile behaviors are strategically 
combined to locally modulate stiffness and flexibility, allowing 
deformation patterns to be programmed via stitch density varia- 
tions. Warp-knitted fabrics incorporating these stitch codes form 
anisotropic stitched shells with integrated air pockets, enabling 
controlled spatial bending in programmable 3D soft textile robots. 
Stitching transforms flat textiles into inflatable 3D actuators 
without adhesive bonding. Although thermoplastic polyurethane 
(TPU) heat-sealed designs are suitable for prototyping, they 
face scalability and puncture limitations in mass production. 
Stitching enhances mechanical robustness and flexibility, but 
needle perforations may induce air leakage in pneumatic systems 
unless coated or laminated high-pressure fabrics are employed. 
Stitch pattern (e.g., zigzag, straight, overlock) directly gov- 
erns deformation behavior, fatigue life, and strain distribution. 
Moreover, integrating conductive or piezoresistive yarns enables 
stitches to serve dual structural and sensing functions [ 259–262 ].

The gathering technique allows for local mechanical program- 
ming in the fabric using only sewing thread manipulation. 
Tightening the sewing thread or creating gathers with elastic 
threads creates a wavy structure in specific areas of the fabric. This 
structure directly controls the actuator’s deformation behavior 
depending on the direction and density of the gathers. For 
example, concentrating the gathers in the warp direction limits 
bending movement during inflation; and looser gathers in the 
weft direction provide high elasticity and wide deformation 
ranges. Thus, different functional zones can be defined on a single 
fabric layer using the gathering technique. Indeed, in the textile- 
based soft robotic glove developed by Correia et al., the gathering 
method was applied at the finger joints instead of the pleated 
design used in previous versions (Figure 7c ) [ 230 ]. This allows 
for more localized flexion during actuator inflation and aligns 
with natural finger joint flexion. Suulker et al. developed a new 
manufacturing approach based on the integration of elastic bands 
to improve the performance of stitch-based pneumatic actuators 
(Figure 7d ). They demonstrated that knitted fabric actuators with 
integrated elastic bands can achieve a full 360◦ bending motion 
even at low pressures (30 kPa) and can reach a force of 22 N at 
100 kPa [ 231 ]. 
Yılmaz et al. developed a stitch-based segmentation strategy 
combining knitted and woven layers of differing widths to induce 
geometric asymmetry and exploit inherent fabric anisotropy for 
controlled bending (Figure 7e ) [ 232 ]. By modulating stitch-line 
configurations across six segment types (A, B, C, 3S, 5S, 7S), actu- 
ator motion was programmably tuned. The segmented designs 
achieved ≈ 200◦ bending while reducing radial expansion by up to 
50%, demonstrating improved directional control without added 
structural complexity [ 232 ]. 
The pleating technique gives the fabric a folded/pleated form. 
When the bladders are inflated, the pleated structure of the fabric 
opens, resulting in enhanced bending motion. This approach was 
applied to the ExHand exoskeleton developed by Maldonado- 
Mejía et al. (Figure 7f ). A rigid fabric and an elastic fabric, Lycra 
(Lycra-Nilon POWER ID-0019-056, Facol, Colombia), were used 
in the production of the actuators. In the study, pockets were 
created by placing pleated elastic fabric placed between two layers 
of rigid fabric, and thermoplastic elastomer (TPE) bladders were 
integrated into these pockets. Achieving bending, extension, or 
complex deformations in fabric-based actuators often requires 
the combined use of elastic and rigid fabric layers. The rigid 
fabric layer provides directional movement by limiting expansion 
in certain directions during inflation. However, this multilayer 
approach complicates the production process, reduces flexibility, 
and can also lead to seam separation problems at the seam 
lines [ 233 ]. 
Recent studies have replaced rigid constraint layers by program- 
ming mechanical anisotropy directly through stitch patterning 
on elastic fabrics, thereby eliminating seam-separation issues 
associated with multilayer assemblies. Yılmaz et al. demonstrated 
a single-layer, self-sensing pneumatic actuator fabricated from 1 
× 1 rib knit (65% Lycra/35% polyamide), where 301 strategically 
placed lockstitches encoded directional stiffness, as shown in 
Figure 7g . This stitch-programmed architecture achieved up to 
180◦ bending and a 3.8 N blocking force without rigid reinforce- 
ment [ 213 ]. Table 2 details the performance characteristics of 
sewing-based actuators. 
4.1.3 Welded and Laminated Fabric Actuators 
The fundamental design principle of actuators manufactured 
using welding and lamination techniques is to create sealed 
chambers directly between fabric layers, eliminating the need for 
internal bladders. Welding enables the molecular fusion of textile 
materials using heat, pressure, or ultrasonic energy, creating 
durable, integrated structures. In lamination, textile surfaces 
are coated with a thermoplastic film, thereby increasing the 
sealing properties and reliability of inflatable actuators. However, 
the thermoplastic coating that makes these textiles airtight also 
restricts their stretchability [ 221 ]. Maintaining inter-material 
bond strength is crucial for long-term use. While the initial 
adhesion strength achieved by welding or lamination is high, 
exposure of the actuator to continuous inflation and deflation 
cycles, mechanical stresses, bending, and environmental condi- 
tions (temperature changes, humidity, and chemical interactions) 
can lead to the weakening of the bond surface. This can result in 
problems such as delamination, microcracking, and a loss of air or 
liquid sealing over time. Therefore, to ensure long-term reliability, 
attention must be paid to maintaining fatigue resistance and 
environmental stability. 
In the scientific community, various groups have developed 
diverse design strategies. Among these, wavy and serpentine 
actuator geometries are increasingly adopted due to their high 
deformation capability at low pressures, making them a promi- 
nent trend in current design approaches. Yap et al. developed a 
glove that provides flexion-extension movements of the fingers by 
ultrasonically welding TPU-coated textiles (Figure 8a ). The flex- 
ible yet extension-resistant structure of the TPU-coated fabrics 
reduced elastic energy storage, enabling the efficient conversion 
of compressed air into mechanical motion. Spacer structures 
optimized airflow, while the addition of a strain-limiting layer 
provided deformation control, resulting in a compact architecture 
that does not require an internal bladder. The 99 g system 
achieved a bending radius of 0.069 ± 0.003 m at 30 kPa and 
generated 14.3 N peak force with 0.3 Nm torque at 70 kPa, 
while its open-palm architecture enhanced usability for motor- 
impaired users [ 234 ]. Connolly et al. developed articulated 
textile pneumatic actuators via lamination, using TPU coatings 
for airtightness and localized polyamide film reinforcements 
to control bending points (Figure 8b ). The segmented design

4.1.5 Knitted Fabric Actuators 
The traditional cut-and-sew method has been widely used in 
the production of textile-based actuators. However, this approach 
creates weak points in the seam areas, causing tears, especially 
in pneumatic systems exposed to high pressure. Furthermore, 
actuators produced with the cut-and-sew method exhibit low 
repeatability and scalability due to the manual labor required. 
To address this issue, Elmoughni et al. were the first to success- 
fully produce seamless pneumatic actuators using computerized 
machine knitting technology, as shown in Figure 10a . Using a V- 
bed knitting machine, tubular structures were quickly produced 
using front and rear needle beds. The combinations of Lycra, 
polyamide, and fusible yarns used in the actuators are critical 
to material engineering. Lycra, the preferred top layer, provides 
high flexibility and a wide deformation range; this property is 
essential for a pneumatic system to achieve the desired bending 
motions. On the other hand, the polyamide and fusible yarns 
used in the substrates contribute to structural stability and force 
transmission due to their lower stretch capacity. The actuators’ 
bending angle, force generation, and mechanical durability were 
tested at pressures up to 150 kPa. The knitted actuators were 
found to be capable of reaching a gripping force of 5 N and 
were more durable than traditional stitched actuators [ 243 ]. The 
availability of the yarns used on an industrial scale, combined 
with the digitalization of the production process, allows for the 
mass production of customizable products tailored to different 
applications and user requirements. In this respect, the study 
supports a sustainable production model in terms of both material 
efficiency and economics. Luo et al. achieved fully automatic 
and programmable production of pneumatic actuators with 
integrated sensors using digital machine knitting technology 
(Figure 10b ). The produced actuators were approximately 2 cm 
in diameter and 16 cm long. When activated at pressures up to 
800 mbar, they exhibited curvatures of up to 0.2 m− 1 and force 
generation of up to 9 N at the tip. The actuators’ durability was 
verified with 100 cycles of inflation/deflation tests conducted at 
700 mbar [ 244 ]. Yang et al.’s study utilized knitting technology 
to produce “caterpillar-like” pneumatic actuators (Figure 10c ). 
Boucle fancy yarns, which use polyurethane (PU) filament as the 
core and PET yarns as the knitted shell, provide the actuators with 
dual-stiffness. This feature provides a clear transition between 
low- and high-elastic regions, optimizing the actuator’s desired 
bending motion and force generation. Under a pressure of 50 kPa, 
the actuator was able to bend more than 216◦ and achieve a 
response speed as high as 1100◦/s [ 245 ]. 
Shape memory alloys have also been integrated into knitted 
textile actuators. The shape memory effect enables materials 
such as nickel–titanium (Nitinol) to recover their original shape 
upon thermal stimulation [ 265 ]. Nitinol offers advantages in 
applications requiring high force because it can support loads 
exceeding one hundred times its own weight. Therefore, it stands 
out as a suitable material when a fast response time is not 
critical. The force provided depends on the wire diameter and 
the number of wires used. However, SMA wires have limitations 
due to their sensitivity to humidity and their slow response 
speed [ 266 ]. When SMAs are knitted, lightweight and flexible 
actuators can be produced, and these actuators can exhibit both 
contraction and bending deformations [ 6 ]. For example, Lee 
et al. developed a glove integrating 14 knitted SMA modules, 
where polyester-wrapped SMA wires were incorporated into 
finger joints using plain and rib knit structures (Figure 10d ). The 
multilayer architecture consists of an inner insulating layer for 
thermal protection, elastic integration layers at the joints, wrist 
stabilization via neoprene support, and an outer porous layer 
for heat dissipation and comfort. Personalized glove geometries 
were generated through 3D hand scanning [ 246 ]. In subsequent

4.2 Sensor Integration Strategies 
Sensors can detect various mechanical deformations such as 
pressure [ 268 ], strain [ 269 ], bending [ 270 ], and torsion [ 271 ]. 
Textile-based sensors can be at the fiber [ 272 ] and yarn [ 273 ] level, 
and are also classified according to the production technique 
used as stitched [ 274–277 ], woven [ 278, 279 ], braided [ 280, 281 ], 
knit [ 282–284 ], nonwoven [ 285, 286 ] and composite-based sensors 
[ 287–289 ]. The level at which the sensor will be developed is deter- 
mined by the type of deformation to be detected (pressure, strain, 
etc.), the desired sensitivity, the application area, the mechanical 
durability/comfort balance and the production possibilities. In 
soft actuators, the relationship between input (voltage, current, 
pressure) and output (bending angle, force) is nonlinear. Further- 
more, these systems exhibit hysteresis and are highly sensitive 
to environmental conditions. Therefore, using only open-loop 
control (e.g., applying a specific voltage or pressure) can lead 
to incorrect positioning, excessive deformation, or insufficient 
force generation. Instantaneous bending angle, length change, 
and force can be directly monitored through strain, capacitive, or 
resistive sensors integrated onto the actuator surface. The feed- 
back from these sensors allows precise control of the actuator’s 
position, force, and speed, ensuring safe, stable, and user-specific 
performance [ 290, 291 ]. Building on this foundation, various 
strategies have been developed to integrate sensors into fabric- 
based exoskeleton actuators (Figure 11 ). In Zhou et al.’s study, 
sensors integrated into the glove were designed using the capac- 
itive principle to monitor both the actuator flexion and the grip 
force at the fingertips (Figure 11a ). Sensors placed on the dorsal 
side of the glove detect the extension that occurs during actuator 
flexion, while those placed on the fingertips measure the contact 
forces generated during grasping. The sensors were fabricated as 
a sandwich structure consisting of a dielectric elastomer layer 
sandwiched between two silver-plated textile electrode layers and 
then shaped using laser cutting. This multimodal sensing enables 
reliable detection of hand–object interaction and supports the 
event-triggered decision mechanism of the glove’s state machine- 
based controller. However, the authors reported baseline drift 
observed in textile-based capacitive sensors and developed a 
dynamic baseline approach to mitigate this issue [ 292 ]. Yilmaz 
et al. also utilized this capacitive sensor to monitor the movement 
of a segmented actuator, as shown in Figure 11b . In this study, 
the integrated capacitive strain sensors provided feedback to the 
closed-loop control system by monitoring the bending motion of 
the actuator; however, the need for separate calibration for each 
actuator demonstrated that sensitivity differences in textile-based 
sensors must be considered in terms of control stability [ 232 ]. In 
another study, Yilmaz et al. developed a strain sensor operating on 
a stitch-based resistive principle on a home sewing-embroidery 
machine and used it to monitor the movement of the actuator 
(Figure 11c ). In sensor fabrication, silver-coated nylon conductive 
yarns (Shieldex 235 f/36 dtex 2-ply HC + B) were embroidered 
on a 1 × 1 rib knit fabric using a zigzag stitch technique (304 
stitch geometry). This method yielded measurable changes in 
resistance due to changes in the electrical path length between 
the yarns during stretching. Electromechanical characterization 
studies revealed that the sensor can produce a stable signal 
with low hysteresis for 2000 cycles under 60% strain, can track 
resistance changes with bending motion with high accuracy (R2 
≈ 0.98), and has a gauge factor of 0.27. In addition, in pressure 
tests between 110 and 195 kPa, the sensor successfully monitored 
bending motions up to 180◦ without negatively affecting the 
actuator’s performance. The sensor output was used in ON–
OFF and PID-based closed-loop control. The observation of 
differences in the inflation and deflation phases, approximately 
1-s system delay, and nonlinear responses under cyclic loading 
necessitate phase-specific modeling in the control design [ 213 ]. 
In the study by Luo et al., two different sensors were integrated 
into the actuators: a resistance-based pressure sensor and a swept- 
frequency capacitive sensor (Figure 11d ). While these sensors 
could potentially support closed-loop control, they were not used 
for continuous feedback control but rather for pre-programmed 
stimulus–response actions. Both sensors are directly incorpo- 
rated into the knit structure and are produced using standard 
conductive yarns (80% polyester, 20% stainless steel). Thus, the 
sensors are obtained with the actuators in a single machine 
knitting process, eliminating the need for additional assembly 
[ 244 ]. In the thermally driven three-dimensional seamless textile 
actuators developed by Atalay et al. [ 217 ] and by Ahmed et al. 
[ 248 ], the resistive sensor function is directly integrated into 
the structure. For this purpose, a U-shaped resistive bending 
sensor was designed in the knit structure using highly conductive 
silver-coated polyamide yarn (Shieldex, 235 f, 36 dtex, 2-ply 
HC + B) (Figure 11e ). The resistive sensor was employed for 
system identification and dynamic characterization; the transfer 
function analysis demonstrated stable and controllable input–
output behavior with a delay of 0.34 s, indicating its suitability for

4.3.1 Pneumatic Actuation 
The basic components of pneumatic actuation in soft robotic 
systems typically consist of elastomeric inner tubes, a pump or 
compressor, a pressure regulator, pneumatic channels, valves, 
and pressure sensors. However, fabric-based exoskeleton gloves 
still rely on rigid and bulky compressors or pumps to supply 
compressed air to the actuators. A truly “soft robotics”approach 
requires not only the actuators themselves but also the energy 
conversion, fluid control, and system integration itself, based 
on soft, flexible, and compact structures. Although not yet used 
in fabric-based exoskeleton glove applications, Cacucciolo et al. 
developed a soft, quiet, flexible, and stretchable pump based 
on the electrohydrodynamic (EHD) principle [ 311 ]. Another 
study proposed an alternative solution for pneumatic systems by 
designing a soft origami pump [ 312 ]. This air is delivered to the 
actuators through thin, flexible tubing. The hoses that convey 
compressed air to the actuators are made of polymer materials 
that can withstand bending and stretching. Generally, pneumatic 
valves are used for movement control, such as servomotor- 
controlled 6 mm ball valves [ 228 ] or proportional regulator 
valves [ 229 ], two-position solenoid valves [ 233 ], and miniature 
solenoid valves [ 234 ]. Servomotor-controlled 6 mm ball valves 
are valves containing balls, operated with a 6 mm diameter 
connection, and precisely positioned by a servomotor for fluid 
control. Proportional regulator valves, unlike traditional on–off 
valves, can adjust output pressure or flow at a specific rate; that 
is, instead of simply being open or closed, they open or close 
steplessly (proportionally) according to a control signal. A two- 
position solenoid valve is a basic type of valve that controls 
pneumatic or hydraulic flow only in the open or closed position 
by opening and closing with an electrical signal. Miniature valves 
are small in size and offer portability. Soft valves, developed 
in recent years to address the limitations of rigid valves, offer 
an important alternative by eliminating the reliance of soft 
robots on rigid control elements [ 147, 313 ]. Pressure sensors 
measure the air pressure within the actuator and provide real- 
time feedback to the control system. Pressure sensors are used 
in applications requiring closed-loop control and precise motion. 
Simple, open-loop control may not require a pressure sensor. Air 
pressure regulators are used to match the high pressure from 
the compressor to the system’s requirements. If the air source 
provides a constant and safe pressure, a regulator may not be 
required. 
4.3.2 Thermally-Driven Actuation 
Traditional pneumatic systems are generally bulky, rigid, and 
noisy [ 314 ]. These characteristics can lead to an uncomfortable 
user experience, interference during movement, and low data 
accuracy. Thermally driven actuators, by contrast, provide wire- 
less independence because they eliminate the need for valves 
and piping systems. In this type of actuation, thermal energy 
causes mechanical deformation, enabling the actuator to perform 
flexion/extension movements. This actuation strategy reduces the 
overall weight of the system and results in a more compact and 
quieter structure. Due to these advantages, interest in thermally 
driven actuation methods has increased in recent years [ 71 ]. How- 
ever, its disadvantage is the slow actuation speed. Nevertheless, a 
recent study demonstrated dexterous motion in under 12 s with 
only 10.8 W of power consumption and a relatively low operating 
temperature of 48◦C [ 248 ]. 
Recent developments have led to the design of untethered, 
mobile, liquid-powered soft robots employing thermal actuation 
based on the evaporation of low-boiling-point engineering fluids 
using textile-integrated Joule heaters. Electrical energy is con- 
verted to heat by passing an electric current through a resistive 
conductive thread. 3 M Novec 7000 was used as the actuation 
fluid. Its low boiling point of approximately 34◦C makes it highly 
suitable for wearable exoskeleton systems that come into direct 
contact with the human body. Its ability to evaporate at lower 
temperatures provides advantages in terms of user comfort and 
safety, while also reducing energy consumption, resulting in 
a more efficient system. On the other hand, 3 M Novec 7100 
requires a higher temperature with a boiling point of 61◦C [ 217 ]. 
This poses a thermal safety risk, particularly in applications that 
come into contact with human skin. Additionally, the higher 
temperature requirement may result in higher system energy 
usage and put more strain on the textile-based heaters’ structural 
longevity. Therefore, Novec 7000 is a more sustainable and 
suitable choice in terms of both material integrity and user 
safety. Another critical component of thermal actuators is the 
pouch, a thin polymer-based film that is built into the thermal 
actuators to hold the evaporating liquid and build internal 
pressure. These films are mostly made of heat-resistant polymers 
with low permeability that retain liquid and have mechanical 
strength and sealing capability during deformation. Due to the 
direct exposure of the pouch material to varying temperatures

4.4 Control Strategies 
Currently, textile-based pneumatic actuators utilize microcon- 
trollers or embedded control cards. Microcontrollers receive 
real-time data from sensors and transmit commands to the pneu- 
matic valves, pumps, and actuators. Simple applications often 
use low-cost, readily available microcontrollers (e.g., Arduino, 
STM32), whereas complex systems requiring high-speed control 
can use computers or processors based on real-time operating 
systems (e.g. Digital Signal Processors (DSPs)). Specific choices 
are based on system specifications, the complexity of control 
algorithms, and application needs. Control electronics remain 
rigid and incompatible with textiles. Emerging textile-based logic 
controllers are being developed [ 315 ]. For example, fluid logic 
circuits may be directly embedded into textile layers to integrate 
on-board computation, memory, and decision-making. Thus, 
there are no more bulky circuit boards or externally connected 
systems; textiles are fully integrated as actuators while remaining 
soft, flexible, and low-profile. Although still in its infancy, this 
electronically free control architecture, powered by compressed 
air, is a part of making wearable robotic gloves in the future 
lighter, more comfortable, and more user-friendly. 
Textile robots have high degrees of freedom, high flexibility, 
and nonlinear material characteristics, so classical rigid robot 
control methods can be limited in their applications. Open-loop 
control systems are common in textile-based pneumatic actuators 
without sensors [ 238, 227 ]. In an open-loop control system, 
the pressure commands sent to the actuator were preset, with 
no information concerning the real-time status of the actuator 
position or motion occurring. Open-loop control inherently can- 
not precisely control actuator motion because it cannot directly 
measure or compensate for nonlinearities in system dynamics 
and material elasticity. Inaccuracy of control can be attributed to 
fluctuations in pressure levels, changes in environmental factors, 
and internal deformations of the actuator. For sensorless open- 
loop control in applications that support a wide variety of human 
hand movements, like exoskeleton gloves, this level of precision 
and adaptability is simply not sufficient. Achieving adequate 
levels of control using an open-loop approach may not be possible 
without real-time feedback, allowing the actuator to be honed to 
the natural movements of the user, and maintain safety. Without 
real-time feedback, effective control may not be possible, and 
this is crucial to recognize, as controlling an actuator requires 
the ability to adapt to the user’s motor-mechanical movements 
while remaining safe at all times. The integration of sensors 
into textile-based pneumatic actuators represents an example 
of closed-loop control systems. In closed-loop systems, state 
variables such as pressure, strain, deformation, and position are 
monitored in real time, either through integrated sensors or via 
environmental sensing methods such as image processing. The 
controller receives all this information and calculates the differ- 
ence between the current state of the actuator and the desired 
target value and transmits commands to actuator units (e.g., 
pneumatic valves and pumps) to minimize this difference. Doing 
this increases the accuracy of motion produced by the actuator 
and allows the actuator to quickly compensate for unforeseen 
deformations and external load effects. Closed-loop control has 
been successfully demonstrated by integrating capacitive textile- 
based sensors directly into the actuator [ 232 ] and resistive textile 
sensors sewn with conductive threads [ 213 ]. Embedding sensors 
within the actuator body has emerged as a primary strategy to 
ensure rapid and accurate state monitoring. 
For multi-degree-of-freedom and complex motion tasks, such 
as those in exoskeleton gloves, hybrid cascade position–force 
closed-loop control strategies are often implemented [ 225 ]. In 
this strategy, position control is provided by the neural network- 
based model identifier, while fuzzy logic-based force control is 
used to more accurately control the force of the actuator. This 
method utilizes real-time bending and pressure data from the 
sensors and passes this feedback to the controller. The controller 
generates commands for the pneumatic valves and pumps to 
optimize both motion and force, creating a high-performing, safe 
control system. This method of motion and force control is robust 
to the nonlinear dynamics of textile-based pneumatic actuators, 
as well as the non-controllable environment. 
The exoskeleton glove offers a variety of control modes to allow 
for user interaction and movement initiation. The modes can be 
seen as inputs that trigger the operation of the system. For exam- 
ple, an actuator control could be activated by a button press;[ 230 ] 
muscle activity could be detected via surface electromyography 
(sEMG) signals [ 229 ]; movement information could be obtained 
from a data glove worn on the healthy hand; or it could be 
pre-programmed [ 234 ] to operate automatically in cycles. The 
control inputs, in essence, act as an input from the user to the 
system whose primary purpose is to act upon the user’s movement 
intention. 
With the advancement of new technologies, which are still not 
integrated in exoskeleton systems, Yang et al. have produced a 
novel class of acting with a control function called bistable fabric 
mechanisms (BFMs) that jointly integrate actuation and control 
inside a single structure [ 316 ]. BFMs provide rapid actuation 
(without the need for electronic valves), pressure regulation, 
and automated autonomous control using pneumatic logic gates 
without electronics. The basic logic behind BFMs is the hysteresis 
produced by the fabric chambers (one flattening and the other 
bending during inflation). In this bistable configuration, the 
embedded tubes could open and close, enabling it to function 
simultaneously as an actuator and valve at the same instant. 
While this concept has not yet been applied to wearable robots, 
such electronics-free control strategies could be directly adapted 
to fabric-based exoskeleton gloves, paving the way for lighter, 
more flexible, and scalable systems.

activated textile actuators. Atalay et al. reported an actuator that 
bends through the process of vaporizing a low-boiling liquid via 
textile-based heaters. The system is based on an on–off control

4.5 Power Supply and Energy Requirements 
The energy needs and supplying methodologies vary by actuation 
type. Pneumatics require millisecond actuation times, as well as 
specific pressures and flow rates necessary to actuate subsequent 
finger movements. Compressed air tanks can release the air at a 
high flow rate nearly instantaneously with the installation of a 
valve. The efficiency of valve–pump systems determines overall 
air consumption; if the valve is not switched efficiently, pressure 
losses during transit increase both air usage and energy demand, 
compromising portability. To address this, pulse-width modula- 
tion (PWM) voltage conversion modules, commonly employed 
in pneumatic actuation systems, are particularly important. 
These modules enable moderate energy management by rapidly 
switching the circuit on and off, rather than fully cutting the 
power supply. Additionally, this technology not only contributes 
to energy savings but also extends the operational lifetime of 
pneumatic and soft robotic components through less thermal loss 
and friction wear. From a power supply standpoint, either electric 
compressors or batteries are typically used for exoskeleton gloves. 
The compressors generate pressure in the glove, and the batteries 
provide the energy to the compressor unit as well as to the valve 
and control circuits. The considerable bulk and weight of both 
the compressor and batteries can restrict usability and comfort 
for users. Therefore, research also indicates a need to utilize 
lightweight and compact energy sources. To address this, Shveda 
et al. developed a textiles-based pneumatic harvesting system that 
could sustainably harness the energy derived from the user’s foot 
impact with the ground during walking. The energy is harvested 
through a textile pump installed in the shoe sole and is stored in 
a wearable textile reservoir, where the energy is used to power 
pneumatic actuators on-demand. The system was optimized for 
performance based on a mechanofluidic model, achieving an 
average power of approximately 3 W with a conversion efficiency 
over 20% [ 317 ]. This value is greater than alternatives using elec- 
tromagnetic, piezoelectric, and triboelectric methods, confirming 
that the system works. Although this approach has not yet been 
validated in exoskeleton gloves, it has shown potential to assist 
adults with upper body functional limitations with activities of 
daily living by using a wearable arm lift device as a shoulder 
movement assistive device, and activate the actuation of a robotic 
arm. Thermal actuation systems only require an electrical source 
(power supply or portable battery). This facilitates wearability of 
these devices, as it does not require any additional components 
such as a compressor or air tank. The energy requirement is 
fulfilled through Joule heating from the current passing through 
the heating filaments. Therefore, the movement of the actuator 
operates by converting electrical energy into heat, and then 
converting the heat into a volumetric change and expansion. 
5 Performance Testing and Validation of 
Fabric-Based Robotic Gloves 
Manufacturing processes and design innovations for textile-based 
robotic gloves alone are not sufficient; it is necessary to demon- 
strate whether the developed prototypes are truly functional, 
reliable, and clinically usable to ensure their translation into real- 
world rehabilitation applications. This section provides a detailed 
explanation of the biomechanical, strength, functional, user 
experience, and durability tests used to evaluate the performance 
of textile-based robotic gloves. 
5.1 Biomechanical and Kinematic Evaluations 
Range of Motion (ROM) of Joints : ROM of actuators is assessed 
by determining their bending or displacement capacity. For 
this purpose, angular changes occurring at the actuator’s end 
points can be recorded and calculated using high-resolution 
camera systems or sensors [ 318, 319 ]. Alternatively, the end point 
displacement is analyzed using geometric methods to obtain 
the bending angle and bending radius [ 320 ]. Measurements 
made at different pressure or tension levels reveal the actuator’s 
ROM-excitation relationship, allowing its performance to be 
quantitatively evaluated. 
Kinematic Analysis : It is a critical evaluation approach to deter- 
mine the effects of movement through the exoskeleton on the 
user, and to determine the differences in natural movements 
performed. The kinematic analysis is based on comparing limb 
movements prior to and after utilizing the exoskeleton. More 
specifically, the kinematic analysis focuses on the change in 
joint ROM to observe the differences in movements from the 
exoskeleton. As a measurement example, the ROM of the knee 
joint, elbow joint, or shoulder joint is made before and after 
donning the exoskeleton. This provides useful data to understand 
the ROM and movements. There are generally two methods for 
the kinematic analysis. The first method involves using inertial 
measurement unit (IMU) sensors, which are employed to gather 
data on speed and acceleration and apply them to clothing so 
that the movements of the user can be recorded. The second 
method relates to 3D motion capture systems being employed 
to measure joint movements in three-dimensional space using 
markers placed on the user and tracked with cameras/laser 
systems. The measurements are evaluated by analyzing the 
movements performed before and after exoskeleton use through 
three-dimensional motion analysis. This comparison shows the 
device’s effect on natural motions. In most of the cases, with

of relevant muscle groups, and the bioelectric signals generated 
during this process can be recorded with sEMG sensors. After 
filtering the raw data, feature values such as root mean square

output), the relevant muscle groups supporting this movement 
are activated, and electrical impulses are generated in the muscle 
fibers. These impulses are called bioelectric signals and represent 
the functioning of the muscles [ 321 ]. 
5.2 Force and Torque Assessments 
One of the key issues with assistive and rehabilitation devices is 
providing enough force to successfully assist in limb movement. 
Typically the applied force must be greater than the user’s muscle 
strength; however, it is also important to ensure that the force is 
not excessive or harmful to the patient [ 172 ]. Therefore, force and 
torque measurements are cornerstone indicators for assessing 
soft glove performance. Grip strength assessments are performed 
using a manual hand dynamometer. The user wears the glove, 
grasps the dynamometer firmly, and the dynamometer records 
the force applied by the user at maximum grip in either Newtons 
or kilograms. This process intends to measure if the glove can pro- 
duce the minimum force needed for daily activities. The fingertip 
force capability is how much squeeze pressure the glove can gen- 
erate at the fingertips using a tip force test. In this test, the peak 
force exerted by the inflated actuators on an object is measured 
using load cells or force sensors located at the fingertips. Mechan- 
ical efficiency, defined as the relationship between actuator input 
pressure and fingertip output force, is further assessed through 
joint-level performance. At this stage, torque limits are quantified 
using torque sensors or rotating dynamometers to record the 
rotational force applied by the actuator to the joint. The torque is 
calculated by multiplying the applied force at the fingertip by the 
moment arm length. These measurements have real utility as they 
indicate the functional adequacy of the glove by demonstrating 
a relative degree of rotational support provided to the finger 
joints. 
Thumb-abduction Performance Assessment : The study by Ge 
et al. investigated the abduction force and thumb abduction 
range of motion produced by a thumb-abduction actuator. 
In this context, abduction force is defined as the force that 
allows the thumb to move laterally from the palm. For the 
abduction testing, the actuator was pressurized against the 
fixed force sensor. An acrylic plate fixed at the top of the 
actuator restricted linear motion, allowing radial force direc- 
tion toward the fixed sensor surface. The pressure applied 
to the actuator was increased between 0 and 160 kPa, and 
measurements were repeatedly recorded. To assess the range 
of motion, a soft glove embedded with the actuator was uti- 
lized, and markers were placed at three anatomical points on 
the thumb. The thumb abduction angle was determined by 
the change in angle between these three anatomical points, 
and the analytical data were generated by image processing 
software [ 208 ]. 
5.3 Functional and Clinical Tests 
Grasping Performance Test : Before devices are tested on para- 
lyzed patients, they are validated in experimental studies with 
healthy participants. Healthy participants must have normal 
hand motor function and be able to demonstrate gross and 
fine grasping movements without any pain or discomfort. The 
anthropomorphic hand assessment protocol (AHAP) is often 
used as a measure of the possible applications of a soft hand 
exoskeleton, relative to activities of daily living. The AHAP was 
developed by Llop-Harillo et al. and is designed to assess dexterity 
in anthropomorphic robotic and prosthetic devices intended for 
grasping [ 322 ]. The AHAP consists of 25 objects taken from the 
Yale-CMU-Berkeley (YCB) object set, created by Calli et al. [ 323 ], 
and performs objective assessments of the devices’ performance 
using components of various sizes, shapes, textures, hardness lev- 
els, and weights. The devices are assessed on eight types of grasp: 
hook grip, spherical grip, tripod pinch, extension grip, cylindrical 
grip, diagonal volar grip, lateral pinch, and pulp pinch. Each grasp 
has a common meaning: past volar grip, lateral compression, and 
pulp compression. In the experiment, the participant learns the 
appropriate grasp posture for each object. The participant holds 
the object for 3 s, rotates the hand naturally through 180◦, and 
holds that position for an additional 3 s. The system is then 
depressurized, and the participant releases the object. The test is 
repeated three times. The scoring system is based on two criteria: 
the ability to grasp and the ability to maintain the object. For 
the ability to grasp, 1 point is awarded for holding the object 
using the appropriate grip type, 0.5 points for grasping in an 
alternate posture, and 0 points for failure to grasp. The score for 
maintaining the object is based on whether it was held stationary 
(1 point), in its native position (0.5 points), or dropped (0 points). 
The scores for the two criteria compose the overall performance 
indicator known as the grasping ability score (GAS), which is 
expressed as a percentage. A GAS of 100% indicates that the 
device performance approximately matches that of a healthy 
hand [ 233 ]. 
Box and Blocks Test (BBT) : One method used to evaluate the 
effectiveness of wearable assistive devices is the BBT. This test 
is widely used to measure the functional benefit of the devices, 
particularly in individuals with limited manual dexterity [ 324 ]. 
The test uses a wooden box (53.7 × 25.4 × 8.5 cm) containing 
150 wooden cubes (2.5 cm side length) and a 15.2 cm-high 
compartment in the center. The procedure is as follows: The 
participant is seated at a table, and the box is placed at the 
participant’s midline. The cubes are positioned so that the side 
containing the cubes is oriented toward the hand being tested. 
The participant is asked to move as many blocks as possible, 
one by one, from one compartment to the next within 60 s. The 
final score is determined by the number of blocks moved. This 
test serves as a complementary assessment method to the AHAP 
protocol, providing additional information on fine motor skills 
and grip performance used in activities of daily living. Thus, the

The Jebsen–Taylor Hand Function Test (JTHFT) : It is a stan-

evaluation of the progress in the rehabilitation process in clinical 
studies and the determination of the effectiveness of soft robotic 
rehabilitation devices [ 233 ]. 
5.4 User Experience and Comfort Evaluations 
Subjective Evaluation : This method aims to analyze users’ opin- 
ions following their experience. For example, issues such as ease 
of donning and doffing and comfort of use can be evaluated. 
Relevant data can be collected through a combination of tests, 
questionnaires, and surveys. The final determination of the 
exoskeleton’s particular application performance is made by 
computing the scores for each subjective indicator [ 321 ]. 
Usability Assessment : To assess user satisfaction, the Quebec 
User Satisfaction Evaluation Survey (QUEST) 2.0, widely used 
in assistive technologies, was utilized. The QUEST 2.0 is a 
standardized 12-item scale, with each item scored on a 5-point 
Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied). The scale 
is divided into three categories: device, services, and overall 
satisfaction. The ExHand Exoskeleton study considered only the 
“Device” category. Six evaluation items were examined within 
this scope: size, weight, adjustability/fit, safety, comfort, and 
effectiveness. After completing the functional tests, participants 
filled out a survey, and the results were examined to determine 
the system’s user experience advantages and disadvantages. This 
assessment provides insightful information on clinical applica- 
bility and user acceptability in addition to the device’s technical 
performance [ 233 ]. 
User Testing : JTHFT and BBT are among the most commonly used 
tests to evaluate the effectiveness of wearable assistive devices. 
These protocols are widely used to measure the contribution of 
devices to activities of daily living, particularly in individuals 
with limited manual dexterity. However, working with such user 
groups presents several critical challenges. First, it is necessary 
to ensure the safety of robotic prototypes for frail or vulnera- 
ble individuals. Furthermore, ethical considerations regarding 
device use should be considered, and potential risks should 
be carefully evaluated. For these reasons, many researchers 
prefer to conduct studies on healthy individuals during early- 
stage testing. However, this approach also has some limitations: 
The natural dexterity of healthy individuals can influence test 
results, distorting the device’s true contribution. The high flexi- 
bility and relatively low actuation forces of soft robotic devices 
may mask device performance or reduce reliability in healthy 
individuals. Instead of depending only on functional testing, 
it is recommended to incorporate EMG sensors into testing 
procedures. EMG-based measurements allow for the quantitative 
determination of the support provided by the device and provide 
more clinically reliable results. 
Subjective Comfort Measurement : The success of wearable assis- 
tive devices is directly related not only to functional perfor- 
mance but also to user comfort. The Comfort Affective Labeled 
Magnitude (CALM) scale is frequently used to measure user 
experiences. This scale, developed by the US Army Natick 
Military Research, Development, and Engineering Center, is a 
standardized method for evaluating the ergonomic suitability and 
user feel of wearable systems (Cardello et al., 2003). The CALM 
scale consists of 11 items and is evaluated on a scale ranging from 
− 100 (uncomfortable) to + 100 (high comfort). A value of 0 is 
considered neutral, indicating neither an uncomfortable nor a 
comfortable experience. This technique enables users to quantita- 
tively rate their comfort while using the device, providing useful 
input for both design development procedures and user-centered 
enhancements [ 211 ]. 
Objective Comfort and Ergonomic Metrics : Comfort and 
ergonomics are evaluated through quantitative performance 
indicators. To reduce the risk of impaired microcirculation and 
tissue damage, continuous interface pressures are 32 mmHg 
clinical threshold [ 326 ]. IEC 60601-1 standard identifies 41◦C as 
a safety reference in contact with the skin, which is particularly 
critical in thermally driven actuators [ 217 ]. According to the ISO 
9237, the air permeability > 500 mm/s facilitates moisture transfer 
and reduces the risk of skin maceration [ 327 ]. Additionally, EMG 
measurements provide ergonomic suitability by assessing muscle 
fatigue. 
5.5 Durability and Reliability 
Long-term durability and reliability are fundamental design 
requirements for sensorized fabric exoskeleton gloves intended 
for daily assistance and home-based rehabilitation. To ensure 
these attributes, some tests are required. Durability tests assess 
fatigue, deformation, and material wear in actuators by carrying 
out repeated inflation–deflation cycles. Reliability tests evaluate 
repeatability of force, torque, and ROM under a variety of usage 
conditions, while also considering the signal stability of the inte- 
grated sensors. Environmental resistance, long-term performance 
stability, and possible failure modes are also assessed as part of the 
reliability proof to validate the reliability of the glove in practical 
situations [ 328 ]. 
Textile standards provide measurable benchmarks for durabil- 
ity and reliability. For example, ISO 13934-2 specifies that the 
minimum tensile strength for work-related apparel is 450 N 
with 12.5%–55% elongation. Applying this standard to actua- 
tor fabric helps to protect its shape during repeated actua-

5.6 Standardized Evaluation Protocol 
To bridge the gap between laboratory and clinical application, 
we propose a three-tiered evaluation protocol that systematically 
validates the glove’s performance. 
In the first tier (Benchtop Validation), the focus is on mechan- 
ical safety, structural integrity, and control performance prior 
to human testing. Essential metrics include actuator range of 
motion (ROM), torque and force output, pressure–force effi- 
ciency, interaction stiffness, actuation latency, and repeatability. 
Material durability and structural reliability should be validated 
according to standardized textile testing protocols, including ISO 
13934-2 (tensile strength) and ISO 13938-1 (bursting strength), to 
ensure stability under repeated pressurization. Fatigue resistance 
and sensor signal stability should also be quantified to establish 
engineering readiness. 
The second tier (Healthy User Feasibility) evaluates biome- 
chanical compatibility, human–robot interaction, and ergonomic 
performance in unimpaired individuals. At this stage, kine- 
matic ROM comparisons between assisted and unassisted 
movements are conducted to verify that the device does not 
restrict natural motion. sEMG-based quantification of muscle 
activation reduction is used to objectively determine assis- 
tive contribution. Standardized functional assessments such 
as the Anthropomorphic Hand Assessment Protocol (AHAP), 
Box and Blocks Test (BBT), and Jebsen–Taylor Hand Func- 
tion Test (JTHFT) are employed to evaluate baseline device- 
assisted dexterity and grip performance. In addition, validated 
usability and comfort scales, including QUEST 2.0 and CALM, 
are administered to assess user perception and ergonomic 
suitability. 
In the third tier (Clinical Functional Validation), efficacy is 
assessed within the target patient population to determine 
rehabilitation relevance. Here, standardized functional tools 
such as BBT and JTHFT are utilized as clinical outcome 
measures rather than feasibility tests, enabling comparison 
with established rehabilitation benchmarks. Condition-specific 
motor recovery scales (e.g., Fugl-Meyer Assessment or ARAT) 
should be incorporated to quantify therapeutic impact. Addi- 
tional metrics include adherence rate, safety monitoring, and 
long-term functional retention to establish real-world clinical 
viability. 
6 Human-Centered Design 
The main principle of human-centered design is to create a 
glove that facilitates movements that are basically natural to the 
user. Thus, it is necessary to develop designs based on actual 
kinematic finger movement data. Many significant advancements 
have occurred in the world of soft exoskeleton gloves in the 
last decade, but strong clinical evidence of the benefits of 
these technologies over traditional rehabilitation treatments is 
limited [ 194 ]. Laboratory studies allow researchers to assess the 
functionality of a device and measure usability, but the true 
nature of these devices may be revealed by the complexities of 
use in real-world conditions. For these reasons, evaluations of 
devices that only consider controlled studies in a laboratory are 
not going to give the complete story; laboratory studies need to be 
complemented with longer-term, ecologically valid field studies 
to inform how technologies can best be used and to improve user 
experience [ 329 ]. 
The process of adopting assistive robotic technologies cannot 
be analyzed solely based on individual factors, like ergonomics, 
quality of the device, or ease of use. This process is also 
shaped by wider social contexts, such as support and encour- 
agement from family, therapists, or caregivers, technical support 
and training, and awareness. When users are included in 
the development process, the design of the assistive robotic 
technology is not always changed in a visible manner, and 
there are often few or no insights into how user feedback is 
translated into engineering decisions. In terms of user feed- 
back, additional transparency into how user feedback is inte- 
grated into design is therefore an important aspect to promote 
increased acceptance and consistent use of the devices. While 
the customization and personalization notion is identified as 
an important pillar of user acceptance, they have very limited 
applicability [ 329 ]. 
A complete human-centered design approach is dependent on 
using an iterative process based on multiple feedback loops and 
engaging users at all points in the process. These steps include 
learning about users and their larger context, defining the prob- 
lems to be addressed, collaborating across disciplines to generate 
ideas, building low-level prototypes, and, importantly, testing 
them with users so designers, engineers, and practitioners can 
evolve them into high-level solutions. The ability for developers to 
suspend their judgment, evaluate their assumptions, and reframe 
those assumptions is an important consideration for success 
[ 330 ]. 
Previous studies have identified user needs through both qualita- 
tive and quantitative methods. Qualitative approaches typically 
include focus groups, workshops, and individual interviews, 
whereas quantitative methods have employed measures such as 
the Barthel Index, the System Usability Scale, and subjective well- 
being scales. Technology acceptance models (e.g., the Technology 
Acceptance Model; the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of 
Technology) have also been applied, reflecting the unique context

Human-centered design can be achieved through material selec-

comfort, safety, and long-term use. At the skin–textile inter- 
face, capillary-driven microfilament polyamide or multi-channel 
polyester yarns (Coolmax) with high air permeability ( > 800–
1000 mm s− 1 , ISO 9237), manufactured via seamless knitting, 
enhance moisture vapor transport and reduce humidity accumu- 
lation within the microclimate [ 331 ]. Segmented and anisotropic 
actuator architectures can be realized through knitting tech- 
niques by locally varying stitch density and incorporating inlaid 
high-modulus yarns, generating direction-dependent stiffness 
profiles with longitudinal-to-transverse modulus ratios. This 
controlled anisotropy guides force transmission along natural 
finger kinematics and, when combined with low-pressure (30–
70 kPa) pneumatic actuation, reduces peak interaction forces 
and hyperextension risk while improving motion predictability 
[ 215, 217, 232, 243, 248 ]. From a control perspective, multi- 
modal sEMG-based closed-loop intention detection with laten- 
cies below ∼ 100 ms ensures the glove feels like a functional 
extension of the body rather than an external burden, thereby 
supporting perceived agency, user trust, and sustained adher- 
ence [ 332 ]. Additionally, user discomfort and social stigma can 
be reduced moving toward silent, electronics-free pneumatic 
logic systems [ 316 ] or keeping operating noise levels below 
∼ 30–40 dB (ISO 1996-1:2016), which is equivalent to typical 
quiet indoor ambient sound levels. Although thermally driven 
actuators provide inherently silent and electronics-minimized 
operation, pneumatic systems offer fast response and high 
force. 
7 User Safety and Ethical Considerations 
Fabric-based soft robotic glove technologies provide compliant 
and lightweight assistance for hand rehabilitation and daily 
support; however, their safe and ethical deployment requires a 
structured risk-based approach. The main mechanical concern 
is preventing excessive joint excursion. Mechanical end-stops, 
calibrated torque and force thresholds, and motion-limiting 
structural designs are necessary to ensure that finger and wrist 
articulations remain within physiological limits [ 185 ]. In addi- 
tion, feedback-based emergency shutdown systems capable of 
interrupting actuation during abnormal operation enhance user 
protection [ 333 ]. 
Fabric-based exoskeletons have potential textile-specific safety 
risks, in addition to conventional mechanical hazards. Seam 
fatigue, stitch leakage, and the separation of laminated layers 
of the actuator can cause unexpected force transmission. If the 
inherent elasticity of textile materials, suitable structural design, 
and balanced load distribution are not guaranteed, regional 
pressure concentrations could happen. Long-term cyclic loading, 
humidity exposure, and repetitive donning/doffing may induce 
material creep, conductivity drift, and mechanical degrada- 
tion. These failure modes distinguish textile exoskeletons from 
rigid robotic systems and necessitate durability-informed safety 
strategies. 
Although specific standards currently do not exist for fabric- 
based exoskeleton gloves, existing regulatory frameworks can be 
applied depending on the application, including clinical rehabili- 
tation or personal care robotics standards. Clinical rehabilitation 
application of it may fall under medical device frameworks 
such as ISO 14971 (risk management for medical devices) 
and the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR 2017/745). An 
assistive personal care application may align with ISO 13482 (per- 
sonal care robots). Electrical safety considerations are addressed 
under IEC 60601-1, and manufacturing quality systems under 
ISO 13485 [ 334 ]. 
Ethical considerations extend beyond physical safety. While these 
systems enhance functional independence, mechanical malfunc- 
tion, algorithmic instability, or software errors may compromise 
user autonomy. The principle of non-maleficence requires that 
the assistive benefit clearly outweigh technological risk. In 
rehabilitation, excessive reliance on assistive actuation may also 
influence motor learning adaptation. This situation carries the 
risk of developing device dependency in the long term and a 
decrease in independent movement capacity. 
Privacy and data governance represent additional ethical chal- 
lenges. Fabric-based exoskeleton gloves typically collect motion 
and biometric data, introducing risks of unauthorized access, 
misuse by third parties, or cyber vulnerability. Compliance with 
data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR), implementation of 
encrypted communication protocols, and transparent data poli- 
cies are therefore essential for responsible societal integration. 
8 Challenges, Limitations, and Future 
Directions 
Fabric-based exoskeleton gloves must transition from lab- 
oratory proof-of-concept prototypes toward clinically robust 
and scalable systems. They will evolve beyond simple actua- 
tion; they will require integrated multimodal sensing systems. 
To achieve this, systems must synergistically integrate pre- 
movement intention detection, kinematic tracking, and tactile 
force estimation. However, the seamless integration of this 
heterogeneous sensor network into compliant textile architec- 
tures remains a critical bottleneck across advanced material 
stability, scalable hybrid manufacturing, and edge-computing 
control paradigms. 
While various soft hand exoskeleton designs have been proposed 
in the literature, the majority of these devices have not yet 
been extensively tested in clinical patient populations and have 
not been implemented beyond laboratory settings. Therefore, 
uncertainty remains regarding the patient groups for which 
they are best suited. Establishing rigorous safety protocols and 
developing dedicated evaluation and manufacturing standards 
for textile-based soft robotics is critical to ensure methodological 
consistency, enable objective comparison across studies, enhance 
product reliability, and accelerate clinical translation.

9 Conclusion 
This review examines the current technological status of fabric- 
based exoskeleton gloves in all its components, systematically 
analyzing design approaches, fabrication methods used for actu- 
ators and sensors, actuation strategies, control strategies, and 
power requirements. The advantages and limitations of each 
approach are highlighted. The study highlights advances in the 
existing literature and also points to areas for development. 
These assistive robotic systems have achieved significant success 
thanks to advances in materials science, sensor technologies, 
actuation technologies, and control algorithms. The fabric types 
used are susceptible to damage from sharp objects. Furthermore, 
the conductive threads used for sensor integration are prone to 
oxidation. 
The vast majority of the devices reviewed are either in the devel- 
opment phase, have only been tested in laboratory conditions, 
or are intended for closed clinical environments. However, long- 
term use and user safety remain among the issues that require 
detailed investigation. Furthermore, common test protocols and 
international standards that would allow comparison of different 
systems have not been established. The maximum forces and 
ROM that devices can produce vary considerably between studies. 
This study provides a comprehensive reference for future 
researchers and engineers, highlighting both current limitations 
and potential areas for improvement. To ensure that users feel 
like natural extensions of their bodies, lightweight and flexible 
designs, low-cost manufacturing methods, and modular systems 
with a high power-to-weight ratio should be the focus. The 
ultimate goal is to develop scalable and integrated textile-based 
robotic systems where actuators, sensors, control units, and 
energy components are all integrated directly into the fabric 
structure. Furthermore, establishing a stronger communication 
network among therapists, physiologists, researchers, and man- 
ufacturers will make rehabilitation devices more functional and 
accessible for both clinical and home use.
