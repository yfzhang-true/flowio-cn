ABSTRACT 
Biomimicry, a methodology adapted from nature, always inspires 
optimum solutions and innovative technologies in human history. 
To get children interested in, excited about, and inspired by biomimicry, 
we introduce KiPneu, a robotic platform that facilitates biomimicry 
education through hands-on, solution-oriented learning and a dig-
ital learning environment. KiPneu allows children to mimic fex-
ible animal locomotion, like fsh swimming or worm squirming, 
using low-cost building blocks and non-electrical pneumatic ac-
tuators. We provide fve types of non-electrical tangible valves to 
adjust robot motion characteristics, such as direction and speed, 
through engaging tangible programming. Additionally, to facili-
tate the whole learning process, KiPneu comes with interactive 
instructional interface that visualize and simulate the pneumatic 
system. To validate KiPneu’s educational efcacy, we conducted 
a three-day workshop with 21 children aged 5-12. Pre-and-post

1 INTRODUCTION 
Biomimicry, the study of the structure, characteristics, principles, 
and behavior of biological systems, has long provided profound 
insights into human technological and engineering endeavors[24]. 
Particularly, biomechanics, which studies the structure and func-
tion of a living body in mechanical methods, is considered one 
of the largest specialty areas in biomimicry, inspiring innovative 
concepts and working principles, such as designs from the stream-
lined forms of ships drawn from fsh to the structural designs of 
441

aircraft informed by bird. For children, delving into the biomechan-
ics knowledge behind diverse animal locomotion not only enriches 
their biological and mechanical knowledge but also lays a founda-
tion for future innovative thinking [63]. Therefore, we discerned the 
opportunity to introduce and integrate this captivating discipline 
into STEAM education. 
The integration of Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and 
Mathematics, also known as STEAM education, has become in-
creasingly important across all levels of schooling. Robotics has 
emerged as a promising teaching tool in this feld [50], particularly 
in its ability to mimic the movements of animals and help children 
develop practical skills and innovative thinking. However, while 
programmable robots ofer a wide range of creative possibilities, 
their bulky mechanical components (e.g., motors and servos) [15] 
can make it difcult to replicate the fuid and fexible movements 
of animals, such as the graceful glide of fsh or rhythmic wriggle 
of worms, which may stife and limit children’s imagination. Soft 
robots have been used extensively in biomimicry design [1, 18], 
and yet their fabrication and pneumatic control processes make 
them challenging to employ in STEAM education. 
This paper introduces KiPneu — a modular, electronics-free pneu-
matic robot platform tailored for K-6 STEAM education. Combining 
Lego-compatible bricks as constraint shells and reusable pneumatic 
actuators, KiPneu allows the creation of fexible structures to mimic 
versatile natural animal locomotion, from swimming turtles and 
crawling insects to running cheetahs or even robots that move 
in unique ways sparked by their own imagination. Additionally, 
KiPneu ofers electronics-free tangible control valves, which are 
user-friendly and amenable to programming, to empower impro-
vised creations with immediate movements. Moreover, a teaching 
interface that conveys all the fundamental principles of KiPneu 
function and biomimicry knowledge is provided to facilitate the 
learning process (Fig. 1). To evaluate our design, we conducted 
multi-session workshops with 21 children aged between 4 and 12 
years (with an average age of 9.6). Our results indicate that KiP-
neu not only enhanced children’s understanding of the principles 
of animal locomotion but also stimulated their biomimetic design 
thinking, encouraging them to draw inspiration from natural move-
ments and apply them to further creation. 
In summary, this paper presents the following contributions: 
• We develop a constructive and intuitive pneumatic kit con-
sisting of multiple Lego-compatible bricks and modular pneu-
matic actuators, allowing for rapid assembly of fexible de-
formation robots. 
• We designed a set of tangible pneumatic valves that allow 
multiple pneumatic actuators without electronics and pro-
gramming, realizing pneumatic control with lower barriers 
and higher accessibility. 
• We build a digital interface for KiPneu tutorials that provide 
biomimicry knowledge with corresponding building instruc-
tions. The interface also includes a challenge community for 
creative exploration. 
• We evaluate the KiPneu platform through workshops and 
questionnaires that ofer insights into fostering biomimetic 
design thinking in children through STEAM education. 
2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 
2.1 Biomimicry in STEAM Education 
Biomimetics is a relatively novel interdisciplinary science. Under 
the defnition provided by ISO, biomimetics is described as drawing 
inspiration from biological structures based on the principle of 
similarity, thereby transferring principles of biological systems into 
technological designs[24]. As its application deepens, integrating 
biomimetics into the educational feld has emerged as an important 
research focus. Merve et al. [9]implemented a ’Biomimicry teaching 
approach’ in local elementary schools, thoroughly demonstrating 
the impact of biomimetic concepts on fostering students’ creative 
ideation. The opportunity of biomimetics for the educational sector 
lies in its ability to spark students’ interest in technology through 
the intrinsic allure of biological solutions while simultaneously 
igniting enthusiasm for the natural world through an understanding 
of technology[58]. 
However, despite the technological achievements of biomimetic 
concepts in felds such as architecture[3], industrial design[18], and 
robotics engineering[17], the ingenious integration of biomimetics 
with technology has seldom been leveraged to inspire the educa-
tional sector. Only a select few universities, organizations, and large 
educational institutions have begun experimenting with transmit-
ting biomimetics as a systematic body of knowledge. Kelley and 
colleagues have incorporated biomimetic knowledge into integrated 
STEM units[27], while the Bionics Kit targets older students, facili-
tating the construction of specifc biomimetic models[15]. 
Recognizing the signifcant role of biomimetics in STEAM edu-
cation, we introduced KiPneu, an endeavor to integrate biomimetic 
motion structures into the educational sphere. KiPneu merges simu-
lation with physical interaction in a pedagogical format, serving as 
an educational toolkit for hands-on creation and a digital means to 
impart knowledge of motion structures. This initiative aims to en-
hance children’s understanding of fundamental scientifc concepts 
and provide them with an initial experience of the interdisciplinary 
nature of modern science, fostering an appreciation for a novel and 
reliable method of inter-disciplinary interaction. 
2.2 Tangible Interface in STEAM Education 
According to the work of Horn et al.[22], children demonstrate 
a preference for tangible interfaces over virtual ones. Tangible 
and modular interaction methods have enhanced beginners’ ac-
cess to creative activities[42]. Bosu[48], with its tangible program-
ming functionality based on kinetic memory, has provided new 
perspectives for kinetic design. Topobo[50], with the ability to 
memorize dynamic movements and replay physical actions, is a 
3D construction system for children, enabling them to assemble 
dynamic biomimetic forms such as animals and skeletons quickly. 
PneuBots[69] has introduced a low-cost entry-level approach to soft 
robotics construction. MakerWear[26] has brought a new wearable 
construction kit to children, utilizing tangible, modular methods 
for wearable creation, engaging them in creating computational 
designs. Such works[59, 62, 70, 72] collectively highlight the signif-
icant advantages and feasibility of tangible toolkits for beginners 
and children.

Beyond traditional assembly kits, novel forms have emerged 
where information technology is further applied. BacPack[36] fea-
tures tangible markers and a large multi-touch table, ofering a 
hands-on experience in bio-design. RoBlocks[55] combines robotic 
modules with a software suite, encouraging users to program by 
connecting modules, analyze their structure via screen tools, and 
reprogram their creations. Game-based learning experiences have 
made compatibility with LEGO bricks a signifcant consideration 
in toolkit construction[29, 32, 65]. For instance, Legoons [65] com-
bines silicone infatable objects with LEGO bricks, introducing these 
non-traditional Infatable Bricks to spark new scientifc curiosity 
among users. 
In terms of knowledge transmission, although works related to 
soft robotics intend to convey knowledge about kinetics[29, 31, 50, 
65], there is a general lack of comprehensive toolkits that cover var-
ious types of biomimetic motion structures to connect and establish 
a comprehensive construction of biomimetic forms. 
KiPneu aims to explore the application of tangible interfaces in 
STEM education further. It favors using simple digital and electrical 
systems to facilitate intuitive and tangible interactions, ofering 
a lower barrier to entry and greater accessibility. By integrating 
pneumatic toys, innovative valve designs, and software develop-
ment, KiPneu provides children with an engaging, interactive, and 
immersive learning experience to foster their interest in science and 
creative thinking. Concurrently, we strive to balance abstraction, 
openness, and complexity in UI design, providing ample sample 
guidance to ensure a child-friendly, tailor-made user experience. 
2.3 Soft Robotics and Pneumatic 
Shape-Changing Interface 
In the natural world, animals harness soft structures to navigate 
and move profciently within intricate external environments. Such 
inherent fexibility has raised the interest of researchers, leading 
them to probe into its applicability within soft robotics [10]. Lever-
aging the intrinsic properties of soft robotic actuators to reduce 
the complexities in both mechanical and algorithmic dimensions, 
coupled with the ability to control physical structures for a more 
intuitive user interface programmatically, has emerged as a signif-
icant research focus in the feld of HCI, spanning across interac-
tive devices [7], wearable technologies [35], and haptic feedback 
mechanisms [13]. To adjust the shape-changing behavior of the 
pneumatic interface, it is vital for researchers to either modulate 
the stifness of infated confgurations within homogeneous mate-
rials [8, 25, 37, 47] or to design composite structures that respond 
to diverse localized constraints [28, 39, 64, 66]. Luo et al.[39], for 
instance, have showcased soft pneumatic actuators crafted using 
machine knitting, integrated with sensory functions suitable for 
soft robotics and assistive wearables. Key innovations also comprise 
linear actuators mimicking the functionality of muscles [16, 28] and 
the strategic placement of airfow channels in elastic substances 
for optimized braking actions [41, 43]. 
In response to the challenges of interacting with soft materi-
als during manufacturing and assembly, modular designs provide 
novices with the chance to do interactive design. InfatiBits [29] 
ofers a specialized silicone chamber toolkit designed explicitly 
for educational purposes in soft robotics. Meanwhile, Legoon [65] 
seamlessly melds traditional LEGO bricks with silicone chambers, 
creating assembleable infatable toys. SoBL[32] facilitates multiple 
connection methods between pneumatic soft modules. MiuraKit 
[11] emphasizes using readily crafted origami structures to enhance 
the efciency of constructing a pneumatic interface. However, ac-
tuators with preset designs face difculties in customization and 
reconfguration. To address this, researchers suggest using external 
constraints to fne-tune the motion of soft robots and individualize 
pneumatic interface [44]. 
KiPneu utilizes basic silicone air chambers as actuators while 
employing 3D-printed modular bricks as external constraint shells. 
Compared with previous research, KiPneu’s shell adopts a universal 
plug-in method similar to Lego and more references to the struc-
ture of animals in deformation. This easy-to-assemble and highly 
expandable design enables users to conveniently create biomimetic 
structures with rich forms. Considering the need for safety and 
lowering the threshold of use, KiPneu adopts a manual infation 
method. However, this also leads to the current inability of KiPneu 
to achieve soft robots with automated control through program-
ming and a relatively weak pneumatic actuation force. 
2.4 Electrics-Free Pneumatic Device Controls 
In the realm of biomimetic soft robotics control, from the perspec-
tives of mechanical engineering, material science, and control the-
ory, soft actuator execution structures largely utilize a rigid pump 
body and electronic components to achieve regular motion[49] 
or employ materials sensitive to environmental changes to real-
ize specifc deformation states. Through these methods, a variety 
of biomimetic structure locomotions have been achieved, such as 
walking[30], swimming[14], jumping[1], and rolling[46]. In terms 
of pneumatic control, pneumatic control devices serve as intermedi-
aries for interaction between the driving source and the controlled 
device, featuring potent purely electronic structures[21, 56]. To 
improve human-machine interaction, innovations from the control 
input side have emerged, such as using the characteristics of pneu-
matic devices to measure intrinsic air pressure values for adjusting 
pneumatic actuation[61] and altering the movement trajectory of 
pneumatic robots through interaction with responsive materials 
like liquid crystal elastomers[20]. 
While fuid control devices possess considerable capabilities, 
they still exhibit shortcomings in certain application scenarios. 
Complex control methods are disadvantageous for designs that 
facilitate children’s understanding of biomimetic motion structures, 
creating obstacles in users’ cognition of the logical relationship 
between air valves and controlled pneumatic devices. Embracing 
the concept of expanding usage scenarios, designs for circuit-free 
air valve structures have gradually emerged in the HCI feld. For 
instance, in fuidic computation, airlogic introduces mechanical 
logic gates into the fuidic domain using 3D printing[54]. However, 
its reliance on airfow interactions leads to some air loss. The fuidic 
computation kit builds a more refned fuidic computation platform 
using unconventional mechanical computing[38]. The emergence 
of gas-controlled "diodes" and "transistors"[23], along with fully soft 
bistable air valves[12], has laid the groundwork for the development 
of entirely pneumatic complex devices[12].

shapes and colors. This design allows children to intuitively grasp

3 PILOT STUDIES AND DESIGN GOALS 
To ensure that KiPneu efectively imparts knowledge during play, 
we conducted a pilot study with children after completing the ini-
tial minimum viable prototype of KiPneu (Fig. 2), which comprised 
6 fundamental deformation modules and 3 non-electric control 
valves, powered entirely by an air pump, without an integrated in-
structional interface. Subsequently, we engaged in in-depth design 
discussions with STEAM education experts based on the results of 
this research. In light of this feedback and fndings, we identifed 
our design objectives and made further refnements to the fnal 
prototype. 
3.1 Pilot Studies 
Figure 2: Overview of prototype used for the pilot study, 
which includes various fundamental deformation modules, 
non-electric control valves, Arduino-controlled pneumatic 
system, and connecting structures. 
To test KiPneu prototype, we conducted a 2-hour session of ex-
ploratory research with 10 children aged 5-12 (4 female, average age 
= 8.7). The session commenced with an introduction to the function-
alities and usage methods of the various KiPneu modules, followed 
by a demonstration of a "swimming turtle" example crafted with 
KiPneu. The children were then given 1 hour of free exploration 
time, during which we encouraged them to utilize KiPneu to de-
sign robots that simulate animal movements. After this exploration 
session, we conducted semi-structured interviews with each par-
ticipant, inquiring about their interest in KiPneu, comprehension 
of the module functionalities, challenges faced during play, types 
of animal movements they wished to create, and the diferences 
they observed compared to traditional building blocks they had 
previously used. 
We observed a pronounced curiosity among children toward 
the deformation blocks. Without any external prompting, they 
naturally associated these blocks with animal movements. Before 
the ofcial commencement of the workshop, the children sponta-
neously tried to use the basic deformation modules upon entering 
the classroom. For instance, one girl excitedly commented, "This 
grey module, when curled, looks like a shrimp!" Throughout the 
workshop, they were able to easily grasp the fundamental assembly 
techniques of the deformation modules and, based on this, designed 
robots that could perform basic motions. 
They also crafted stories revolving around these robots. 
Despite these positive observations, our experimental logs high-
lighted several common issues: 
• Younger children tend to design relatively simpler structures. 
They are more inclined to use a single module and stack 
other blocks on top of it. 
• Most children seldom utilized the control valves to diversify 
the robot’s movements. As one 9-year-old refected, "Each 
valve looks quite similar; I fnd it hard to distinguish their 
diferences and how to use them accurately. " 
• Implementing Arduino programming to control the air pumps 
for stable robot movement proved challenging for the chil-
dren. During robot motion testing, extended infation times 
frequently resulted in damage to both the air core and the 
valves. 
• Our designed blocks could only be attached to Lego from a 
single direction. Furthermore, due to size discrepancies, the 
connection with Lego wasn’t particularly sturdy, undoubt-
edly limiting the children’s creative scope. 
3.2 Expert Interview 
Upon completing the Pilot Studies, we conducted interviews with 
fve professional STEM experts who have four to ten years of ex-
perience in STEM education. These experts have provided STEM 
education to students aged 6 to 12 in biology, physics, science, 
and technology practice courses. We held 2-hour semi-structured 
interviews with the experts. First, we presented the current proto-
type, jointly reviewed the Pilot Studies’ experimental records, and 
collected their feedback. Additionally, during the interviews, we 
specifcally asked the experts about their views on the application 
of constructivist toolkits in the educational feld, including their 
opinions on the toolkits’ specifc advantages and the signifcance of 
learning biomimicry for children. Finally, we discussed how KiPneu 
would iterate in the future and its design goals through a brain-
storming session. After completing the interviews, we analyzed 
the interview records using thematic analysis[6] and iterative open 
coding[57]. Two researchers coded the recordings and evaluated 
emerging themes, and the co-authors discussed the research results 
iteratively until a consensus was reached. 
In response to the challenges encountered during Pilot Stud-
ies, experts in the STEAM feld suggested the need to refne the 
workshop process. Firstly, the principles of animal movement and 
the inner structure of tangible valves should be elucidated with 
animated presentations. Subsequently, children should be guided to 
construct robots that simulate animal locomotion based on tutorial 
examples. Once they have attained a profound understanding of

3.3 KiPneu Design Principles and Goals 
Based on pilot studies and interviews with STEAM educators, we 
established the following fve design principles and goals for KiP-
neu: 
Learning through play: Interest is the best teacher. Our goal is 
to create a fun and educational experience for children, allowing 
them to naturally acquire knowledge during playtime. To achieve 
this vision, we combine children’s innate interest in animals with 
biomimicry to stimulate their curiosity and interest. Through ac-
tive participation, practice, and exploration, children can build and 
develop their knowledge and understanding. 
Supporting exploration: As Resnick et al. mentioned[51], users 
must be able to easily experiment and backtrack when unsuccess-
ful; tools must be "self-revealing, " allowing users to clearly under-
stand their capabilities. In the process of creating mobile pneumatic 
robots, iteration and repeated experimentation are essential steps. 
Even if children do not follow the tutorial for building, they can 
easily undo operations when encountering failures and quickly try 
diferent building schemes. 
Growing alongside: For very young children who have just been 
introduced to KiPneu, it should be ergonomic and intuitive, allowing 
them to easily get started without any specialized knowledge related 
to soft robot design and manufacturing. As their skills and cognition 
gradually mature, these tools and resources are designed to guide 
children in constructing more complex robots, ensuring that their 
learning needs are met from early childhood to advanced cognitive 
development stages. 
4 THE KIPNEU SYSTEM 
KiPneu (Fig. 3) primarily consists of three components: (i) Construc-
tive Bricks. Through the modular combination of bricks and Soft 
Pneumatic Actuators, KiPneu can simulate fexible deformations 
that closely mimic animal locomotion.; (ii) Electronics-free Tangi-
ble Valves. By programming these user-friendly tangible valves, 
various movements can be imparted to the creations; (iii) Teach-
ing Interface. We can vividly demonstrate the ways animals move. 
This not only guides children in building corresponding models 
but also encourages them to delve deep into understanding the 
unique behaviors of animals. " This section introduces the design 
and production of these three components of the kit. 
4.1 Constructive Bricks 
4.1.1 Combining shell-bricks with sof pneumatic actuators to create 
deformation modules. In our eforts to enable KiPneu to vividly and 
accurately emulate various animal locomotion styles with as few 
components as possible, we consulted the categorization of animal 
movements summarized by R. McNeill Alexander et al. [2]. Based 
on this, we further subdivided these movements into two primary 
categories: the frst encompasses motions achieved through a single 
reciprocating action, while the second involves compound motions 
necessitating the combination of multiple actions. We performed a 
fexible deformation simulation for each individual reciprocating 
action, thereby determining the seven types of deformation modules 
we intended to design (Fig. 5). Specifcally, these modules include 
Single-axis bending, such as the primary lift of a crab’s leg along 
the x-axis (Fig. 5a) and the leg motion of Acilius sliding in the y-axis 
direction during rowing (Fig. 5b). Dual-axis bending, exemplifed by 
the fapping of a penguin’s fipper on both the Y and Z axes during 
swimming (Fig. 5c). Tri-axis bending, as in the wing movement of a 
bird during fight, oscillates along the XYZ axes (Fig. 5d). Elongation 
motion is like the elongation and contraction of a worm’s body. 
Twisting motions, such as the body twist of a fsh when changing 
direction. Infation patterns, like a puferfsh adjusting its position 
in water through body expansion and contraction. 
To elucidate in detail how KiPneu achieves fexible deformation, 
this paper takes the fundamental uni-directional bending Defor-
mation(Fig. 4) Module as an exemplar. The Deformation Module 
primarily consists of two essential components: the shell-bricks 
that serve as a restrictive layer and the Soft Pneumatic Actuators. 
Fig. 4a illustrates the 3D digital models of these two parts. The 
shell-bricks are 3D printed with PLA material on the Raise 3D E2. 
PLA was chosen due to its attributes that align with the requisite 
characteristics for interlocking and silicone embedding, including 
an appropriate surface roughness. The top and bottom surfaces 
of the shell-bricks are designed with a plug-in structure compati-
ble with Lego bricks, aiming to enhance the module’s scalability, 
thereby allowing children to explore and experience a plethora 
of design forms. Additionally, shell-bricks can be seamlessly and 
closely connected through ball joints on both sides of their base, 
allowing for rotation between them. The Soft Pneumatic Actuators 
are cast using Smooth-on Ecofex 00-30 silicone, a skin-friendly, soft, 
and stretchable material that does not permanently deform and is 
extensively used in various toys. All Soft Pneumatic Actuators are 
designed with a hollow rectangular shape internally. In operation, 
each shell-brick is frst connected via ball joints and is then nested 
on the surface of the Soft Pneumatic Actuators. Subsequently, the 
Soft Pneumatic Actuators are infated and pressurized. Due to the 
seamless and tight connection at the base of the shell-bricks, which 
limits the expansion of the Soft Pneumatic Actuators, the Actuators 
can only expand above the shell-bricks. This results in the bending 
deformation of the overall structure of the Deformation Module 
(Fig. 4b3). Furthermore, we can enhance the deformation efects of

4.1.2 Utilizing decorative-bricks and connect-bricks to facilitate 
the creation of more movement forms. Considering the modular-
ity of building block components, our kit also includes two crit-
ical non-infatable elements: "decorative blocks" and "connector 
blocks. "During the initial research phase, our goal was to partner 
with Lego— a leader in the building block domain—making the 
KiPnue worth playing. Since Lego doesn’t ofer specialized kits 
specifcally for soft robotics, we have included various function-

such multi-functional blocks in the Kipneu kit not only adds to 
the aesthetic value of the constructed shapes but also extends the 
functional capabilities of the soft robots. 
The kit also contains other auxiliary parts, such as rubber tube 
connectors, air plugs, and brick connectors. Connectors and air 
plugs help to realize a system with good air tightness. In contrast, 
the block connectors with various connection structures help to

KiPneu’s tangible valves build upon the principle of tube-kinking, 
which is a reliable and substantiated logic control mechanism in 
electronics-free fuid control [12, 53]. Since silicone tubes can be 
axially compressed, the external mechanical structures can link 
the tube and attain a critical compression threshold. At this point, 
it undergoes a bistable snapping transition, efectively occluding 
the internal airfow. We combine the instabilities of the kinking 
phenomena in tubular structure and the spatial positioning between 
tangible components to build intuitive valves with simple fnger-
initiated interactions like press and slide. Due to the use of air tubes 
as the controlled medium, there is excellent connectivity between 
the valves, providing users with a conducive creative environment. 
Press Valve. A self-locking spring mechanism is embedded within 
the valve(Fig. 7a), leveraging the instability of the button position 
in conjunction with the kinking instability of the air tube. When 
the button is pressed, the tube kinks and obstructs airfow. Upon 
subsequent actuation of the button, it is released, allowing the tube 
to return to its original state and enabling normal airfow.

5 TEACHING INTERFACE 
KiPneu ofers a complementary interactive teaching interface to 
enhance children’s observation and understanding of animal loco-
motion. This platform guides children using shape-shifting modules 
and tangible valves to construct robots that simulate animal loco-
motion. 
The interface consists of three parts: Assembly Tutorial, Valve 
Principle (Fig. 11), and Challenge Community. Section 5.1 will elab-
orate on the Assembly Tutorial interface. The Valve Principle en-
ables children to better understand airfow and valve control prin-
ciples through visual presentations. The Challenge Community en-
courages children to create animal-inspired designs and practice 
biomimetic locomotion.[71] 
5.1 Interface Workfow 
In this section, we demonstrate how our teaching interface intro-
duces children to the principles of turtle locomotion by using the 
construction of a robotic turtle simulation as an example. 
5.1.1 Observation: animal locomotion. The children initially watched 
video clips of various animal movement characteristics in the real 
world, followed by corresponding simplifed motion schematic dia-
grams. After watching these video instructions, the children were 
allowed to interactively explore the relationship between animal 
structures and their locomotion with the animations in the software 
(Fig. 10 a1, a2). 
5.1.2 Learning and imitation: constructing animals based on the 
teaching interface. Based on prior understanding and the provided 
instructional interface, we supplied children with the KiPneu con-
struction kit comprising 4 soft pneumatic actuators, 4 white de-
formation modules, and 2 squeeze infators. Utilizing these tools, 
children were able to sequentially assemble a robot that mimics the 
movement of a sea turtle following the guidance of the instructional 
interface (Fig. 10 b1,b2). 
5.1.3 Practice: utilizing tangible valves to construct more animal 
locomotion. Utilizing two Switch Valves allows for controlling the 
diverse movement directions of the sea turtle. When both Switch 
Valves are activated, the sea turtle’s legs a & d and b & c move 
alternately, propelling the turtle forward (Fig. 10 c1, c2). Conversely, 
when both Switch Valves are deactivated, either the a & c or b & 
d legs of the turtle move synchronously, facilitating a left or right 
turn (Fig. 10 d1, d2). 
5.1.4 Creation: construct inspired by animal cocomotion. Lastly, our 
proposed Challenge Community Interface aims to inspire children 
to iteratively test and refne their models. Through this platform, 
children are encouraged to design robots inspired by the natu-
ral movements of animals, delving deeply into the principles of 
biomimicry. Once they have completed their creative constructions 
using the KiPneu, they can share their creations within the com-
munity interface. 
6 USER STUDY 
To assess the extent to which KiPneu achieves our design goal 
of getting children interested in, excited about, and inspired by 
biomimicry, we designed and conducted a short-term user study 
with the following research questions: 
• RQ 1: Is KiPneu appealing to children, and does it capture 
their interest? 
• RQ 2: Can children learn and operate KiPneu smoothly? 
• RQ 3: Does using KiPneu enhance children’s understanding 
of animal locomotion? 
• RQ 4: Can children draw inspiration from nature? 
In this user study, we recruited 21 children (10 female) aged 5-12 
(M=9.6; SD=2.2) from an elementary school afliated with a local 
university. Parents were present at the activity, not providing con-
structive guidance or assistance but ofering emotional reassurance 
and support. 
6.1 Procedure 
The workshops in this study were conducted over three days in a 
row, with one session held each day. Each session was organized and 
hosted by a team of three researchers and took place in classrooms 
at our research institution. We have audio- and video-recorded the 
entire process for documentation and further analysis. The detailed 
process of the workshops (Fig. 12) is as follows: 
6.1.1 Session 1: Traditional Teaching Methods and Initial Assess-
ment. We endeavored to establish a positive rapport with the chil-
dren in the frst session. Subsequently, we employed traditional 
PowerPoint presentations supplemented with video clips illustrat-
ing various animal locomotion and explanatory lectures to teach 
the children about animal locomotion. Upon completing the in-
structional session, the children were asked to use a black pen to 
complete an initial assessment task designed to evaluate their un-
derstanding of animal locomotion (detailed in section 6.2.1). After 
the assessment, we introduced the children to the functionalities 
of KiPneu. Subsequently, participants were given 15 minutes to 
explore and play with KiPneu freely. 
6.1.2 Session 2: Teaching with Kipneu and Evaluation. In this ses-
sion, we employed the KiPneu instructional interface for teaching 
children about animal locomotion (detailed in section 5.1). After 
the instruction was concluded, the children revisited the questions 
from session 1 using a blue pen to evaluate the learning outcomes. 
6.1.3 Session 3: Gaming and Qestionnaire. The third session aimed 
to allow children to craft their nature-inspired designs based on 
animal locomotion, thereby practicing biomimicry. During this 
phase, children could propose design concepts and construct robots 
using KiPneu. Upon completion, they were encouraged to share 
and evaluate their design ideas and robots with their peers. After 
the activity, participants were asked to complete a questionnaire 
to assess their interaction experience with KiPneu, their level of 
interest, and their satisfaction with their creations. 
To gain deeper insights into the participants’ user experience 
and expectations, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 
each participant. As a token of appreciation for their participation, 
each child received a gift valued at $10. This study has passed the

6.2 Measures 
6.2.1 Evaluating children’s understanding of animal locomotion. 
We developed a comprehensive assessment test question to investi-
gate the cognitive changes in children’s understanding of animal 
locomotion mechanisms following short-term instruction (See ap-
pendix Fig. 14). In constructing this assessment, we drew references 
from middle school biology examination questions on animal loco-
motion and incorporated literature on animal behavior [2], child 
development [68], and questionnaire design [52]. This test encom-
passes various types of questions: multiple-choice, matching, and 
image-based questions primarily assess children’s basic knowledge 
and identifcation capabilities of animal locomotion. Short-answer 
and situational questions aim to examine children’s in-depth under-
standing and reasoning of animal locomotion, assessing their ability 
to describe and infer animal movements under specifc scenarios 
or conditions. Specifcally, these questions are designed to evaluate 
holistically children’s foundational knowledge on particular modes 
of animal locomotion, such as how animals move and which body 
parts they utilize for locomotion, refecting their understanding 
and cognition at diferent levels. 
6.2.2 Qestionnaire and semi-structured interviews. In the initial 
phase of our study, we collected basic information from partic-
ipants through a questionnaire, including their age, grade, and 
prior relevant experience. To gain deeper insights into the partici-
pants’ user experience with KiPneu, we designed and distributed 
another questionnaire later in our research. This questionnaire 
primarily investigated the following aspects: the usability and com-
prehensibility of KiPneu, the level of interest when using it, sat-
isfaction with their creations, intentions to use KiPneu again in 
the future, and cognitive understanding of diferent transformation 
modules, electronics-free tangible valves, and pneumatic robots. 
The questionnaire content combined closed-ended Likert-scale [19] 
questions with semi-structured interviews. We had dedicated re-
searchers read the questions for younger child participants and 
record their responses. We reported the mean (M) and standard 
deviation (SD) for the Likert scale questions, with a maximum score 
of 5 being the best. All names used are pseudonyms (age, gender). 
We analyzed the activity recordings using a thematic coding 
approach that integrates both inductive and deductive methods 
[6] Two researchers crafted an initial coding manual based on 
the research objectives and pilot study experiences. This manual 
encompassed key information such as the level of focus, challenges 
encountered during the operation and their resolutions, and choices 
of referencing animal locomotion.

7.2 User-Friendly Design Facilitating Children 
in Constructing Pneumatic Robots 
In initial game experiences, children often require more guidance 
due to unfamiliarity with the controls. We observed that many chil-
dren did not evenly arrange the shell-bricks on the soft pneumatic 
actuators when manipulating the red elongation module. This fre-
quently led to situations where the soft pneumatic actuators would 
"bubble up, " as described by Lucas (age 10, boy). We also found 
that younger children were more inclined to experiment with the 
pre-assembled transformation modules we provided repeatedly. In 
contrast, older children quickly began to disassemble the toys, re-
moving the soft pneumatic actuators from the shell-bricks bricks to 
investigate the underlying transformation mechanism. Although all 
participants had some understanding of the brick transformations, 
those who had disassembled the core had a more profound compre-
hension of its principles. When the connection arrangement of the 
shell-bricks was uneven, the older children proactively adjusted 
the spacing to allow the soft pneumatic actuators to infate evenly, 
achieving the desired state. 
After the frst round of the workshop, through interviews, we 
found that most children could determine the deformation efects 
based on the connections and appearances of the modules. However, 
Sophia (age 7, girl) forgot the impact of the purple deformation 
module. When asked whether "KiPneu is easy to understand and 
use?", 17 children rated it 5 out of 5 (M=4.6; SD=0.7). When we 
further inquired about its comparison with traditional LEGO motors 
or pulley modules, the consensus was that pneumatic actuators 
ofer a more streamlined drive mechanism for brick movements. 
In the second round of gameplay, after receiving guidance, older 
children could follow the instructions on the educational interface 
and correctly use the electronics-free tangible valves, endowing the 
same animal model with richer movement states. 
Furthermore, thanks to our decision to switch to squeeze infators 
as driving devices after the pilot study, there were hardly any cases 
of soft pneumatic actuator damage during the three workshop 
sessions. The only case of damage occurred when Benjamin (a 7-
year-old boy) applied excessive force, pulling out the silicone tube 
inside the soft pneumatic actuators. 
7.3 Enhancing Children’s Understanding of 
Animal Locomotion through Interactive 
Visualization interface 
After undergoing two rounds of instruction, frst utilizing tradi-
tional teaching methods and then the Kipneu teaching approach, we 
conducted evaluations after each session, amounting to two tests 
in total (for test details, see 6.2.1). These assessments were aimed 
at gauging the children’s comprehension of animal locomotion. 
Through scoring analysis of the test results, we found that students’ 
average scores increased from 54.9 (SD=17) to 82.0 (SD=8.0). In the 
multiple-choice section of the test, one question was: "Which part 
of a fsh swings to enable it to swim forward?" Before instruction, 
46% of students chose "fns" as the answer, while23% chose "belly". 
However, after instruction, the rate of students selecting the cor-
rect answer — "tail" — increased signifcantly, with 82% choosing 
this option. In the short-answer section, when asked, "How does a 
cheetah utilize its body to dash when chasing prey?" Harper (age 
12, girl) initially responded, "The cheetah runs very fast, using its 
legs and tail to chase prey." While this answer is close, it doesn’t 
capture the essence of the cheetah’s movement. After the instruc-
tion, her response improved, as exemplifed by: "The cheetah’s body 
takes on a bow shape, allowing it to sprint forward rapidly." In subse-
quent interviews, children generally expressed that through using 
the system, they gained a deeper understanding of how animals 
locomotion.

8 DISCUSSION 
8.1 Design Trade-Ofs : Why Teaching Children 
Animal Locomotion? 
The natural world harbors a multitude of strategies without explo-
ration and emulation. The purpose of biomimicry research is to 
delve into understanding how these strategies are simulated and cat-
egorize them into three primary classes: form-based, process-based, 
and system-based biomimicry. Precisely, form-based biomimicry 
emulates biological structures to achieve specifc functionalities; 
process-based focuses on the way materials are generated and their 
natural characteristics, while system-based biomimicry integrates 
all the biomimetic principles to manifest as an ecosystem inspired by 
nature, spanning from manufacturing to transportation [40]. How-
ever, it’s challenging to present the depth and breadth of biomimicry 
to children using a single educational tool. Hence, KiPneu, consid-
ering the complexity levels appropriate for diferent age groups in 
biomimicry education, opts to integrate the foundational aspects 
of biomimicry into STEAM education, allowing children to explore 
nature through observing and mimicking animal movements. 
Although we can simulate many animal movements by freely 
combining KiPneu’s fexible deformation modules with tangible 
valves, it’s important to note that animal motion isn’t exclusively re-
liant on fexible structures. For instance, while their leg joints ofer 
limited fexion, ants and spiders primarily rely on their rigid ex-
oskeletons for support and force transmission. In our experiments, 
we observed that children’s creativity was somewhat constrained 
due to the absence of support from rigid components. To address 
this challenge, we are exploring modular pneumatic mechanical 
structures that integrate with our existing fexible modules. 
8.2 Flexible Shape-changing as a Medium to 
Enhance Children’s Imagination 
The adoption of fexible shape-changing is rapidly expanding across 
diverse devices and contexts, primarily due to its introduction of 
a novel and immersive mode of interaction that engages multi-
ple senses [45, 67]. Firstly, the fexible physical properties of these 
materials enhance the safety of educational tools, mitigating the 
potential harm associated with traditional rigid toys. More impor-
tantly, fexible deformation allows for more intuitive shapes and 
structures, as well as movements that closely mimic natural motion, 
surpassing the limitations of conventional toys in terms of their 
interactive possibilities [65]. 
In our study of fexible deformation modeling tools, we discerned 
that they transcend mere tangible entities and serve as innovative 
mediums for exploration. They profer a boundary-less creative 
arena for children, potent in sparking and fostering their imagi-
native and inventive faculties. Through tactile interactions such 
as touching, pinching, twisting, and reshaping, children can delve 
into understanding the multifaceted characteristics of objects, con-
currently honing their cognitive, manual, and spatial recognition 
skills. For instance, while observing a specifc elongated defor-
mation module, one child analogized it to a lizard extending its 
tongue. In contrast, another perceived it as akin to the undulating 
motion of a worm. This discovery underscores the advantages of 
integrating fexible deformation technology into educational tools. 
Compared to traditional motor-driven toys, this pneumatic and 
adaptable approach more naturally aligns with biological princi-
ples and the movements observed in nature, allowing children to 
transcend the conventional constraints of form and function during 
interaction. This openness stimulates creative expression among 
children and provides new perspectives for exploring shapes, func-
tions, and underlying narratives, further nurturing their creativity. 
Each deformation experience challenges and enhances the chil-
dren’s cognitive and imaginative abilities, opening a new window 
for them to understand the world.

processes [33]. Unlike traditional construction kits that require pro-
gramming or electronic skills, KiPneu features a design compatible 
with LEGO bricks and a manual, non-electrical infation mecha-
nism that signifcantly lowers the barrier to entry. This design lets 
children quickly start and explore various construction possibilities

8.4 Promoting Biomimetic Thinking in 
Children through STEAM Education 
In evaluating students’ fnal projects on biomimetic designs, we no-
ticed that older students (ffth and sixth graders) clearly referenced 
the functional structures of animal movements. This suggests their 
design approach transcended mere imitation of biological appear-
ances, delving instead into a deeper understanding and application 
of true biomimicry functions. For instance, using KiPneu, children 
constructed machines mimicking the trunk of an elephant as an 
excavator, patrol boats resembling sea turtles, clamps inspired by 
squids, and gliders emulating eagles. 
In fact, in a case study by Bakirlioglu and Yekta [4], even master’s 
students in industrial design education formally applied biomimicry. 
Similarly, in research by Boga-Akyol and Timur-Ogut [5], students 
of industrial design "tended to mimic the physical forms of nature. " 
Moreover, not just students but even professional industrial de-
signers found it challenging to move beyond mimicking nature’s 
physical forms to realize natural biological mechanisms [60]. 
The profound understanding of biomimicry demonstrated by 
older students in their designs can be attributed to the ample sup-
port they received in inspiration and knowledge. This reinforces 
the validity of the notion that children with ’formal operational’ 
skills (typically aged 11 and above) are fully capable of nurturing 
and developing a biomimetic design mindset when given adequate 
education and guidance [9]. 
9 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 
In this section, we shall expound upon several limitations of our 
work and explore potential directions for future study. To further 
enhance and delve into KiPneu’s development, we’ve identifed a 
few key areas to focus on. 
Expanded User Testing. Although initial feedback from a three-
day workshop indicated the potential value of KiPneu, its long-term 
educational impact still requires further exploration. To evaluate 
its educational benefts comprehensively, we intend to conduct an 
extensive study over several weeks, collaborating with more schools 
and educational institutions. Our objective is to engage children 
from diverse age groups, thereby ensuring a more comprehensive 
representation in our research. 
Age-adapted Curriculums. Although KiPneu ofers an innovative 
bio-inspired learning experience for children, we acknowledge its 
limitations in curriculum design and difculty grading. Currently, 
KiPneu’s instructional content does not cater to children of vary-
ing ages and abilities, which can lead to younger children feeling 
overwhelmed by overly complex subjects, while older children may 
perceive the content as overly simplistic. To address this, we plan 
to collaborate with child education experts in the future to refne 
KiPneu’s curriculum. Our goal is to draw inspiration from success-
ful models like Lego Education [34] to develop a structured and 
progressive teaching outline, ensuring that children of all ages and 
abilities can access a tailored learning experience. 
Diverse Knowledge Domains. We have observed that KiPneu’s 
pneumatic platform includes several non-biomimetic components, 
such as pneumatic controls and basic logic gates. This presents an 
opportunity to introduce fundamental engineering and scientifc 
knowledge that goes beyond the boundaries of biomimicry. In this

10 CONCLUSION 
The study introduces KiPneu, a modular, non-electronic pneu-
matic robot platform. Leveraging its design, coupled with LEGO-
compatible blocks and user-friendly pneumatic actuators. By lever-
aging the potential of non-electrical pneumatic actuators and tan-
gible programming, KiPneu facilitates an immersive, hands-on ex-
perience for learners to replicate and understand intricate animal 
locomotion mechanisms. Preliminary evaluation, as carried out in 
a workshop with children aged 5-12, underscores the platform’s 
dual capacity: reinforcing the understanding of natural locomotion 
principles and kindling an enthusiasm for innovative construction 
using the newly acquired insights. Through multiple workshops 
and pre-post evaluations, we observed a signifcant enhancement 
in participants’ understanding of animal locomotion, sparking their 
biomimetic design thinking and creativity. These fndings validate 
the potential of KiPneu in STEAM education, providing insights for 
future integration of soft robotics in biomimicry learning. As sup-
ported by its instructional interface, KiPneu’s combination of tactile 
and digital tools emerges as a promising paradigm in bolstering 
solution-oriented education and capturing the allure of biomimicry 
for the younger generation.
