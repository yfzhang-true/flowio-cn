Electropermanent Magnetic Muscles: Power-Ef ﬁcient,
Low-Voltage, Compact Magnetic Actuators to Power
Wearables and Untethered Robots
Arturo Castillo Ugalde,* Carina Carrasco, Brijesh Chavda, Thileepan Stalin,
and Pablo Valdivia y Alvarado*
1. Introduction
Artiﬁcial muscles play a pivotal role in advancing robotics and
human-machine interaction by providing actuation solutions
that are compliant, ef ﬁcient, and capable of generating linear
and complex motions without rigid mechanisms. [1–4] Their
potential applications extend across wearable devices, soft robot-
ics, and prosthetics, where attributes such as safety, portability,
and nature-like motion are critical. [5–8]
However, existing arti ﬁcial muscles face inherent limitations
dictated by their underlying transduction principles. For instance,
electrostatic actuators require high voltages, posing risks and hard-
ware complexity;[9–11] shape memory materials suffer from low
frequencies and poor ef ﬁciency;[12,13] and traditional ﬂexible ﬂu-
idic actuators rely on external pumps and pneumatic reservoirs,
making them tethered and less portable. [14–16] Overcoming these
challenges often requires addressing funda-
mental trade-offs inherent to their operating
principles.
These limitations are particularly evi-
dent in wearable technologies, such as
prosthetics, assistive devices, and human
augmentation systems, where the demand
for compact, powerful, and ef ﬁcient actua-
tors is critical. [7,17] These systems must
operate for extended periods while ensur-
ing safe and reliable interaction with users
in proximity.
Similarly, in robotics, the need for
energy-efﬁcient, compact, and untethered
actuators has driven research toward new
transducer materials that enhance both per-
formance and autonomy.[14] This is particu-
larly important for robots operating in con ﬁned spaces or harsh
environments, where extended mission duration and power ef ﬁ-
ciency are essential. [18]
Recent advances have also broadened the landscape of artiﬁcial-
muscle technologies beyond traditional transduction mechanisms.
Fiber- and yarn-based actuators have achieved large reversible
deformation and high power density by leveraging thermal or elec-
trothermal stimulation, while liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs)
have emerged as multistimulus materials that respond to heat,
light, or electrical ﬁelds.[19–23] These modalities represent impor-
tant and rapidly growing actuator classes. However, many of them
rely on continuously applied thermally induced or environment-
driven stimuli, such as temperature change, light absorption, or
humidity variations, which inherently limit actuation speed, con-
trollability, and energy efﬁciency.
Electromagnetic actuation, while primarily utilized in motor-
driven systems, presents an attrac tive alternative. It offers advan-
tages such as low operating voltages, fast response, and seamless
integration into existing technologies. Recently, ferromagnetic com-
posites have gained attention in electromagnetic actuation for
their ability to generate large, untethered forces. [24–27] However,
their practical use is often limited to controlled environments where
large magnetic ﬁeld generators are available, limiting their use in
portable applications. As a result , solenoid- and voice coil-based
actuators remain the most widely used solution for compact elec-
tromagnetic actuation. These actuators have been explored across a
range of applications, from rigid[28–30] to soft[31–34] and hybrid com-
pliant devices,[35] demonstrating their versatility in bio-inspired
robotics,[36,37] medical devices,[38] and soft robotic systems.[39,40]

Artiﬁcial muscles are essential for advancing bio-inspired robots, prosthetics, and
wearable devices, as they enable compliant, ef ﬁcient actuation without complex
mechanisms. Existing actuation technologies often face limitations such as high
voltage requirements, low efﬁciency, or tethering constraints. This work introduces
a solenoid-based actuator system utilizing soft-magnetic and hard-magnetic
materials to achieve portable bistable actuation, reducing energy consumption by
only requiring power during state transitions. The actuators demonstrate low-
voltage operation, competitive stress and strain performance, and high power
efﬁciency, making them ideal for untethered robots and wearable devices.
Preliminary applications in a prosthetic hand and an amphibious robot highlight
their potential in terms of low energy consumption, simplicity, and safety.

Despite these beneﬁts, electromagnetic artiﬁcial muscles, espe-
cially solenoid- and voice coil-based designs, are limited by high
energy consumption.[30,41] These devices typically require contin-
uous energy input to maintain actuation, leading to signi ﬁcant
heat generation, reduced efﬁciency, and constraints on their appli-
cation in human-interactive systems and untethered robots.
To address these challenges, Knaian [42] introduced electroper-
manent magnets (EPMs) as an energy-ef ﬁcient alternative for
electromagnetic connection systems. These devices leverage fer-
romagnetic materials with distinct magnetic properties to estab-
lish a bi-stable magnetic circuit. By applying brief energy pulses to
a solenoid, these devices achieve a controlled switch between mag-
netic attraction (EPM on) and nonmagnetic attraction (EPM off )
states, without requiring continuous power input. This principle
has facilitated the development of low-power electromagnetic
latching systems used in valves,[43–45] climbing[46,47] and reconﬁg-
urable robots,[48–50] low-speed high-torque motors, [51,52] human-
machine interfaces,[53] and electromagnetic suction cups. [54]
Building on this concept, this research paper proposes a
novel solenoid-based actuator system that extends the EPM
principle beyond latching mec hanisms to muscle-like actu-
ation. Unlike prior applications, which primarily focused on
static holding and switching functions, the proposed electroper-
manent magnetic muscles (EPMM) approach integrates both
soft-magnetic and hard-magneti c materials to actively generate
and sustain forces between the magnets without continuous
energy input. By leveraging this bistable magnetic interaction,
the EPMM design enables controlled muscle-like actuation with
minimal energy consumption, requiring power only to switch
between attraction (contraction) and repulsion (relaxation) states.
This innovation offers a low-voltage, compact, ef ﬁcient, and scal-
able solution for small-scale rigid and soft robotic systems, pre-
senting a signi ﬁcant advancement in electromagnetic actuator
technology for untethered robotics and wearable applications.
To validate the proposed actuator system, solenoid-wrapped
AlNiCo magnets paired with NdFeB magnets of various com-
mercial sizes were characterized. A systematic study on magnet
dimensions, speciﬁcally length and diameter, in ﬂuencing attrac-
tion and repulsion forces was conducted to determine the
actuator’s contraction force and stress output. In addition, the
energy required for magnetization was characterized, re ﬁning
the switching parameters such as pulse duration and voltage,
improving energy ef ﬁciency.
Building on these insights, actuator arrays were implemented
into a ﬁber arti ﬁcial muscle for tendon-driven grasping. These
were integrated into a prosthetic hand platform, where the actua-
tors replicated muscle-like function by pulling tendons to ﬂex
individual ﬁngers. The design enabled safe and reliable grasping
without continuous power input, requiring energy only to change
states.
To further explore the applications of the proposed actuator,
untethered locomotion through the design of an amphibious
robot was also demonstrated. By recon ﬁguring the actuator array
geometry and magnet polarity, compliant mechanisms for crawl-
ing and swimming were developed. This system demonstrated
underwater propulsion and terrestrial locomotion with a compact
structure using portable electronics.
Together, these results highlight the potential of our actuator
technology for wearable and autonomous robotic systems. The
compact design, low-voltage operation, and energy-efﬁcient bista-
ble behaviour offer a promising alternative to traditional arti ﬁcial
muscles in bio-inspired applications.
2. Results
2.1. Actuator Working Principle
The proposed actuation method exploits the magnetic interaction
between two ferromagnetic compounds: a hard-magnetic mate-
rial, Neodymium-Iron-Boron grade N35 (NIB), and a soft-
magnetic material, Aluminum-Nickel-Cobalt grade 5 (AlNiCo),
leveraging on its different magnetic properties to generate
actuation.
Ferromagnetic materials are known for their strong interac-
tion to magnetic ﬁelds. This is due to their ability to align their
internal magnetic domains in the direction of an external mag-
netic ﬁeld, generating a resultant magnetization force vector on
the material itself.
In the case of AlNiCo grade 5 and NIB N35, while both possess
a high remanence, which measures their ability to remain mag-
netized after the removal of the initial magnetization ﬁeld, they
differ signiﬁcantly in coercivity, which is a measure of the mate-
rial ability to preserve its magnetization, in other words, its ability
to keep its magnetic domains orientation under the in ﬂuence of
an external magnetic ﬁeld.
These key properties allow us to differentiate between soft-
magnetic, hard-magnetic, and super-paramagnetic materials,
depending on the material’s proﬁle on its demagnetization curve
or BH curves. [24]
By exploiting the low coercivity of AlNiCo, its magnetization
direction was controlled by applying short electromagnetic pulses
through a small solenoid wound around it. This resulted in con-
trollable attraction and repulsion forces during its interaction with
the NIB magnet, shown in Figure 1 . Building on this polarity-
interaction-controlled actuation concept, a simple linear array of
3 magnets was assembled. This magnetic array contracts and
relaxes, in a bistable way, by the direction of the controlled electric
pulse applied on its solenoid. This simple but powerful concept
allows to decouple the device actuation force from the continuous
energy consumption of traditional solenoids, creating a magnetic
catch state, without the need of additional mechanisms or latches
(see Video S1, Supporting Information).
2.2. Characterization
The basic unit of our magnetic arrays consists of a cylindrical
AlNiCo magnet paired with a NIB magnet, both de ﬁned by their
diameter ( D) and length ( L). In this study, three commercial
AlNiCo sizes (D3L12, D5L22, and D6L25) were used; a wide vari-
ety of off-the-shelf NIB dimensions allowed us to create 30
unique magnet-pair con ﬁgurations. This study aimed to investi-
gate how variations in both magnet sizes in ﬂuence the perfor-
mance of the actuator pair and provide guidance on how these
parameters can be adjusted to optimize actuator performance in
terms of force, stress, strain, and ef ﬁciency.

2.2.1. Optimal Magnetization Parameter
The key to generating actuation in these devices lies in precisely
controlling the magnetization of their low-coercivity compo-
nents. This process is primarily governed by the strength and
direction of the applied magnetic ﬁeld, which, in turn, depends
on the solenoid’s electrical characteristics, design, and the power
supply’s ability to sustain the required energy input.
Figure 2 a shows the magnetization levels of three different
sizes of AlNiCo magnets under varying driving voltages using
long energy pulses. As voltage increases, the saturation curve ﬂat-
tens, indicating that further voltage increments have little effect
on remanent magnetization and, consequently, on the actuator ’s
attraction force. This threshold voltage, which increases with
magnet size, was considered the optimal magnetization voltage
for each sample.
Using this optimal voltage, the solenoids were subject to
magnetization cycles with varying pulse widths, as shown in
Figure 2b. Similarly, as pulse duration increases, the saturation
curve ﬂattens, meaning that beyond a certain point, longer pulses
do not signi ﬁcantly enhance remanent magnetization or attrac-
tion force. This threshold is termed the optimal pulse width and
is inﬂuenced by the inductive nature of the solenoid, which intro-
duces a delay in current rise time and, consequently, in the for-
mation of the surrounding magnetic ﬁeld.
By leveraging these two control parameters, precise and energy-
efﬁcient magnetization of the AlNiCo components was achieved,
enhancing the overall ef ﬁciency of EPMM actuator systems.
Figure 2c shows the optimal magnetization pulses for the three
AlNiCo magnet sizes used in this study. Each pulse was applied
using the previously determined optimal voltage and pulse width.
The energy required for magnetization, computed as the inte-
grated instantaneous power over the pulse time, increased with
magnet size, measuring 28 mJ, 172 mJ, and 256 mJ, respectively.

The attraction force pro ﬁles of AlNiCo magnets paired with
NIB magnets of varying lengths and diameters are shown in
Figure 3a. As anticipated, the attraction force increases exponen-
tially as the gap between the magnets narrows, it also scales with
the overall size of the magnet pair. These results provide a valu-
able reference for the design of magnetic arrays, offering insights
into the initial attraction at various gap distances as well as the
maximum holding force achieved at close proximity.
Figure 3b shows the distribution of maximum attraction

minimal gains in attraction force. This ﬁnding is signi ﬁcant,
as selecting an appropriate NIB length enables overall actuator
length optimization, which directly in ﬂuences strain perfor-
mance and weight.

2.3. Performance Benchmarking
To assess the relevance of the EPMM technology in the context of
wearable and portable systems, we compiled a comparative table
featuring three widely studied arti ﬁcial muscle technologies,
each based on distinct transduction principles (see Table 1 ).
These were evaluated across several performance metrics rele-
vant to such applications.
Most performance values were obtained from reference data
in the literature, chosen to represent typical behavior for each
technology. Among the metrics compared, Holding Power reﬂects
the energy required to maintain a contracted state under load,
without relying on external locking mechanisms. Two portability-
related indices were also introduced: the Speciﬁc Portability Index
(SPI) and the Volumetric Portability Index (VPI), which measure
the ratio of active to nonactive actuation mass and volume,
respectively. These indices serve as complementary indicators
to commonly reported values like Work Density and Speciﬁc
Work, which often consider only the actuator element itself
and neglect peripheral systems (e.g., pumps, electronics) that sig-
niﬁcantly impact the overall portability.
To provide context, the benchmark values for natural mamma-
lian muscle are included as a biological reference.
The EPMM actuators demonstrate competitive performance
across most categories. Among the nine parameters compared,
Speciﬁc Work is the only one where this system ranks lowest
(2.14–3.86 J Kg/C01), with values slightly below those of Peano-
HASEL actuators. [9] This is primarily due to the high density
of the magnetic materials used, which limits the work output
per unit mass. However, in terms of Work Density , our actuator
outperforms Peano-HASEL by delivering greater work output per
unit volume (11.47 –22.47 kJ m3), bene ﬁting from a compact
magnetic design that reduces overall actuator volume.
In terms of stress output, the EPMM reaches 432 kPa, exceed-
ing the reference value for natural muscle. Its achievable strain,
ranging from 10 to 25%, is higher than that of shape memory

2.4. Applications
2.4.1. Wearables: Low-Power Grasping Prosthetic Hand
To demonstrate the practical utility of the EPMM actuators, these
were ﬁrst integrated into an open-source prosthetic hand plat-
form.[55] The actuators extended along the forearm to replicate
the placement of human muscles responsible for hand grasping.
Each actuator array functioned as an arti ﬁcial muscle, contracting
to pull a tendon routed through the prosthetic hand toﬂex a ﬁnger.
Activation was controlled by a portable control unit ( Figure 4).
A 3D model of the modiﬁed prosthetic hand model (Figure 4a)
depicted four EPMM actuators, each connected to a tendon for
individual ﬁnger control.
The magnetic array for each arti ﬁcial muscle (Figure 4b) com-
prises NIB and AlNiCo solenoids enclosed in a nylon-braided
mesh that integrates the magnets into a single compliant hous-
ing, maintains their axial alignment during polarity switching,
and de ﬁnes their maximum separation distance (4 mm), which
sets the actuator ’s initial contraction force. This ﬂexible mesh
tensed during relaxation and bulged on contraction. The arti ﬁcial
muscle array had theoretical stroke of 16 mm, corresponding to
/C2520% strain.
The magnetic bistability of EPMM actuators provided a key
advantage for prosthetic applications (Figure 4c). Once contracted
by a magnetization pulse, the actuators remained contracted
without additional energy consumption, enabling inde ﬁnite
grasp. The actuators also remained backdrivable, enhancing safety
during human –device interaction (see Video S2, Supporting
Information).

tests (see Video S3, Supporting Information) on a single muscle
ﬁber, tendon unwrapping force measurements of the index ﬁn-
ger, and a four- ﬁnger power-grasp test to quantify maximum
grasping force, respectively in Figure 4d. Subsequently, the hand

3. Discussion
3.1. Performance, Evaluation, and Benchmarking
This study presents the working principle and comprehensive
characterization of EPMM actuators. By analyzing the magneti-
zation behavior of the soft-magnetic components, we identify
optimal driving voltages and pulse widths that enable energy-
efﬁcient actuation while minimizing losses. These results estab-
lish key operational thresholds for the reliable and effective use
of this technology.
A detailed analysis of magnetic a ttraction forces across varying
actuator dimensions, including length and diameter, provided
insights into the achievable force ranges and serves as a practical
reference for future researchers developing devices based on this
platform. This evaluation also identiﬁed the optimal design limits
to minimize actuator length while maximizing strain, thereby con-
tributing to performance improvements in future implementations.
The exploration of different geometric combinations high-
lighted actuator con ﬁgurations that optimized force, stress, and
transduction efﬁciency. These ﬁndings serve as design guidelines
to support the ongoing advancement of this actuation technology.
EPMM actuators were benchmarked against three widely used
artiﬁcial muscle technologies, with natural muscle included as a
biological reference. The comparison covered key performance
metrics including stress, strain, and transduction ef ﬁciency.
The results emphasized the importance of evaluating full-system
characteristics, considering parameters such as mass and vol-
ume, rather than focusing solely on actuator performance. In this
context, pneumatic actuators demonstrated limited portability
due to their bulky peripheral components.
One of the key advantages of the EPMM technology is its zero-
energy holding capability. Unlike HASEL actuators, which can
experience leakage currents under high voltage, or SMAs, which
require continuous heating to maintain position, EPMM actua-
tors consume energy only during activation. This makes EPMM
actuators well-suited for low-power tasks such as static grasping.
Overall, the comparison highlighted the power ef ﬁciency and
portability of EPMM actuators, demonstrating their strong poten-
tial for wearable and untethered applications.
3.2. Applications and Demonstrations
To further evaluate their suitability for wearable systems, the
actuators were integrated into a tendon-driven prosthetic hand
model. The actuators were positioned in an arrangement analo-
gous to that of forearm muscles and controlled ﬁnger ﬂexion
through tendon actuation. The system operated at low voltage,
with no risk of overheating, and demonstrated safe backdrivabil-
ity, an important feature for human-interactive devices. The pros-
thetic hand successfully held common daily-use objects without
any additional energy input following the initial grasp, con ﬁrm-
ing the potential of this technology for energy-ef ﬁcient prosthetic
applications. Although the system was not optimized for force
generation, initial unwrapping force measurements were prom-
ising, and further improvements in actuator and prosthesis inte-
gration could further enhance grasping strength.
The calculated cumulative energy consumption during the hold-
ing task comparison further underscored the energy advantage of
the actuator’s catch state. However, these results should be consid-
ered as indicative rather than absolute, as energy consumption can
vary with system architecture and may not scale linearly across dif-
ferent implementations.
In the underwater locomotion experiments, the actuators
demonstrated signiﬁcantly lower energy consumption compared
to conventional servomotors when used in a compliant ﬁn, under
equivalent operating conditions. Although their transmission
mechanisms differ, both achieve comparable ﬁn kinematics
and loading. In the EPMM-powered ﬁn, energy was required
only at motion reversal points, with the actuator self-adapting
to surrounding ﬂuid density regulating the stroke speed. This
characteristic suggests that this technology can enhance energy
efﬁciency in untethered aquatic robots, which is critical for
extending operational time.
In the amphibious robot, an alternative actuator con ﬁguration
enabled polarity-controlled crawling motion using a similar mech-
anism to that of the ﬁn. This demonstrated the versatility of the
actuation method, with distinct gaits generated simply by rear-
ranging magnet polarities within a consistent mechanical design.
The robot achieved both swimming and crawling by reconﬁguring
the actuator array, without requiring major changes in the struc-
tural layout. The compact system, based on monolithic compliant
mechanisms and plug-in magnetic elements, simpli ﬁes fabrica-
tion, reduces part count, and allows rapid assembly. Its low-voltage
requirements also support the use of standard, commercially
available electronics, enabling straightforward integration and
scalability.
Collectively, these ﬁndings establish EPMM actuators as com-
pact, low-voltage, and power-efﬁcient alternatives to conventional
artiﬁcial muscle technologies. They offer a well-balanced perfor-
mance pro ﬁle with notable advantages in energy ef ﬁciency and
portability, making them highly suitable for wearable and unteth-
ered robotic systems.
3.3. Design Considerations, Limitations, and Reliability
The actuation behavior of the EPMM system is inherently bistable,
driven by the magnetic ﬁeld gradient that develops between the
embedded magnets. Once triggered, if the actuation force is larger
than the load, the actuator transitions gradually from the unactu-
ated to the fully contracted state without requiring continuous
energy input along the stroke. As a result, intermediate positions
are not maintained unless external mechanical constraints are
introduced. The contraction dynamics, however, can be modulated
by environmental and resistive factors such as friction, load, or
ﬂuid interaction. For instance, in the ﬂapping-ﬁn demonstration,
the actuator exhibited slower contraction in water compared to
air due to viscous resistance, despite identical control inputs.
Maintaining intermediate states would require active magnetic sta-
bilization, which is better suited to high-frequency electromagnet-
based systems rather than the present low-power con ﬁguration.
Nevertheless, the integration of multistable mechanisms, such
as structural features that introduce intermediate energy wells
along the actuation path combined with controlled partial magne-
tization inputs, could enable future designs capable of achieving
stable partial contractions.
The stress and strain performance of the EPMM actuators
depends on the magnetic attraction force between their elements,

ric constraints, and material deformation modify this response,
resulting in more complex and reduced force–displacement behav-

applications.
Repeated testing of the EPMM actuators and integrated pro-
totypes demonstrated high reliability, with no major mechanical
or magnetic failures in the core units. However, peripheral struc-
tures such as the 3D-printed ﬂexure mechanisms in the ﬁn and
salamander robots occasionally failed after extended use due to
material fatigue and stress concentration at the bending region.
These issues could be reduced through geometric re ﬁnement
and improved fabrication methods. Minor wiring detachments
were related to handling rather than operational fatigue. In con-
trast, off-the-shelf motor drivers occasionally fail after prolonged
use, likely due to cumulative current spikes during polarity
switching, despite operating within their nominal transient lim-
its. Future work will focus on designing dedicated driver circuits
and performing systematic fatigue studies to ensure long-term
reliability for wearable and prosthetic applications.
3.4. Response Time and Operating Frequency
Although the present work primarily focused on the energy ef ﬁ-
ciency and stress and strain actuation characteristics of the EPMM
technology, representative estimates of response time and operat-
ing frequency were obtained from existing prototype recordings.
The analysis shows that the typical response time ranges from
/C2540 ms for on air unloaded actuators to about 110 ms for loaded
or coupled to mechanisms, and 780–1000 ms for underwater, cor-
responding to operational frequencies between 0.5 Hz and 12 Hz
depending on design, load, and environment. These values are
consistent with the intended application domain of the EPMM
actuator, which emphasizes low-power, low-frequency operation
where gradual motion or static holding is advantageous, such
as in the wearable and bio-inspired robotic systems presented
in this work. Higher operating frequencies could, in principle,
be achieved by reducing the mass of moving components or
employing higher driving currents, but such con ﬁgurations were
considered beyond the scope of the present low-power study.
4. Conclusion
This study introduced and characterized novel actuator technol-
ogy based on electro permanent magnetic interactions. Through
a detailed investigation of magnetization behavior, optimal oper-
ating parameters were identi ﬁed to ensure energy ef ﬁciency and
reliable actuation. Experimental analysis of magnetic force and
actuator geometry provided insight into performance trade-offs
and design guidelines for future implementations.
Benchmarking against established arti ﬁcial muscle technolo-
gies demonstrated that EPMM actuators deliver competitive
performance across key metrics such as stress, strain, and trans-
duction ef ﬁciency, while offering signi ﬁcant advantages in sys-
tem-level portability and energy consumption. Their ability to
sustain loads without continuous power sets them apart in appli-
cations where power ef ﬁciency is critical, such as wearable and
untethered robotic systems.
This potential was validated through the integration of EPMM
actuators into two functional robotic platforms. In a prosthetic
hand, the actuators enabled safe, low-voltage tendon-driven
grasping with zero-energy holding. In robotic locomotion, they
powered both swimming and crawling behaviors in an amphibi-
ous salamander-inspired robot through simple recon ﬁguration
of magnetic polarities. These demonstrations highlight the mod-
ularity, compactness, and adaptability of the actuation system.
Altogether, EPMM actuators represent a promising direction
for the development of next-generation soft and portable robotic
systems. Their unique combination of energy ef ﬁciency, design
simplicity, and functional versatility offers new opportunities for
scalable and accessible actuation solutions in wearable devices,
assistive technologies, and autonomous untethered robots.
5. Experimental Section
Materials and Fabrication : Single cylindrical rods of sintered AlNiCo-5
magnets were used as the soft-magnetic component in this study.
Nickel-Iron-Boron (NIB) N35 magnets were utilized in various coin-
shaped con ﬁgurations, ranging from 1 to 5 mm in thickness. These
NIB magnets were stacked to form larger magnets in the variable-length
characterization experiments.
All solenoids wound around the AlNiCo magnets consisted of a single-
layer coil covering the entire magnet length, using 0.25 mm insulated cop-
per wire. The driving circuit for the characterization experiments was
based on a microcontroller (ATmega328P) and an H-bridge MOSFET
driver, which were used to control the discharge of a 1000 μF electrolytic
capacitor connected in parallel to a voltage elevator set to the driving volt-
age of each magnet size and supplied from 12v 2A regulator.
Magnetization Characterization : A Tenmars TM-197 AC/DC magnetic
ﬁeld meter was used to measure variations in the sample ’s magnetic ﬁeld.
A custom-made, nonmagnetic ﬁxture was designed to hold both the mag-
netometer and the samples in a ﬁxed relative position. Measurements
were taken at a single point, approximately at the center of one of the
magnet’s poles.
To determine the saturation point of the AlNiCo samples, cyclic forward
and backward magnetization was applied using pulses of increasing dura-
tion until no further increase in the magnetic ﬁeld was observed. The
“reset pulse ” was de ﬁned as the minimum pulse length required to fully
magnetize the sample. This pulse was applied between each trial to return
the sample to the previous fully magnetized state. This knockdown mag-
netization technique prevented the sample from shifting into smaller B-H
curve regions, where lower residual magnetization would occur.
Magnetization Procedure: 1) The sample was secured on the ﬁeld meter
using a nonmagnetic ﬁxture. 2) The sample was magnetized using ﬁve
consecutive reset pulses to establish a reference ﬁeld value. 3) A magneti-
zation pulse of duration t was applied in the opposite direction, and the
resulting ﬁeld was recorded. 4) Steps 2 and 3 were repeated, increasing t
gradually until no signi ﬁcant increase in magnetization was observed.
The same procedure was performed using voltage as a variable instead
of pulse width ( t) to explore the optimal saturation voltage.
Force Measurement Setup : The force generated by the interaction
between magnetized AlNiCo and NIB magnets was measured using
an IMADA EMX motorized tensile testing machine equipped with

thesis and measure the unwrapping grasp force.
Amphibious Robot Fabrication : The compliant mechanism of the robot
was printed on PLA with a 0.4 mm nozzle. Two identical mechanisms were
attached to create the crossed ﬂexor beam design and glued together using
commercial cyanoacrylate glue. Magnets were plugged in pressﬁt, and elec-
tronics were encapsulated on a cast dragonskin30 (smooth-on) silicon skin.
The connection from inside the silicon skin to the magnets was securely
sealed using Skintite silicone (Smooth-on). The volume of the skin was cal-
culated to compensate for the weight of the full system and provide positive
buoyancy. The system operated using a 2S LiPo battery (300 mAh), a 9–30v
voltage regulator, two 35v 1000uf electrolytic capacitors, and 3 MOSFET
drivers rated for 36v and 9 A. An Arduino Nano was used to control the
system, assisted by a IR receiver and a remote control. The robot feet mech-
anism was designed to generate an anisotropic friction during crawling,
using a sliding PLA surface at the front tip of the foot and a small latex patch
at the base. The foot was able tilt forward over the PLA tip during steps
forward and fall back on the latex-covered base when dragged back.
