ABSTRACT
MemoGlove introduces a new interaction paradigm for capturing,
storing, and re-experiencing haptic memory in Extended Reality
(XR). Unlike conventional XR systems that prioritize static visual
and spatial data, MemoGlove centers on haptic traces—the dy-
namic, embodied ways users explore and engage with their sur-
roundings through touch. This framework enables not only the re-
construction of object geometry, but also the preservation of the
sequential tactile experience itself as a form of memory.
To support this interaction, we developed a working prototype:
a lightweight pneumatic glove embedded with pressure sensors,
paired with Quest 3 hand tracking and a Unity-based XR environ-
ment. When users touch an object, the system begins collecting
spatial data and logs the pressure and motion data as XR haptic
traces, which can later be physically replayed via pneumatic actua-
tors in the glove.
We demonstrate MemoGlove’s potential through scenarios such
as education, where a coach’s tactile interaction can be experienced
by a student in XR. Preliminary feasibility tests suggest that our
approach enables intuitive capture and playback of haptic XR ex-
periences, raising user awareness of subtle interaction patterns and
supporting recall and reflection on prior moments. We offer this
design as a starting point for rethinking how interaction, memory,
and touch converge in future XR systems.

1 I NTRODUCTION
Memory has long been a central research area in cognitive psychol-
ogy. While photos and videos have long used as passive memory
aids, the rise of XR technologies enables the capture of experiences
that are not only immersive but also interactive and reconfigurable.
XR technologies offer innovative interaction methods and redefine
memory and perception experiences [ 11]. It also provides a more
verisimilitude and realistic experiment environment for psycholo-
gists to studying memory phenomena [20]. Furthermore, VR could
also serve as a tool for history learning and improve memory [2].
Touch, or haptic interaction, plays a foundational role in both
sensory cognition and memory [ 14]. Beyond its cognitive func-
tions, touch is fundamental to emotional and social functioning, in-
fluencing bonding, stress regulation, and social compliance, thereby
enriching the lived experiences that form personal memories[ 22].
Despite its significance, there is limited research on haptics within
the XR memory field, with even less attention paid to the temporal
sequence of haptic interactions. Yet, these haptic interaction se-
quences are a crucial component of XR memory and memory in
general.
This paper introduces MemoGlove, a system designed to en-
hance XR memory by providing a complete loop for capturing, re-
constructing, and replaying haptic interactions. Unlike many XR
memory projects that focus on creating digital twins of objects
or environments, MemoGlove specifically addresses the nuanced,
temporal nature of haptic interaction as a sequence of movement,
rather than isolated actions or gestures. This means that haptic
traces provide an enhanced sense of presence, encompassing not
only the remembered scenario but also the complete process of in-
teraction within that context. For instance, a hug is not merely a

2 R ELATED WORKS
2.1 Haptic Interaction in XR
Haptic interaction is an essential and rapidly evolving modality in
XR, complementing the extensively developed visual and auditory
channels. Tangible interaction serves as a key medium connecting
the physical and digital worlds [10]. Research in XR and Haptic
Interaction has explored various simulation approaches. Illusion-
based or passive haptics, such as haptic retargeting [1] and non-
contact ultrasonic arrays for facial haptics [16], enhance realism
without requiring physical proxies. More embodied, proprioceptive
systems have been developed to support spatial congruence, includ-
ing devices that dynamically align with virtual and physical objects
[12][4]. Wearable, modular input systems [8][23] restore tangible
interaction affordances in XR. Moreover, social haptic, even subtle
cues, can significantly enhance interpersonal connection in collab-
orative XR (Gallace and Girondini 2022). While existing research
successfully explores real-time haptic illusions and passive aug-
mentation techniques, most systems lack the ability to record and
replay tactile interactions over time. Temporal continuity of touch,
such as haptic memory traces, is rarely considered, leaving a criti-
cal gap in designing long-term, embodied interactions. In addition,
little attention has been given to scenarios where haptic traces are
presented back to users, offering them a tangible and interpretable
representation of their own or others’ embodied actions.
2.2 Haptics in Perception & Memory
Haptics, memory, perception, and social connection are deeply in-
tertwined. Haptic interaction plays a foundational role in cognition
and embodied memory[7][21]. Studies show that active haptic ex-
ploration can improve memory retention of digital objects[19]. The
cognitive and neural bases of tactile memory have been reviewed,
emphasizing its role in perception and memory[7][21][21] . Touch
also plays a fundamental role in emotional and social collaboration
and interpersonal connection, thereby contributing to the richness
of lived experiences that form personal memories[22][6][24][25].
While substantial research has explored how haptics supports mem-
ory, cognition, and social bonding in physical or traditional comput-
ing contexts, there is a notable lack of integration with immersive
XR systems. Few studies investigate how tactile memory traces
or affective haptic communication could be embedded within XR
environments in ways that allow users to revisit, share, or reinter-
pret embodied experiences. This represents a critical opportunity to
bridge psychological findings with interactive system design.
2.3 Haptic Gloves & Trace
Recent advances include high-resolution, untethered haptic gloves
using electroosmotic pump arrays and smart gloves for adaptive
tactile interaction transfer [3][15][13]. Some studies investigate
how textile wearable interface or VR and action recording to en-
hance training procedures [18][13]. Furthermore, artistic explo-
rations, such as using trace point clouds in dance and art, under-
score the inherent value of capturing and representing dynamic hu-
man movement[5]. Despite these advancements, a common limi-
tation persists: most XR haptic systems and related artistic efforts
are primarily designed for output. Bidirectional systems, capable
of both sensing and replaying touch, remain rare. This fundamental
unidirectional characteristic limits the design of systems that sup-
port tactile memory, embodied experience sharing, or touch-based
storytelling.
MemoGlove addresses these gaps by introducing a
closed loop framework for recording and replaying hap-
tic memory in XR. Unlike existing systems that focus only on
realtime feedback or task performance, MemoGlove treats touch
as a communicative and memory medium. Its lightweight pneu-
matic glove captures pressure data through embedded sensors and
replays haptic traces via soft actuators, all synchronized with visual
overlays in Unity. This enables users to record, revisit, and even
share haptic interactions, expanding the narrative, educational, and
emotional potential of XR. MemoGlove thus repositions haptics not
just as momentary feedback, but it put forward a complete interac-
tion loop. It thus be able to transform embodied interaction into an
archiveable, reconstructible, and expressive medium for XR mem-
ory.
3 S YSTEM DESIGN
3.1 User Experience Design
The user experience design of MemoGlove is simple and intuitive.
As depicted in Figure 1, the user could wear the MemoGlove and
start any haptic interaction with the environment. The system au-
tomatically detects and records these tactile experiences, rendering
them as highlighted lines—referred to as haptic traces—within the
XR environment, visible through the headset. Even as the physical
environment changes over time, these haptic traces are preserved as
XR memories, quietly anchored in their original positions, awaiting
recall. When the user returns, they can see these traces, recall past
interactions, and, by touching them with the glove, receive haptic
feedback that simulates the remembered experience. Through this
integrated process, MemoGlove creates a closed-loop interaction
system for XR memory.

3.2 System Implementation
We implemented MemoGlove as a lightweight, robust, and eas-
ily replicable system. Its overall architecture comprises three main
components: a pneumatic haptic glove, a VR headset, and a soft-
ware platform, all connected via a custom data pipeline that enables
synchronized interaction and feedback.94

3.2.1 Pneumatic Haptic Glove
The glove provides haptic feedback using a series of pneumatic in-
flatable pads positioned across each fingertip and the palm (Fig-
ure 2). These pads are connected via tubing to FlowIO [17], a
custom hardware module that programmatically controls airflow
through five valves. A pressure sensor embedded within the tub-
ing continuously monitors internal pressure levels. Under idle con-
ditions, the air pads are semi-inflated. When the user touches a
surface while wearing the glove, the increase in internal pressure
triggers the start of the haptic recording process, which continues
as long as elevated pressure is maintained and stops automatically
when pressure returns to the baseline.
During playback, when the system detects the hand entering the
bounds of a virtual object or intersecting a previously recorded hap-
tic trace, FlowIO inflates the corresponding pads to deliver tangible
feedback, simulating the sensation of physical touch. This creates
a closed loop between recording and replay, effectively capturing
and representing haptic interaction memory.
Figure 3: Data Pipeline of MemoGlove
3.2.2 VR Headset and Software Platform
We employed the Meta Quest 3 headset as the XR component of the
system. Its integrated 3D scanning and camera-based hand tracking
provide real-time spatial data and accurate hand pose estimation.
The headset also renders the recorded haptic traces as highlighted
lines within the XR environment, enabling users to visualize and
revisit their tactile interactions.
The software platform was developed using Arduino and Unity.
The Arduino system (FlowIO is running on an Adafruit Feather
Sense microcontroller) manages input from the pressure sensors
and controls the haptic feedback by regulating the inflation and de-
flation of the valves. Arduino communicates with Unity via a serial
connection. In Unity, the haptic trace data—including time and
spatial coordinates—captured from the Quest 3 is stored in CSV
files. These files are then used to generate visual representations of
the haptic traces and to detect virtual collisions between the user’s
current hand position and previously recorded interactions.

4.1.1 Memory Recall Across Time
With its ability to record and replay past interactions, MemoGlove
enables profound XR memory reconnections across time. Inspired

Beyond time, MemoGlove fosters emotional and social connec-

timate, non-verbal, tactile expressions—often lost in digital com-

strengthen human bonds.

and motor imitation. In the scenario where a coach demonstrates
a complex hand movement—such as a dance gesture or a precise
tennis swing—while wearing MemoGlove, the system records the
expert’s nuanced haptic interactions. A student can then replay and
physically experience these expert traces through their own glove,

kinesthetic learning process accelerates skill acquisition and im-

5 P RELIMINARY FEASIBILITY TEST
To explore MemoGlove’s potential and gather qualitative insights,
we conducted a series of preliminary feasibility test, with a group
of 12 participants who are experts in this field. Each user expe-
rienced a MemoGlove interaction cycle from haptic recording to
replay. After each test we conducted semi-structured open-ended
interview to collect their initial impressions and thoughts.
Participants commented that the system’s core fea-
tures align well with its design goals. They were able to
record their haptic XR memories and later replay the experiences.
Most users appreciated the system’s innovative complete loop of
recording and reproduction. They found the process of capturing
haptic traces intuitive and felt that the haptic feedback added a
meaningful sensory layer to the experience. Several participants
noted that it made them more aware of subtle, often-overlooked in-
teraction patterns and effectively supported the recall and reflection
on past moments.
At the same time, several limitations emerged from
these early trials. Users raised concerns about hygiene when
sharing the glove, citing potential risks of bacterial transfer. Some
also noted that, over time, the visual accumulation of haptic traces
in the XR environment could interfere with or obstruct natural
interaction, particularly during prolonged use. Another common
suggestion was that integrating visual recordings alongside haptic
traces could improve immersion during memory replay and provide
a more complete recollection of past experiences.
Based on this feedback, we have considered several
directions for improvement. To address hygiene concerns,
we propose adding a disposable inner glove liner to minimize di-
rect skin contact in shared settings. To manage the accumulation
of traces, we suggest implementing a control interface that would
allow users to filter visible traces by time range or interaction type,
reducing visual clutter. As for the integration of visual recordings,
while the current project is intended as a proof-of-concept focused
on haptic trace capture and replay, we recognize the potential of
combining haptic and visual memories and view this as a promis-
ing avenue for future research and development.
6 L IMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Based on the feedback gathered from our preliminary feasibility test
and the challenges encountered during the development process,
we identify several limitations of the current system and propose
directions for future improvement and research.
Limited expressiveness of haptic traces constrains the
amount and type of information that can be embedded in the sys-
tem. While the recorded traces effectively capture the process of
haptic interaction, they do not convey the full visual or contextual
environment in which the interaction occurs. Future work could
enhance the system by integrating recordings or models of the sur-
rounding physical environment and objects involved in the interac-
tion, offering a more comprehensive and immersive representation
of embodied memory.
Inaccuracy in motion tracking presents another key limi-
tation. The current system relies on the built-in hand tracking ca-
pabilities of the Meta Quest 3, which may lack sufficient precision
when users wear the haptic glove. This impairs the system’s abil-
ity to support scenarios that require fine-grained tracking of finger
movements, such as musical instrument practices. Future iterations
could benefit from a self-implemented tracking solution or integra-
tion with higher-precision tracking systems to improve recording
fidelity.
Limited scope of user testing restricts the generalizabil-
ity of our findings. The current feasibility test includes only 12
participants. We plan to conduct a more extensive and formally
structured user study involving a larger and more diverse partici-
pant pool. This study will include both qualitative and quantita-
tive assessments, exploring users’ emotional responses, perceived
vividness of memory recall, and the effectiveness of the system as
a medium for haptic memory. Such an evaluation will contribute to
understanding MemoGlove’s impact on embodied cognition, emo-
tional engagement, and memory preservation across different appli-
cation contexts.
Broader application potential through technical ad-
vancement is a promising direction for MemoGlove. Improved
tracking accuracy will enable its application in areas such as mu-
sic education and keyboard typing analysis. Furthermore, future
development could focus on analyzing recorded traces to classify
gestures and movement patterns, enabling the system to better un-
derstand and respond to users’ actions. Additionally, expanding
MemoGlove to include additional sensory modalities such as syn-
chronized audio and visual recordings, multi-user interaction, and
shared haptic memory environments would support more personal-
ized, adaptive, and collaborative experiences. These advancements
could significantly extend the system’s role within the XR memory
ecosystems.
7 D ISCUSSION
The various use scenarios and user tests of MemoGlove demon-
strate its initial success as a medium for XR memory. Across dif-
ferent applications, the consistent format of the haptic trace high-
lights its universal value for representing haptic memories, while
the multi-sensory interaction showcases its practical benefits in
real-world use. In the following, we discuss MemoGlove from two
key perspectives.
7.1 Embedding Multi-Sensory Interaction
Memory is inherently a multi-sensory experience. MemoGlove
not only introduces the concept of haptic trace but also provides a
closed-loop pipeline for recording, visualizing, and replaying hap-
tic interactions. This multi-sensory system allows users not only to
see prior haptic memories but also to re-experience them through
touch, fostering a sense of resonance with their past embodied self.
By engaging multiple senses, MemoGlove helps reconstruct past
interactions with vivid detail, offering a powerful means to evoke
and relive XR experiences.
7.2 Haptic Trace as a Representation of XR Memory
As discussed earlier, current approaches to representing memory in
XR often overlook the sequential nature of haptic interaction, which
is fundamental to the haptic experience. The concept of the haptic
trace addresses this gap by highlighting the temporal and spatial se-
quence of touch. Memory is not merely a static environment or a
digital twin model, but it involves heavily with the way user inter-
act with the environment and objects. It is a matter of behavioral
sequence about time. By capturing the spatial trajectory of inter-
action over time, it creates a time-space composite that provides a
richer and more comprehensive representation of embodied experi-
ence. Importantly, this time-space composite reflects not just a fea-
ture of MemoGlove, but a fundamental characteristic of memory
itself: space, objects, and the actions performed on those objects
over time form the core parameters of how we remember. We pro-
pose that the haptic trace holds potential as a generalizable model
for representing XR memories across applications and could inspire
further research in this area.

8 C ONCLUSION
MemoGlove solves a key limitation in current XR systems by en-
abling intuitive and comprehensive reproduction of past haptic in-
teractions. By combining visual traces anchored in space with syn-
chronized pneumatic feedback, users can both see where touch hap-
pened and physically feel it again. This closed-loop design turns
touch into something recordable, revisitable, and shareable, sup-
porting clearer memory recall, emotional connection, and embod-
ied understanding.
Unlike conventional haptic systems that focus mainly on perfor-
mance or realism, MemoGlove explores how touch can serve as
a medium for communication, learning, and memory. Our proto-
type demonstrates this approach across scenarios such as memory
replay, education, and long-distance connection, highlighting the
broad potential of haptic memory in XR. We position the haptic
trace not as an isolated technical demonstration, but as a potential
standard for recording, preserving, and sharing haptic memory on a
broader scale.
Looking ahead, future work will explore alternative actuation
methods for richer tactile precision, analyze recorded traces to
classify gestures and movement patterns, and integrate multi-user,
multi-modality and AI-driven features to create more adaptive and
shared haptic experiences.
