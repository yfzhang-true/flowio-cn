A Silent, Low-Heat Pinch Valve Driven by a
Single Rolled Dielectric Elastomer Actuator
Qi Shao , Member, IEEE, Xin-Jun Liu , Member, IEEE, Fumiya Iida , Senior Member, IEEE,
and Huichan Zhao , Member, IEEE
Abstract—Fluidic control for untethered soft robots is
currently limited by the noise and bulk of solenoid valves.
While dielectric elastomer actuators (DEAs) offer a promis-
ing soft alternative, existing designs often rely on high-
frequency resonance, which induces mechanical vibration,
or complex multiactuator stacking to generate sufﬁcient
force. Here, we present a miniature (5.0 g) pinch valve
driven by a single rolled DEA operating in a nonreso-
nant mode, simplifying the structural and driving system
requirements. Our direct-drive design achieves measured
sound levels close to the background noise level ( ∼ 49 dB)
and low heat generation ( 5
◦C rise) while maintaining a rapid
response time ( ∼ 20 ms). We further integrate the valve
into an untethered pneumatic multilegged robot, demon-
strating reliable ﬂuidic control at a locomotion speed of
77.5 cm/min.
Index Terms—Dielectric elastomer actuators (DEAs), ﬂu-
idic control, pinch valves, pneumatic systems, untethered
robots.

and expansion, FEAs enable versatile biomimetic motion. How-

The control of FEAs is typically approached in one of two
ways. The ﬁrst involves generating ﬂuid pressure in-situ through
chemical reactions or combustion, such as the decomposition of
hydrogen peroxide or controlled microexplosions [5], [6].T h e

soft robots [9].
A valve is an actuation system that gates and directs ﬂuid ﬂow
from a power source to an actuator, a function that typically re-
quires an internal actuator. In soft robotics, pinch-type valves and
variants based on tube bending or twisting are also widely used
for their structural simplicity and ﬂuid isolation capability [10],
[11], [12], [13]. While some soft logic valves are powered by
the working ﬂuid itself [10], [12], the more common approach
uses electronic actuators to bridge the control system with the
ﬂuidic circuit. Among electronic types, solenoid valves are the
most widely adopted for their reliability and speed. However,
their high density, heat generation, and noise create signiﬁcant
limitations for miniaturized and untethered soft robots. Other
smart materials face different tradeoffs; magnetorheological
elastomer valves involve large coils and generate considerable
heat [14], while shape memory alloys respond too slowly [15].
Piezoelectric valves offer low power and fast response but are
limited by small displacement, high cost, and brittleness, re-
stricting their use to precision applications rather than robust
robotic systems [16].
Dielectric elastomer actuators (DEAs), another class of elec-
tric ﬁeld-responsive actuators, are a candidate actuator for valve
control [17]. They offer the high speed and low holding power
of piezoelectric actuators but with the key advantages of larger
strain, greater robustness, and lower cost [18], [19]. These prop-
erties make DEAs suitable for valve actuation, and several stud-
ies have explored this potential. One prominent approach utilizes
dynamic actuation, where DEAs are driven at high frequencies

II. D
ESIGN AND FABRICA TION OF THE DEA PINCH VAL VE
A. Valve Design and Principle
As a linear actuator, a DEA could be used to directly replace
the electromagnetic core in a conventional spool valve. However,
this approach retains the complex mechanical components that
are contrary to the design philosophy of soft robotics, which
favors structural simplicity and compliance. To maximize com-
patibility with ﬂexible ﬂuidic systems and simplify integration,
we adopted an inherently softer architecture: the pinch valve.
A pinch valve regulates ﬂuid ﬂow by physically compressing
a ﬂexible tube, a mechanism that is exceptionally simple and
well-suited for integration into soft robotic systems. Our imple-
mentation, as shown in Fig. 1(a), uses a single rolled DEA to
actuate the pinch mechanism on a commercial ﬂexible silicone
tube that serves as the ﬂuid conduit. One end of the DEA
is anchored to the valve frame, while the other end acts as
a compression head. The compression head moves along the
DEA axis toward the base platform to compress the silicone
tube, while a screw mechanism beneath the base platform allows
adjustment of the DEA preload and the initial tube clearance.
The current prototype uses a lightweight rigid frame to provide
support and preload adjustment for the DEA and the tube. The
entire valve assembly is 40 mm tall with a 15 × 15 mm cross
section and a mass of 5.0 g (excluding tubing), of which the
DEA itself accounts for 3.6 g.
The working principle is illustrated in Fig. 1(b). In the un-
driven state, the silicone tube remains open or only slightly
precompressed; upon voltage application, the DEA elongates
axially and drives the compression head to compress the tube. As
the DEA output force equilibrates with the tube compression re-
action force, a speciﬁc oriﬁce area is formed, enabling both two-
position and proportional ﬂow regulation. This design avoids
complex valve body fabrication and achieves complete isolation
between the actuator and the ﬂuid medium, ensuring ﬂuid purity.
These features make the valve suitable for compact soft robots
where acoustic noise and local heating are constrained.
B. DEA and Valve Fabrication
The valve performance depends strongly on the DEA dimen-
sions. For a rolled cylindrical DEA, the active height primarily
determines the achievable displacement, while the diameter de-
termines the blocking force; both must be sized to satisfy the tube
compression requirements. As shown in Fig. 2(a), we fabricated
a cylindrical DEA with a 12-mm diameter and an active height
of 20 mm, using a combined stacking and rolling process for
manufacturability. The process began with the creation of a mul-
tilayer ﬁlm. Dielectric layers were produced via blade-coating,
while compliant electrodes were made by vacuum-ﬁltering a
carbon nanotube (CNT) aqueous dispersion (TNWDS, Chengdu
Organic Chemicals) and stamping [23]. This resulted in a ﬁlm
stack comprising eight dielectric layers, each approximately
50 μm thick, with seven interdigitated electrode layers. The
two outermost dielectric layers were designated as nondriving
layers serving as passive insulation. The dielectric elastomer
was composed of a 3:1 mixed silicone formulation of Silbione
LSR 4305 (1:1 mix ratio, Elkem) and SYLGARD 184 (20:1 mix
ratio, Dow).
As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), the ﬁnal cylindrical actuator was
formed by rolling two multilayer DEA strips obtained by cutting
the multilayer ﬁlm into 180 mm× 30 mm sections. The resulting
30 mm-tall actuator consists of a 20-mm central active region,
ﬂanked by 5 mm passive sections at the top and bottom that

A. Airﬂow Control Characteristics
The pneumatic performance was evaluated using the testbed
in Fig. 5(a), consisting of an air compressor, a proportional pres-

mass ﬂow sensors (Honeywell AWM series).
First, we measured the valve’s switching characteristics. The

to the compliance of the DEA, which limits the achievable tube
compression under high upstream pressure.

C. Low-Heat and Low-Noise Characteristics
Compared with conventional solenoid valves, the DEA valve
exhibits different characteristics in terms of noise, power con-
sumption, and heat generation.
As shown in Fig. 7(a) and demonstrated in Video Part I of
the Supplementary Material, the measured system sound level
of the DEA valve during operation is close to the background
noise level of 49 dB, with no mechanical noise observed to be
signiﬁcantly above the background in the experimental environ-
ment. In contrast, a commercial solenoid valve (X205LF, Parker
Hanniﬁn) operating at 10 Hz generates a distinct noise, raising
the measured level to 69 dB. The DEA valve’s acoustic perfor-
mance approaching background noise levels can be attributed
to its compliant actuation mechanism and the absence of rigid,
impact-based moving components.
The power consumption was measured for both valves under
1 Hz square-wave actuation. For the DEA valve, the driving
current and voltage were fed back by a high-voltage ampliﬁer
(Trek 615-3, Advanced Energy) and recorded using an oscil-
loscope (EDUX1002 G, Keysight). For the solenoid valve, the
voltage and current were measured using a digital multimeter

V. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented the design, fabrication, and char-
acterization of a miniature (5.0 g) pinch valve driven by a
single rolled DEA. Our results show that this device effectively
addresses the noise and heat limitations of conventional solenoid
valves, achieving measured sound levels close to the background
noise level ( ∼49 dB), low temperature rise ( < 5◦C), and ap-
proximately 90% reduction in per-cycle energy consumption at
1 Hz. Distinct from dynamic DEA valves that leverage high-
frequency resonance to achieve high blocking pressures for hy-
draulic applications [20], our quasi-static, direct-drive approach
prioritizes acoustic stealth and system simplicity. Although a
rigid frame is still retained for preload adjustment, the soft direct-
drive architecture trades peak blocking pressure for quieter,
cooler, and structurally simpler operation; the resulting force is
nevertheless sufﬁcient for typical low-pressure pneumatic soft
robotic tasks, which is important for fully untethered operation.
The results indicate that a single rolled DEA can provide
sufﬁcient stroke and force for low-pressure pneumatic valve
control while reducing acoustic noise and heat generation. The
current design remains limited by leakage above 40 kPa, but it
provides a compact valve option for untethered pneumatic soft
robots. The proportional ﬂow curves reported here are based
on steady-state measurements; the underlying viscoelasticity
also produces the observed asymmetry between active closing
and passive opening response times. Model-based feedforward,
incorporating hysteresis compensation, could address both ef-
fects and improve high-bandwidth control accuracy. On the
hardware side, although the silicone tube shares similar material
properties with the DEA dielectric, wear and surface damage
under long-term cyclic clamping may limit durability and war-
rants systematic lifetime testing. Mechanical ampliﬁcation of
the pinching force, together with cotailoring of the DEA and
tube parameters for different pressure and ﬂow regimes, would
further extend the valve’s operating range.
This article validates a quasi-static pinch valve driven by a
single rolled DEA as a lightweight ﬂuidic control element with
low acoustic noise and low heat generation. These results suggest
that single-DEA pinch valves are suitable for compact, low-
pressure pneumatic soft robots where noise and local heating
are constrained.
