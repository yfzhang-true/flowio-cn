1 Introduction
As Virtual Reality (VR) becomes more popular, research into “hap-
tics” or tactile feedback is gaining attention as a vital part of VR
experiences. With technology such as piezoelectric components,
linear resonant actuators (LRA), and eccentric rotating mass (ERM)
motors, vibration feedback has become commonplace in gaming
consoles and mobile phones. However, these technologies are typi-
cally limited to delivering tactile input to specific body parts, such
as the hands. Moreover, the complex wiring in the design circuit
poses challenges for prototyping, especially when using multiple
vibrators in research and development.
Educational institutions have recently started integrating hap-
tic technology into STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and
Mathematics) education. Open-source devices, such as the low-cost
Hapkit [18], provide force feedback in classroom settings, enabling
students to explore fundamental concepts of haptics. However,
there is a notable gap in educational tools designed explicitly for
vibrotactile feedback, which remains underrepresented in this field.
Existing toolkits, such as the TECHTILE Toolkit [17], provide frame-
works for implementing haptic feedback in artistic, educational, and
research contexts. However, extending their functionality—such
as adding new operational modes or configuring collaborative in-
teractions between devices—often requires significant technical
expertise and extensive customization, making them less accessi-
ble to beginners and non-experts. Adding new operational modes
or configuring collaborative interactions between devices—often
requires significant technical expertise and extensive customiza-
tion, making them less accessible to beginners and non-experts.
Moreover, modular haptic devices like ChainFORM [19] and Sensor-
Tape [3] offer flexibility by allowing users to reconfigure their size
and shape to suit various applications. Nevertheless, these devices
often demand intricate component assembly, posing challenges
for users with limited experience designing and operating haptic
systems.
To address the limitations of existing haptic feedback technolo-
gies, we developed “Tape-Tics”, a tape-like device inspired by the
concept of LED strip light, featuring a series of small vibrators that
can be cut to size. Each node of Tape-Tics consists of an LED control
IC called NeoPixel, a small disc vibrator, and a full-color LED, all
mounted on a flexible printed circuit (FPC) board. This allows users
to cut the device to any desired length, attach it to any location, and
receive visuo-tactile feedback. The inclusion of LEDs helps users
identify which node is vibrating, facilitating mutual understanding
of haptic sensations between the wearer and the observer [7]. The
vibration intensity can be adjusted in 256 levels, providing users
with precise control over the tactile feedback Additionally, each
node’s vibrator can be independently controlled. The NeoPixel li-
brary, used for controlling the LEDs, is publicly available and was
adapted to create the control program. As a result, Tape-Tics is
compatible with NeoPixel, which has extensive online resources,
greatly enhancing its scalability. To ensure that even beginners
without programming skills can use Tape-Tics, we have provided
a GUI controller that runs on a PC. Using these tools reduces the
difficulty of device development, eliminating the need to assemble
components from scratch or advanced programming skills.
We hosted a hands-on workshop for high school students, who
frequently engage in creative projects, to test the device’s effective-
ness. After a brief lecture on haptic technology and explaining how
to use Tape-Tics, participants were encouraged to create projects
using the device. As a result, all participants could easily use the
device, enthusiastically modifying and attaching it to their bodies
or other objects. The unique projects they created, such as a vibra-
tion alarm wristband and a hat that notifies the wearer through
vibrations, demonstrated their growing interest in vibrotactile tech-
nology. Survey results indicated that Tape-Tics was perceived as an
effective tool for incorporating vibrotactile feedback into education.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Proposing a novel modular design for a haptic device that
resembles a tape-like form, enabling users to attach it to
various body parts and customize its length by cutting it
to size. This design lowers the development difficulty and
allows for flexible applications without requiring specialized
technical knowledge.
• Introducing a user-friendly GUI controller that runs on a PC,
facilitating the intuitive operation of the device, especially
for beginners in haptic technology.
• Evaluating the device’s affordability and power consumption
to ensure it meets the requirements for integration into edu-
cational institutions, including determining the maximum
effective length of the tape.
• Demonstrating the versatility and effectiveness of the device
through workshops, where participants attach the device to
different body parts to explore its potential applications, and
analyze the projects they create.
2 Related Work
2.1 Vibrotactile devices
Modern smartphones and gaming devices are increasingly equipped
with small vibrators to generate haptic feedback, enhancing the user
experience. These are commonly referred to as “handheld” haptic
devices. In recent years, notable examples include the PlayStation
DualShock and the Nintendo Switch Joy-Con in the video game
industry. In the smartphone sector, there are the iPhone’s Tap-
tic Engine and Sony Xperia’s Dynamic Vibration System, among
others.

In contrast, there are “wearable” haptic devices, which are di-
rectly worn on the body, such as “glove-type” devices worn on
the hands or “vest-type” devices worn on the torso. These types
of devices have been the focus of active research in recent years.
For instance, Tasbi [ 25] is a wristband-type haptic device worn
around the wrist, where small vibration motors are evenly spaced
to replicate a sense of tension, thereby improving interactions with
virtual buttons. Additionally, Tanabe et al. [32] developed a glove
with low-latency linear vibrators placed across the palm. This glove
provides tactile feedback to convey stimuli and material sensations
that correspond to the speed of motion when interacting with vir-
tual objects. Miniaturization has progressed to the point where
devices can fit on the fingertips, making them easy to wear and
lightweight, which are advantages particularly suited for VR ap-
plications with hand tracking [15, 26, 34]. Additionally, research
has been conducted on suit-type haptic devices that provide tactile
stimulation to the entire body. The Synesthesia Suit [5] is a wireless
wearable suit offering full-body haptic feedback with customizable
haptic module placement. Another method avoids delays in haptic
feedback by using light from a projector as input to the haptic
display device in the suit [33].
However, despite these advancements, existing wearable haptic
devices are typically designed to provide feedback to specific areas,
such as the hands or chest. Delivering feedback to multiple body
parts simultaneously requires developing and wearing devices tai-
lored to each specific area, which introduces several challenges.
These include increased complexity in device design, difficulty in
achieving seamless integration across different body parts, and sig-
nificant discomfort due to the bulkiness of multiple devices. As a
result, such systems are often impractical for everyday use, costly
to produce, and difficult to scale for widespread adoption.
2.2 Haptics development support toolkit
Various toolkits have been provided to support the development
of haptic devices in research, education, and artistic design. These
toolkits include source codes, circuit board data, and GUI-based
development software, and are designed to be accessible even to
non-experts. Individuals can use them without knowledge of cir-
cuit design or coding. A representative toolkit is the TECHTILE
Toolkit [17], which is a haptic device capable of recording and
replaying tactile information, and has attracted the interest of “non-
expert” users in haptic devices.
Various haptic editors have been released for both research and
commercial purposes. For example, there is “Meta Haptics Studio”
which is compatible with the Meta Quest series controllers1. This
desktop application allows users to design and test haptic feedback
intuitively. Additionally, there is TactJam [37], a tool that allows
designers to prototype vibrotactile feedback on the body, create,
record, and share vibrotactile patterns online, and collaboratively
design these patterns. The Stereohaptics Toolkit [ 9] features an
audio-based hardware configuration that allows the creation of var-
ious haptic interfaces and applications using commercially available
audio hardware components and custom-developed software tools.
They also provide graphical user interfaces that allow beginners to
create complex vibrotactile patterns [1, 10, 22, 24, 31].
1https://www.meta.com/experiences/meta-haptics-studio/6759764157450104/
However, while these tools support intricate vibrotactile patterns
and hardware configurations, they may require prior knowledge of
haptic systems. Inspired by these advancements, the GUI controller
for "Tape-Tics" is designed with simplicity as its core principle.
This GUI minimizes operational complexity, allowing non-experts
to operate the device through an intuitive, click-based interface.
This approach bridges the gap between advanced haptic prototyp-
ing tools and the needs of beginners and educators, ensuring that
"Tape-Tics" remains an accessible and flexible system for various
applications.
2.3 Educational haptic tools
The advent of platforms such as Arduino, M5Stack, and Microbit has
significantly facilitated electronic DIY projects. Modular electronic
platforms like Grove2 have also made working with sensors and
other components easier. Additionally, visual-based programming
development environments, such as Scratch, have been increas-
ingly established for programming. These tools, both hardware
and software, have been utilized in STEM education and adopted
in numerous schools for research and hobbies and as educational
tools3.
However, when it comes to incorporating haptics into education,
the devices are often expensive, and building them from scratch
can be challenging. Researchers worldwide have developed haptic
toolkits specifically designed for educational purposes to address
this. The aforementioned TECHTILE Toolkit [17] has been used
to conduct numerous workshops, promoting the significance of
haptics through vibrotactile experiences [16]. Regarding kinesthetic
haptic devices, Martinez and colleagues at Stanford University de-
veloped a low-cost, one-degree-of-freedom haptic kit called Hapkit,
which students can assemble and is actually used in classes [ 18].
The design plans are available online as open-source, utilizing 3D-
printed plastic structural materials that allow easy construction
and customization with readily available tools. For spatial haptic in-
terfaces, there is a module-based kit called WoodenHaptics [4] that
even people with little electro-mechanical experience or beginners
can easily manufacture and assemble.
Moreover, when introducing such devices into educational insti-
tutions, providing one for each student is essential, resulting in a
need for mass production. This makes cost a critical factor in the
design and implementation of the device.
2.4 Reconfigurable modular haptic device
Modular haptic devices have been developed to provide a flexible
user interface that allows users to freely combine and configure the
shape, size, and type of tactile stimulation. There are also detachable
haptic modules that enable users to easily swap out different types
of vibrators and attach the device to various parts of the body and
any object [6, 8, 23, 27–29, 38, 39].
Additionally, LineFORM [20] is a shape-changing interface equipped
with small servo motors, and its improved version, ChainFORM [19]
, incorporates LED light sources and touch sensors, allowing for the
development of various applications, including prototyping tools.
SensorTape [3] is a tape-like sensor module inspired by LED strip

While these modular devices offer flexibility, they typically re-

such as soldering or cutting tools. In contrast, one of the objectives
of this research is to develop a haptic device that eliminates the need
for soldering or specialized tools. The device itself is pre-assembled
and ready for use, connecting to a PC for control. This simplicity
ensures that Tape-Tics is easy to operate and accessible to a wide
range of users, from beginners to educators and researchers.
2.5 Flexible devices
“Rigid devices”, which use traditional circuit boards, as discussed in
the previous section, typically require assembly and are not easy
to start with. In contrast, “flexible devices” come with components
pre-mounted on a flexible substrate. SensorTape and SoftVibe fall
into this category; they allow the user to create the desired device
simply by cutting to the required length or connecting the devices
with a cable.
Furthermore, research supporting prototyping directly on the
body has been reported. SkinKit [ 14] and Wearable Bits [ 11] is
a kit that enables rapid prototyping of on-body interfaces. It in-
cludes modules composed of reusable Flexible Printed Circuit Board
(FPCB) blocks, which can be easily attached and detached. iSkin [36]
proposes a new type of skin-worn sensor that allows touch input
on the body. It can be manufactured in various shapes and sizes to
suit different body parts, such as the finger, forearm, or ear.
The repurposing of flexible displays has also been explored in
research. PrintScreen [21] proposed a technology for digitally fab-
ricating customized, flexible displays using thin-film electrolumi-
nescence (TFEL). This technology enables the production of highly
customized displays in small quantities, quickly and at low cost.
CoiLED Display [30]is a technology that can transform ordinary
objects into displays simply by wrapping the device around them,
exploring a new method for displaying information on objects.
As seen in these studies, flexibility allows for attaching devices
to the body or other objects. Tape-Tics follows this philosophy by
using FPC technology, achieving simple usability, and making it
easy for users to apply the device to various scenarios, whether on
the body or other surfaces.

3.1.2 Tape Node. The configuration of the Tape Node is shown in
Figure 3 (B). Considering the need to attach the device to curved
surfaces, it was developed using flexible printed circuit (FPC) tech-
nology. A 2-layer polyimide-based substrate was used, which incor-
porates a thickness of 0.11 mm. Nodes are arranged with a 40 mm
spacing, and the tape width is 25 mm to ensure compatibility with
the FFC connector on the Master Node. Power is supplied from a
5V source, with two voltage regulators providing 5V to the WS2811
IC and LEDs, and 3.3V to the vibrator. Additionally, a signal is sent
from the WS2811 to the vibrator, and a photocoupler is used to
isolate the WS2811 circuit from the vibrator. To allow for flexibility,
larger components were placed as centrally as possible. There are
three solder pads on both sides of the tape: power, data, and ground
lines, allowing for easy connections. The circuit diagram of the
Tape Node is shown in Appendix 2 of the Additional Materials.
3.1.3 Control IC. The flow of signals within Tape-Tics is shown in
Figure 4. Each node has an RGB full-color LED driver IC WS2811
(World Semi)5, compatible with NeoPixel. This IC features an anode-
common type PWM dimming synced driver, allowing color values
with 256 levels for each RGB component to be controlled by a
5http://www.world-semi.com/ws2811-family/175.html
microcontroller. Additionally, multiple ICs can be cascaded using a
single serial communication line.
3.1.4 Vibrators. The vibrator used is the LCM1027A2445F (LEADER)
eccentric disc vibration motor6. The selection criteria were its good
availability, small size (diameter of10 mm), low power consumption
(drive current: 80 mA), and affordability (US$0.33 per unit).
3.1.5 LED. Each node is equipped with the full-color LED WS2812B-
V (World Semi) compatible with the NeoPixel library7. The bright-
ness of the LED changes in 256 steps proportional to the intensity
of the vibration. This allows Tape-Tics to provide visual feedback
to both the user wearing it and third parties. During device devel-
opment and debugging, it is also helpful for visually confirming
which node is vibrating and to what extent.
3.2 Software
3.2.1 Communication. The WS2811 chips communicate with each
other through serial data transmission. When sending data from a
PC, the first LED chip receives its own RGB value data, shapes the
remaining data, and then sends the color data to the next WS2811
chip. This eliminates the need for complex wiring and the individual
slave and address settings required in I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit)
communication. The communication speed between WS2811 chips
is 800 Kbps, which is faster than the 100 Kbps to 400 Kbps speeds
typically seen in I2C communication8.
3.2.2 LED Control Library. NeoPixel is a brand by Adafruit for
their WS2812 LEDs and compatible products9. NeoPixel (WS2812)
color LEDs have an integrated IC that controls the LED’s color and
brightness using a single data line. This setup allows for chaining
multiple color LEDs together while still being controlled by a single
signal line.
High-speed and precise timing is required to generate the control
signal for these LEDs. Programming for this can be complex, re-
quiring knowledge of MCU assembly language. However, Adafruit
offers an open-source NeoPixel library that simplifies the control
of NeoPixel (WS2812) LEDs. The NeoPixel series operates with
just three connections: power, signal line, and ground (GND). The
NeoPixel library works with Arduino, ESP32, M5Stack, and Rasp-
berry Pi platforms.
In this research, we opted for FastLED, which is faster and more
memory-efficient than NeoPixel and suitable for use within the
limited memory of M5Stack.

4.3 Vibration Specification
The acceleration data were obtained using the IMU MPU6886 in-
tegrated into the M5Stack Atom Matrix, with a vibration motor
attached to the M5Stack. The PWM signal for the WS2811 was var-
ied from 0 to 255 in increments of 10, and vibrations were recorded
at each step. The vibration intensity for each axis (X, Y, Z) was
calculated as the combined acceleration in terms of RMS values.
During testing, the vibration unit operated for 5 seconds at each
intensity level, and the average acceleration was recorded after 5
repetitions. The results were plotted as a graph in m/s2, as shown
in Figure 7. It was observed that the vibration motor began to acti-
vate around PWM level 40, and the vibration intensity increased
monotonically as the PWM level was raised.

5 User Evaluation
To evaluate the usability of Tape-Tics for individuals without spe-
cialized knowledge, we conducted a hands-on workshop. The aim
was to observe whether participants understood how to use Tape-
Tics and to explore the creative works they could produce. Ethical
approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review
Board of our organization.
5.1 Method
5.1.1 Apparatus. Each team was provided with three sets of Tape-
Tics, each containing 11 nodes, along with a Windows PC equipped
with a built-in GUI controller. To facilitate creativity and broaden
the scope of craft projects, we also supplied scissors, soldering
irons, cables, double-sided tape, Velcro, glue, rubber bands, and
other materials.
5.1.2 Participants. Ten participants took part in the study: two
students from the Major of Mechanics and eight students from the
Major of Electronics, all males, ages 15-18 (M = 16.3, SD = 0.9).
They were high school students from the Computer Research Club
at Miyakojima Technical High School in Osaka, Japan11. Table 3
presents the backgrounds and demographics of the participants.
According to the pre-workshop interviews, participants P1 and P4
had a strong interest in crafts and regularly engaged in making
things. The remaining eight participants were beginners in pro-
gramming, with experience limited to club activities and regular
classes. Participants were pre-arranged into pairs to work together
during the workshop.
5.1.3 Workshop Structure. The workshop was held in the computer
lab at the high school. It lasted two days, each approximately six
hours long, including pre-explanations and project development.
The workshop proceeded as follows:
(1) Introduction and consent (30 min): The session began with
a clear explanation of the experiment. Participants were provided
with the “Consent Form, ” “Withdrawal Form, ” and “Data Disclosure
Consent Form, ” all of which were signed.
(2) Introduction and lecture (40 min): The session started with
participant self-introductions, followed by a lecture on haptic tech-
nology. Since participants had limited prior exposure to haptic
technology, the lecture provided foundational information. Topics
covered included the history, technical aspects, and recent develop-
ments in haptics, as well as related devices. The content was based
on the book written by the creators of the TECHTILE Toolkit [16].
(3) Tape-Tics overview and hands-on (2 hours): An overview
of Tape-Tics, including its specifications and usage, was provided.
Tape-Tics devices were distributed to each team, and participants
practiced using their functions. They learned how to control, cut,
and reassemble Tape-Tics using the provided GUI controller. Several
exercises were assigned to help participants familiarize themselves
with the GUI controller.
(4) Idea generation (2 hours): Brainstorming session was held
to generate ideas. Participants wrote their ideas on sticky notes
and arranged them on a large sheet of paper to facilitate analysis
during the experiment.
(5) Project development (5 hours): Teams worked collabora-
tively on developing their projects using Tape-Tics. Support was
available for any questions or challenges encountered. Time was
also allocated to prepare their presentation materials.
(6) Presentations and reflection (1 hour): Each team presented
their project for 10 minutes, which included the “project name, ”
“application, ” “reasons for creation, ” “challenges and difficulties, ”
and “desired additional features. ” A demonstration accompanied
each presentation. After the presentations, teams exchanged their
projects and participated in hands-on experiences with others’
work.

7 Disscusion and Future Work
7.1 Device limitations
While participants showed interest in expanding Tape-Tics’ func-
tionality, doing so without additional technical knowledge was
challenging. To address this, future models will include plug-and-
play sensors compatible with the Grove system, which is supported
by M5Stack. This will greatly reduce the difficulty of sensor inte-
gration, allowing even novice users to easily expand the device’s
capabilities.
Some participants noted that the vibrations lacked sufficient
strength for specific applications. Based on this feedback, future
iterations of Tape-Tics may incorporate more powerful motors, such
as linear resonant actuators (LRAs), to enhance vibration intensity
where needed. We also plan to further evaluate vibration strength
and develop customizable options to meet participants’ specific
requirements. Additionally, the weight of the battery and the need
to use a DC jack compromise the flexibility of Tape-Tics, which
is one of its key features. To address this issue, future iterations
will consider integrating a built-in battery and using a thin lithium-
polymer battery to maintain the device’s flexibility.

7.2 Usability
While the current GUI enabled beginners to control the device
effectively, there were requests for greater platform compatibility,
particularly with smartphones. To improve accessibility, especially
in educational settings where students may not have access to
PC, the next version will include a mobile-friendly GUI for use on
smartphones and tablets.
Several participants mistakenly connected the device by insert-
ing it into the connector in the wrong direction, a frequently ob-
served issue. We plan to add visual guides such as arrows to address
this in future models. These improvements aim to prevent incorrect
connections and enhance usability.
Additionally, challenges arose with attaching the Tape-Tics mod-
ules to particular objects or body parts, especially ensuring a sta-
ble connection during use. Future improvements could focus on
refining the tape’s adhesive properties or developing specialized
attachments for different applications, ensuring stability and ease
of use.
7.3 Future work
Future workshops will target a broader audience, ranging from ele-
mentary and junior high school students to graduate students and
professional haptic researchers. This diverse range of participants
will provide valuable feedback and help improve Tape-Tics as both
an educational tool and a research device. Additional experiments
will examine the extent of vibration propagation between modules,
conduct comparative evaluations with existing haptic development
tools, and verify detailed specifications of the substrate’s flexibility.
Furthermore, circuit designs and software information are available
on GitHub 12 , allowing users to customize Tape-Tics by adjusting
its size or replacing components, such as the vibrator, according to
their preferences.

8 Conclusion
This study introduces Tape-Tics, a modular vibrotactile feedback
system inspired by the concept of tape LEDs. Tape-Tics allows users
to intuitively create arrays of small vibration motors and LEDs,
which can be cut to any desired length and easily reconfigured for
various applications. Like traditional tape, Tape-Tics is made of a
flexible, cuttable substrate. This feature allows users to easily cut
it to the desired length with scissors, reconnect tapes, and attach
them to curved surfaces, such as the human body, taking advantage
of its flexibility. Tape-Tics can be easily controlled through a user-
friendly GUI controller, enabling users to operate individual nodes
and provide localized tactile feedback in wearable designs.
In a hands-on workshop for high school students, all participants
successfully used the GUI controller to achieve their desired vibra-
tion effects. Additionally, they fully customized Tape-Tics, leading
to the emergence of creative ideas. Survey and interview results
revealed that most participants enjoyed the workshop, became
more aware of vibrotactile feedback, and demonstrated that using
Tape-Tics for understanding haptics in education was effective.
