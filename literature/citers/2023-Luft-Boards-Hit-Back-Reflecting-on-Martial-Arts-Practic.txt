1 INTRODUCTION 
Designing for bodily activities is becoming an increasingly pop-
ular topic in the feld of interaction design. There are interaction 
design projects for running [46], dancing [2, 16, 35], and extreme 
sports [56, 57]. In these works, it is clear how technology adds novel 
experiences [57], changes existing [7] or even creates for entirely 
novel bodily practices [43]. As with any new technology entering 
into a pre-existing practice, changes will occur to: the practice itself; 
those engaged in the practice; the organisations surrounding it; the 
rules, the objectives and participants [34]. For example, the mar-
tial arts vest designed by Chi and colleagues [7, 8] for Taekwondo 
competitions required competitors, judges and spectators to adjust 
their expectations and strategies. Turmo Vidal describes how yoga 
experts changed their practice to accommodate new technology 
she had developed [72], adapting their teaching methodology, and 
occasionally the exercises themselves, in order to accommodate 
the technology and its role in the practice. What we ask here goes 
to the deeper aspects of the bodily practices – its norms and un-
derlying aims and how those may become discernible, articulated, 
challenged and even creatively changed in the design process. As 
argued by Shusterman [63], there is a diference between “routine, 
unintelligent habit” and “intelligent or artistic habit” fused with 
thought and feeling. What we will argue here, is that soma de-
sign methods [26, 29] may help practitioners uncover their own 
deeply ingrained, tacit, normative habits making them attainable 
for change. Through a detailed phenomenological account, we will 
show how the frst author, an expert practitioner acting as soma 
designer, changed through the design process, enabling him to 
cultivate and create new artistic habits.. 
The soma design process led to a tool aimed to be used in training 
sessions – named Koinryo¯ 1. Martial arts in general and Bujinkan 
Budo Taijutsu, practiced by the frst author, provides an engaging 
domain for design due to its strong normative stance – prescribing 
certain movements, principles, strategies, pedagogy, and values. 
While we might at frst assume that this would mean that prac-
titioners would resist change, that the norms are so strong that 
nothing can be changed, we note instead how creative change and 
development is encouraged in Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu. It is seen as 
a path to improving the practice. This said, any changes need to re-
late to the norms of the practice, harmonising with its overarching 
philosophy. 
In the soma design process of Koinry¯o, the tension between 
normative expectations and movements enabled by diferent ver-
sions of the prototype, helped the expert to uncover and articulate 
some of the tacit norms of the martial art practice itself. As we 
will show, the design process unearthed norms, re-enforcing some 
while challenging others. In the end, the soma design process led 
him to extend his repertoire of solo training exercises not only with 
the artefact, but also without it, for example, as we will show in 
detail below, practicing elbow locks with unbendable objects.

Our main contribution lies in the detailed, phenomenological 
account of a bodily practice expert in a soma design process. Even 
though bodily practice experts are often engaged with design work 
as users, facilitators and designers, their engagement is often posi-
tioned as contributing to designed artifact(s) to be used by others. 
Moreover, while expert-led or autobiographical design processes re-
ported in the literature have led to changes in participant behaviour 
or understandings (such as in [2, 38, 57, 65, 70, 72]), they have, with 
few notable exceptions [35, 39, 51]) not engaged with changes of 
movement-based habits or increasing aesthetic appreciation of so-
matic experience. We add a meticulous, detailed, description of how 
a soma design process changed the somatic practitioner himself. 
We argue that such a refective and creative design process could 
be employed by bodily practice experts as a path to not only en-
riching an established bodily practice, challenging and developing 
its norms and practices, but also changing the experts themselves – 
no matter whether the design outcomes becomes a standard part of 
the practice or not. The estrangement methods and (self-) refection 
ofered by the soma design process is thereby of relevance to those 
who (1) want to become better experts in their own bodily practice, 
or (2) want to improve and change their practice. 
2 BACKGROUND 
2.1 Soma Design 
We base our design explorations on soma design [26, 30] – a frame-
work that focuses on bringing the felt experience of bodily activities 
to the foreground of awareness and using those felt experiences 
as a resource in the design process. Soma design utilises a frst-
person perspective and brings attention to the creative meaning-
making that emerges during the design process. Soma design has 
proven to be generative to designing for somatic practices, such as 
dance [2, 15, 35], singing [11], breathing [36, 65] and yoga [72]. 
Grounded in the pragmatist philosophy of somaesthetics [63] 
and Sheets-Johnstone’s work in evolutionary biology [60, 61], soma 
design adopts a non-dualistic stance towards of our lived experi-
ence. By introducing the concept soma, Shusterman refers to "our 
bodily subjectivity", an integrated whole where movement, emo-
tion, thought and intercorporeality are not separable, and where 
the subject possesses bodily, somatic intentionality and subjective 
agency. Soma design calls for cultivating awareness and places our 
somas centrally as the site of meaning-making when interacting 
with technologies and the world at large. In order to cultivate so-
matic awareness, soma design uses a variety of techniques, some of 
them build on engaging with somatic practices such as Feldenkrais, 
yoga, dance, and in this work, martial arts. 
The philosophical grounding of soma design resonates well with 
recent analysis of martial arts by philosophers and autoethnogra-
phers. The identifcation of somaesthetics appreciation in martial arts 
was suggested by Mullis [48], who discussed how such appreciation 
is cultivated in both practitioners and spectators of Chinese martial 
arts. Cohen’s "somatic codes" [10] explain the meaning-making 
process karate practitioners engage in when learning movements. 
The somatic codes correspond to what Sheets-Johnstone framed as 
"somatic logos" [60]. That is, the meaning of a sequence of move-
ments is embedded in the movements themselves – no language is 
needed to explain them. Lastly, Pettinen [55] introduces the concept

2.2 Designing for Established Bodily Practice 
Nylander and colleagues [53] identifed four themes in HCI research 
on sports: design to increase bodily control and awareness; sports 
motivation and fun; pain and discomfort; and the use of pre-existing 
technology in sports. More recently, a 2021 literature review by 
Turmo-Vidal and colleagues [75], mapped out the design space 
of wearables in sports and ftness. The review grouped works by 
topics such as: the practice used; output and input technologies; 
social aspects of the design; and the length of the interaction. Their 
survey, despite their primary focus on wearable devices, identi-
fed certain aspects of the design space that are relevant to this 
work: synchronous versus asynchronous interactions, changing 
practices, prescribed versus non-prescribed interaction, and the 
role of the person using the technology. In a survey from 2009, Baca 
and colleagues [3] reviewed diferent technological advances and 
their efect on sports. In their survey, they covered the systems for 
performance analysis, for assisting coaches, for making judgement 
in sport events and systems used for entertainment. They set em-
phasis on the future systems that could facilitate novel strategies 
and interventions – rhyming with our intentions here. 
By instead classifying the works by their contribution towards 
practitioners the following broad categories emerge: (1) systems 
that are focused on improving the performance of an athlete/practitioner, 
usually with the assistance of a coach or other expert; (2) systems 
that are aimed at assisting in the instruction of a bodily practice;

and (3) systems that aim to enrich, transform or reinvent the bodily 
practice by technological intervention. 
In the frst category, we fnd several works exploring perfor-
mance enhancement in martial arts using technology. Benitez San-
tiago and Miltenberger [4] explored the possibilities of using video 
feedback to improve performance of Capoeira practitioners by 
recording and replaying their movements for further analysis. Hämäläi-
nen [23] explored a "video mirrors" – systems that use video record-
ing to mirror the user – and their appropriateness for use by martial 
artists and acrobats wishing to improve their technique using difer-
ent interface strategies. Jeong and colleagues introduced sensors to 
the handle of a shinai 2 with the goal of using measured pressure on 
the handle to improve top athletes performance. Performance en-
hancement is in focus for many design works in sports, for example 
for skiing[42], running [52], golf [52], and strength training [54]. 
In the second category, we fnd works that examine how artefacts 
can be used to enhance instruction on how to perform a particu-
lar bodily practice, often involving an instructor, but sometimes 
by letting the end-user do it themselves. The BodyLights system, 
designed by Turmo-Vidal and colleagues [72–74] could be used by 
an instructor in such bodily activities as yoga and weight-lifting 
to help practitioners visualise their posture and correct it accord-
ing to normative expectations of the practice. Turmo-Vidal further 
explores the use of diferent wearable systems for purpose of as-
sisting instructors in teaching bodily practices [71]. She frames 
it as designing for intercorporeality and focuses on interactions 
between teachers and students, and on the use of biofeedback. As 
another example of using technology to enhance instructions, Chu 
and colleagues [9] used Tai-Chi, a form of a Chinese martial art, 
as a case study for teaching physical tasks using virtual reality. 
They compared several projections of a prerecorded teacher in the 
VR space, including frontal, mirrored, surrounding and co-located, 
and their afect on movement accuracy, eventually coming to the 
conclusion that none of these techniques improve upon traditional 
teaching Tai-Chi. 
Finally, in the third category we fnd designs that enhance and 
sometimes extend on the experience of practitioners of a bodily 
practice. These includes works that use visualisation to enrich such 
practices as skateboarding [57], martial arts [24], climbing [33], 
trampolining [21] and dance performances [16, 35]. Haptic feedback
was suggested by Allen d’Ávila Silveira and colleagues [2] to act 
as a choreographer, disrupting and guiding dancers’ movement 
in order to facilitate exploring new dance moves. Drones were 
also explored as means to enrich and expand established bodily 
practices such as Tai-Chi [37, 38], opera [14] and running [17, 44, 
46]. A sensor vest was designed by Chi and colleagues [7, 8] as 
a mean to improve upon the judgement process in Taekwondo 
competitions, therefore enriching the experience of competitors, 
judges and spectators. Within the "exertion games" framework [43], 
designers have engaged with physical, exerting game play involving 
interaction between several players inspired children games such 
as tug-of-war [41] and sports such as rugby and boxing [45]. In 
these designs, full-body movement capture was combined with a 
projection in order to encourage players to interact with each other

2.3 Expert practitioners in design 
The core idea of participatory design [47] is that including those 
who will be using new technology into the design process, thereby 
leading to better design as well as empowering those who will be 
using the tools designed. Sometimes, like in the original UTOPIA 
project from the 1970’s [67], this means involving domain experts, 
thriving of their expertise. Expert-led design processes [20, 76] 
lets domain experts innovate in their feld of expertise through 
supporting their processes. In the feld of designing for somatic 
practices, Schiphorst and colleagues [59] propose involving somatic 
connoisseur – an expert practitioner who helps designers to enhance 
their somatic appreciation. Similarily, in embodied sketching [50], an 
expert bodily practitioner helps sensitise the design team as well as 
creating in and through the embodied practice. Expert practitioners 
have played an important role in many soma design projects, for 
example, in designing musical performances [11, 15] or meditative 
experiences [65]. 
In these projects, only a few examined the relationship between 
the expert practitioner and the designed artefact or considered 
how the introduction of an artefact would afect the practice or 
the practitioner. One such examination was done by Karpashevich 
and colleagues’ [35] describing the experiences of a ballet dancer 
attempting to dance with their interactive ballet dress. The dancer 
reported how the limitations and visualisations in the artefact let 
her explore new means of expression, moving in previously "un-
thinkable" ways. In Lie and colleagues’ [39] participatory design of 
the SonicHoop, the expert aerial hoop dancers in the study reported 
that the sonifcation of their movements encouraged them to ex-
plore movements that generate interesting soundscapes, rather then 
let the music guide their movement. Another example is Hämäläi-
nen and colleagues [24] who projected exaggerated movements of a 
martial arts practitioner in a game environment. In their user study, 
practitioners using the system over a prolonged period voiced con-
cerns that it might encourage martial artists to engage only with the 
well-depicted movements portrayed in the game, while dismissing 
other ones; in turn violating the norms in their martial arts style. 
Such challenges to norms are also addressed by Chi and colleagues 
[7, 8] who designed a wearable vest to be used in Taekwondo com-
petitions, assisting referees in setting scores. The introduction of 
the vest required changes in tournament rules and as consequence 
adjustment of competitor-, referee- and spectator expectations. An-
other example by Turmo-Vidal and colleagues [72] reported on how 
yoga instructors changed exercises and structure of classes when 
wearable pointing devices helped students learn better alignment 
of diferent postures. 
In summary, expert-led design work is both common and of-
ten, not always, successful [22], in terms of leading to innovative 
technology and changed practices of relevance to an established 
bodily practice. Within the framework of soma design, the design 
approach is shown to be transformative both for designers who 
engage with building it [28, 64], and for the users who engaged 
with the resulting artefacts [64]. However, none of the studies 
reported here, have provided a detailed, phenomenological account

3 DESIGN PROCESS 
3.1 Methods 
Following the example set by Beuthel and Wilde [5], the design 
process presented here is a simplifed, linear, depiction what was a 
non-linear, messy, back and forth process entangled with and de-
termined by the afordances of available technologies and fnancial 
limitations. 
The design of Koinry¯o was done in the framework of soma de-
sign [26] in which the designer(s) engage deeply, through all their 
senses, with the design materials, be they physical, digital, cultural 
or movement-based, in order to cultivate their aesthetic sensibility 
and create innovative design. Soma designers often rely on pro-
cesses of defamiliarising what is habitual and tacit [40] through 
estrangement methods [78] thereby becoming sensitive to what 
is habitual as well as what could be novel, non-habitual practices. 
Estrangement is a disruption of the habitual way of moving, think-
ing, or feeling. Conceptual element are taken from or added to the 
body or the action, causing norms, traditions and other structures 
to become unstable. This destabilization brings new experiences 
into awareness, making them accessible for scrutiny and poten-
tial change [27, 78]. Practically, estrangement practices interferes 
with the habitual through asking designers to perform an action 
"the other way around", in the non-habitual manner. This can be 
applied both to movements, such as folding your arms the other 
way around, or more generally to norms, ideas, thought patterns, 
emotional responses. Estrangement calls us to challenge what is, 
asking designers to respond diferently, act, move and feel in novel 
ways. Exactly how to devise an estrangement experience depends 
on the design challenge hand. In the design process described here, 
estrangement centred on disrupting Bujinkan Budo¯ Taijutsu move-
ment patterns, but also engaging with interactive digital and other 
materials with the habitual Bujinkan Budo¯ Taijutsu movement pat-
terns and observe how those challenged or changed the practice. 
Estrangement methods, in fact, already play a signifcant role 
in the acquisition of new movement practices, and are common in 
many established bodily practices including martial arts [10]. 
This design work was autobiographical [51], aimed at designing 
for the author Yoav’s own practice – he is a 3rd Dan black belt in 
the martial art Bujinkan Budo¯ Taijutsu. This meant that Yoav was 
required to fnd ways to defamiliarise his own martial art practice. 
Resonating with autobiographical design, in this work author Yoav 
used auto-ethnographical methods, following that of Höök [25]. 
This entailed documenting his experience with a new technology 
or practice, grounding design process in these observations. He 
meticulously documented his exploration of the design material in 
short written- or voice-based notes, images and videos. The second 
author, Pavel Karpashevich, acted as the supervisor of Yoav. Pavel 
had previously engaged with experiences bordering between com-
fortable and uncomfortable in soma design processes [32, 35, 36, 69]. 
He was keen to explore martial arts as it also borders on the un-
comfortable. Pavel had also engaged with boxing in the past. The 
third author, Kristina Höök, has a long-standing commitment to 
soma design [26]. Her approach to soma design is as a design re-
search program as outlined by Redström [58]. With this project, she 
was hoping to explore the boundaries of the program — how does 
the rougher, harder, sometimes uncomfortable experiences lead 
to somaesthetic engagement and appreciation? Pavel and Kristina 
followed Yoav’s autobiographical journey, meeting several times to 
discuss choices of materials, lessons learnt on a conceptual level, 
and ways of engaging with Koinryo.¯ 
3.2 Exploring the Design Space for Martial Arts 
After discussing diferent options, author Yoav presented to the 
team a void between individual martial art practice and training 
with training partner, amplifed by onset of the COVID-19 pandemic 
which limited group training. The team set as a goal to explore this 
design space. Author Yoav engaged in a series of dedicated indi-
vidual training sessions aimed at exploring this design space. In 
order to defamiliarise and uncover habitual martial movements, 
he engaged in a variety of estrangement exercises, some of which 
were already established within Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu, while oth-
ers were inspired by Wilde and colleagues [78]. These exercises 
included: 1) disrupting movement of the legs, shoulders and center 
of mass by tying the ankles or knees 30-50 centimetres apart; 2) 
limiting the shoulder movements by afxing an elbow at a certain 
position to the body with a wooden stick or tying the elbows to the 
torso; 3) using weights and resistance belts to alter movements such 
as side-stepping and punching by holding weights or connecting 
weights to the belt to create an improvised "tail"; 4) manipulating 
his own body, for example by pulling his own knee using a rope 
anchored at the wall, in a manner similar to a takedown manoeuvre 
practiced in some martial arts (see fgure 4). These exercises helped 
to deconstruct specifc movements, for example, side-stepping was 
deconstructed by wagging a "tail" made of weights, amplifying the

During the exploration phase, diferent exercises were docu-

These early explorations of martial arts movement explored also 
how the natural environment can be used as a design resource [66], 
as author Yoav trained mostly outdoors. By engaging in martial 
arts training both indoors and outdoors, Yoav explored the expe-
riential qualities and dynamic gestalt of the materials he engaged 
with, such as the ruggedness of tree bark, the slipperiness of an 
icy feld and the elastic properties of the rubber bands and young 
tree branches. The protruding branches of fallen tree invited author 
Yoav to consider them as indicators of imagined attacks that can 
be moved around, attacked from below, or against. These explo-
rations included conditioning exercises that are practiced in author 
Yoav’s branch of Bujinkan, such as hitting a dead branch until it 
breaks, or pulling pieces of bark from decaying logs and stumps. 
Similar to how brick-breaking or tatami-cutting is practiced in other 
martial arts, breaking these parishable materials provided reassur-
ance of the practitioner’s martial prowess. Such practices cultivated 
awareness of the impermanence of the materials used, and sparked 
interest in the opportunities presented by using materials who can 
be destroyed through practice. 
3.3 Early Prototypes 
Based on the insights from the defamiliarisation of the habitual 
martial art routines, two diferent forms of interaction were se-
lected as the most evocative: restricting and resisting movement in 
grappling exercises, and attacking and defending in striking tech-
nique exercises. These were further researched by constructing and 
testing low-f prototypes, shown in Figure 5. 
Restricting and resisting movement in grappling exercises was 
explored by cardboard, elastic band and rope prototypes attached to 
hips and arms, blocking or resisting joint movements. This was done 
in order to simulate a) a standof with a sparring partner, in which 
arm locks would be used as an attack technique, b) maintaining 
balance in the situations when the legs are blocked. 
Attacking and defending in striking technique exercises was 
explored by using tree branches, fallen trees or a rope tied between 
trees as makers for the possible movements of an imagined oppo-
nent. Extending a boxing bag or a tree trunk with "arms" made 
from wooden sticks, combining them with a d¯ogi (training jacket), 
rope or resistance belt, allowed Yoav to explore such movements 
as pulling, pushing, blocking, defecting and throwing; all with 
the added "arms". Yoav experimented with free swinging pendu-
lums, constructed from rope and weights, as well as rigid ones, 
constructed by attaching a training sword to a stick in a cross-like 
structure, moving in synchronisation with pendulum, or swinging 
the pendulum and evading or blocking it on its return movement. 
Most of these experiments were conducted outdoors utilising the 
natural environment, embracing a Bujinkan tradition of training in 
nature[18, 19]. 
Through the engagements with the prototypes representing 
grappling through movement restriction, this form of interaction 
was discarded. The prototypes revealed that designing for this 
particular type of movement restriction required two connected 
bodies, as well as whole body engagement, and thus, it could not 
be reduced to limiting movements of a single or a small group of 
joints in just one body. 
Therefore, the further design work was focused on the artifacts 
that could engage in striking techniques, such as boxing bags and 
"wooden dummies"3. Diferent ways to allow these prototypes to 
"attack", "block" or "evade" were considered, for example by at-
taching diferent arm- and leg-like appendages capable of diferent 
modes of movement, such as three degrees of freedom or rota-
tion around one or more axes, to the swinging part of the early 
prototypes (see fgure 5, G & H, fgure 6 bottom right). 
The cases, when the prototype swung its "arms" towards Yoav, 
were reminiscent of an attack of a training partner and made Yoav 
want to try out diferent blocking stances in return. The movement 
of the whole prototype in space, especially when covering a wide 
area, invoked a feeling of the prototype evading or engaging. 
Engaging with the early prototypes made it clear to Yoav how 
the actuators could be added to the interactive artifact – specifcally, 
for manipulating the arms’ positions, as well as starting, stopping 
and adjusting the swinging motion.

Koinry¯o was built as an interactive opponent for practicing attack 
and defense techniques. The intent was to bring the experience of

Koinry¯o consists of several parts, from top to bottom (see fgure

5.2 Movement Qualities 
In Bujinkan, movement norms are embedded in katas 8 – a sequence 
of defned movements used for training purposes – and wazas 9 – 
structured confrontation-like movement sequences practiced with 
a partner. The kata and waza convey knowledge on how to move 
in prescibed ways to attack or ward of an attack. But in order 
to perform martial arts, you cannot solely rigidly move in only 
one way. The ways katas and wazas are constructed also aim at 
teaching more abstract principles of movements in martial arts, 
usually through the use of henka10 which deliberately diverts from 
the original kata and waza in order to let participants explore and 
be challenged to, for example, maintain balance or divert the force 
of an attack.

5.3 Changing Koinryo¯ – Changing Myself 
In this section, the pronoun "I" will be used to describe Yoav’s per-
sonal experience. As shown through the material explorations and 
the new movement practices arising, the soma design framework 
provided a path to deconstruct martial art habitus, defamiliarisation 
of the various movement practices, and ways of introducing difer-
ent technologies and materials into the training. Here, I would like 
to discuss how engaging in the design work, helped me to develop 
better somaesthetic appreciation of my own martial art practice. 
This appreciation contributed not only to improving myself as a 
practitioner, but also made me articulate (to myself) previously 
tacitly understood stylistic norms. The design process itself also 
generated novel training exercises inspired by Koinry¯o that I con-
tinued to engage in my normal training routines, even without the 
artifact. 
Let us start with an account of the novel training exercises, before 
turning to how I changed my expertise and started to uncover some 
of the norms of my practice. 
New training exercises – inspired by Koinryo. Ideas and 
novel movements, which were devised during the training with 
Koinry¯o, have been added to my everyday practice and also inspired 
novel forms of exercises. These included the use of positioned 
branches to practice blocks, tai-sabaki16, as well as counter-attacks. 
Another exercise inspired by the design of Koinry¯o is the use of 
branches to practice locks – such as muso-dori, musha-dori and oni-
kudaki as well as throws such as ganseki nage, depicted in Figure 
15 – all of which involve an elbow lock as part of their fulcrum. By 
placing branches in a position, where the attacking hand would be, 
it became possible for me to practice positioning my body for the 
lock and to apply pressure on the branch until it breaks. This, in turn,

Improving Practice. The designed artefact and the design pro-
cess itself, with its attention on refective practices, enabled me to 
identify ways, in which my technique was no longer, in all parts, 
compatible with the norms of Taijutsu. An example of that is the 
engagement with "bad" interactions shown in Figure 14. In these 
example, movements that had felt correct were later identifed 
through the analytical process as transgressive to Taijutsu norms. It 
is a principle imparted by many techniques that you either position 
yourself at such range from the opponent that it would require them 
to take another step to attack, or that you maintain several points 
of contact with your opponent in order to discern your opponent’s 
next move by tactile means. In this engagement with Koinry¯o, I did 
neither. 
Articulating Tacit Norms. Another form of transformation 
spurred by the design process was a better articulation of what 
is considered normative in the martial art. This transformation 
was facilitated by designing prototypes that challenge the norms, 
and then refecting on the tension generated in order to articulate 
what norm was challenge, why it was challenged, and whether I 
wished to accept the norm, reject it or continue challenging it. This 
can be illustrated by the "wing-chun" hand movement – prior to 
the design work and interaction with Koinry¯o, I would have not 
recognised this movement as a transgression on Taijutsu norms. 
Only by performing this movement both against passive and re-
sponsive prototypes and refecting upon these interactions, I came 
to recognise that it is indeed transgressive, and I came to articu-
late why it was so, by comparison with similar non-transgressive 
interactions. The Taijutsu norm that is being transgressed is the 
one that constitutes that manipulation of the opponents hands and 
posture should always be driven and supported by footwork and 
the use of the whole body17. When exploring movements against 
rigid arms, I was forced to use footwork in order to manipulate 
the relative position of myself against the arm. While using soft 
and passive arms, it was not obvious to me that I am using only 
my hand to manipulate them. Only when combining a structure 
that is both rigid and interactive it became apparent to me that the 
movement is transgressive – this was due to Koinry¯o’s resistance 
to the movement, although not strongly enough to force me to do 
adjust my footwork. The refexive practices encouraged in soma

to Koinry¯o, shows many instances of clashes between what is being 
designed and the pre-existing norms of the practice. If Koinryo¯ 
would be more regularly used as part of Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu it 
would most likely change aspects of the practice. 
We might argue that introducing technology into a martial arts 
practice is not necessary or even desirable as it changes the ’purity’ 
of the art form that otherwise does not rely on any technology. 
At the same time, the overarching philosophy of martial arts en-
tertains and encourages change. Martial arts practices are seen 
as an on-going creative practice as practitioners need to develop 
new techniques to overcome the established practices in their feld, 
which in turn becomes the new norm which needs to be overcome 
by new techniques. This transformation happens both in the move-
ment patterns trained, as well as in instruments such as the set of 
rules (in competitive martial arts), its values and basic assumptions. 
In fact, new technologies often lead to novel martial arts forms. For 
example, the martial arts practice named Kendo was a response to 
introducing shinai, a wooden baton used in manner similar to a 
sword, together with protective garments, in turn leading to the 
development of new techniques and rules [13]. In martial arts, tech-
nologies and novel movement patterns are not seen as breaches 
but sought after as they may renew the practice, invigorating and 
improving on it. 
But our point here was not to discuss how to arrive at the per-
fect design adhering to the underlying norms of the practice nor 
how to make changes that would improves the practice. Instead 
we aimed to provide a rich phenomenological account of how the 
design process changed the expert practitioner himself. Through 
the detailed account of his autobiographical soma design journey 
above, it is clear how tacit norms of the practice became articulated, 
challenged, and understood by Yoav. It involved not only under-
standing the meaning and purpose of some particular kata, but the 
underlying philosophy behind why that kata is trained the way 
it is. It allowed him to deconstruct the habits he had learnt, and 
moreover, let him device novel artistic habits. Let us turn to some of 
the somaesthetic theories to explain and deepen our understanding 
of Yoav’s process and why it is important. 
6.1 Aesthetic appreciation 
In his writings on somaesthetics Shusterman repeatedly comes 
back to the need to disrupt habits and become more aware, discern-
ing the diferent parts of a movement or emotion, feeling every 
little change in your body. For example, training your breathing to 
achieve better focus, or correcting your eating habits from what 
is “habitual” to shape new, more refective, thoughtful movements. 
Returning to Dewey, Shusterman [63] discusses the diference be-
tween “routine, unintelligent habit” and “intelligent or artistic habit” 
that is fused with thought and feeling. To Shusterman, the art of 
somatic refection and conscious control is thus itself a “refned 
intelligent habit emerging from and coordinating a background of 
countless other habits that constitute the developing bundle of complex, 
unstable, opposing attitudes, habits, impulses we call the self.” 
What is it that we achieve if we disrupt the habitual, if we listen 
more attentively to our own movements, discerning all the small 
changes and processes that are involved in moving? Shusterman 
argues that such engagement may lead to better appreciation, richer 
bodily ways of being in the world, and deriving more pleasure and 
beauty. In the phenomenological account above, Yoav has been 
learning and creatively changing his body practice through the 
meeting with a design process. He has not only expanded on his 
repertory of possible movements, but creatively devised new ways 
of training, new ways of moving, new experiences that improves on 
his practice. Höök argues that this is the main contribution of soma 
design [26]. While soma design is grounded in somaesthetics, it 
does not rely on pre-made bodily practices, such as yoga or martial 
arts, as a path to extending on our repertory of experience. Instead, 
by using creative methods such as estrangement it allows us to 
create novel movement patterns, arriving at novel experiences, 
experiences that we may never have had before [26, 36], and, as we 
have shown here, potentially changing the expert designer. 
Looking back over the account of Yoav’s design journey, through 
the estrangement methods, the meeting with the digital and phys-
ical materials, and designing iterations of the Koinry¯o design, he 
came to see the underlying norms and reasons for, e.g. certain kata 
and waza. He also learnt to distinguish between changes to the 
established practices that could improve his practice and those that 
would break with the overarching philosophy of the practice. His 
practice became "intelligent or artistic habit", fused with thought 
and feeling, and his practice changed. 
6.2 Transforming designers 
As discussed while surveying the prior art above, experts have 
played a signifcant role in design work done in HCI before, by 
being a representative user [20], by being part of a user study [24], 
by contributing their expertise to sensitising the design team [50, 
59], or by taking a central role in the design process [11, 21, 33]. 
However, the bodily practice of the expert and how those changes 
through the process is often omitted, or presented as if they are an 
unchanging, anchoring element independent of the design. Here, 
we provided an account of how intimate engagement in design 
work by a bodily practice expert did in fact transformed the expert 
himself. It thereby provides another example of the transformative 
power of soma design, previously shown for designers who are not 
bodily-practice experts and for users [64]. 
6.3 Limitations and Ethics 
There are well known tensions between autobiographical design 
and HCI practices [12] which have infuenced the design and re-
porting of this paper. One such tension is that autobiographical 
design cannot provide any claims of generalisability. Throughout 
we have deliberately avoided any such generalisation. Our claim 
is not that the design is of relevance to martial artists. Koinryo¯ 
represents specifc needs and values of Yoav at the time: forced to 
train by himself, and being disconnected from his training group, 
Koinry¯o was shaped to ft these particular needs and context. In 
fact, we are not making any knowledge claims pertaining to the 
design as such, instead our knowledge claims concern the change 
in Yoav and why it came about. 
This brings us to a second tension between autobiographical 
design and HCI research: how to verify the authenticity of reporting 
and authorial voices. What the autobiographical voice ofers is 
qualitative depth, and as discussed by Neustaedter and Sengers, 
CHI ’23, April 23–28, 2023, Hamburg, Germany Luf et al. 
this depth in reporting may reveal big efects [51]. The big efects 
we report on here concern how norms are made explicit, changed 
and challenged by a soma design process. 
The tension between norms and designed artifacts present ethical 
considerations for future designers: can we predict how our artifacts 
going to shape future users [14]? Will they change and develop 
their aesthetic skills in the direction they want to change? Here, 
we claim that using soma design, and specifcally using experts as 
designers who refect on how they are transformed by the design 
process, can identify at least some of the tensions between their 
practice’s norms and the designed artifact. As argued by Eriksson 
and colleagues, self-cultivation is an ethical act in itself. Articulating 
the tacit norms of a bodily practice is therefore a crucial step in 
designing systems that engage with these norms – reinforcing or 
challenging – in an ethically responsible manner. 
7 CONCLUSIONS 
Through a soma design process, a novel artefact, Koinry¯o, able to 
participate and potentially enrich certain aspects of martial arts 
training, was devised. It was designed specifcally for the Bujinkan 
Budo Taijutsu martial art practice through an autobiographical soma 
design process. 
From the design of the artefact we have identifed and articulated 
some of the requirements and limitations for future designs target-
ing sparring in martial arts, such as the material requirements on 
robustness, fragility, strength, responsiveness or ways of surprising 
the user. We have also brought forth some conceptual strategies to 
work around limitations such as emphasis on softness or cultivating 
a relationship of care with the artefact. 
This said, our main contribution lies in uncovering how a soma 
design process can help expose the tacit norms of a practice and 
thereby help expert practitioners develop their own practice. The 
soma design process, involving a bodily practice expert who used an 
autobiographical design process when designing Koinry¯o enriched 
our understanding of what those norms are on a felt level rather 
than as an abstraction. The design work proved to be generative 
for the expert’s martial arts training even after the artefact was 
decommissioned. Our analysis revealed how layer after layer of 
meaning-making and inner-outer stances in the martial art practice 
were brought to the fore, and made attainable for change. It encour-
age Yoav to take a more refned look into any tensions between what 
is practiced and that the novel design afords and recognise that 
designs can simultaneously conform and challenge a movement 
culture rather than neatly fall into one category. 
Let us end with a quote that captures the richness of Yoav’s 
design journey: 
“Think that what is written down here was done just 
for you, and do not consider simply looking at it or 
familiarising yourself with it or imitating it. Rather, 
you should consider these principles as thought they 
were discovered from your own mind, and continually 
make great eforts to make them a physical part of you. ” 
– Miyamoto Musashi. "The Book of Five Rings".[49]
