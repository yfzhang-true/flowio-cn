Abstract
Inflatable structures have been widely explored in architecture, ro-
botics, and Human-Computer Interaction for their expandability
and shape-retention capability. However, conventional designs are
limited to a single, pre-defined transformation and often rely on
complex configurations and continuous air supply. In this study,
we introduce Foldflatables—a novel class of inflatable structures
featuring programmable hinge directions and angles that can be
TEI ’26, March 08–11, 2026, Chicago, IL, USA Masanobu Kanazawa, Yuki Nakata, Kanako Toyoshima, and Yasuaki Kakehi
dynamically specified. Thermoplastic hinges are affixed to both
sides of the inflatable body, modulating their stiffness through lo-
calized heating. During inflation, the heated side folds and solidifies
upon cooling, allowing the structure to retain its form without
sustained internal pressure. These hinges can be reprogrammed
by reheating, enabling multiple shape transformations using a sin-
gle air pump. We evaluate the system’s angle control precision
and transformation latency, and demonstrate application scenarios
including household items, compact packing solutions, and large-
scale information physicalization.

1 Introduction
Inflatable structures have become increasingly utilized in archi-
tecture, robotics, and human-computer interaction (HCI) due to
their expandability and ability to retain shape after inflation. Their
dramatic volumetric transformation enables a wide range of ap-
plications, including compact deployable playgrounds [ 29], fur-
niture [46], soft robotics [ 18], and adaptive vehicles [ 37, 47]. In
HCI, inflatable structures have driven the development of shape-
changing interfaces, creating new paradigms for interactive physi-
cal environments [2].
Despite significant progress in inflatable HCI and pneumatic
interfaces, existing systems remain constrained to simple defla-
tion/inflation cycles with fixed folding patterns, limiting in-situ
adjustability and repurposing. Pneumatic shape-changing inter-
faces achieve high expansion and reliable shape retention using
pre-programmed geometries and sealed air channels. Systems like
PneUI [66], aeroMorph [42], and PneuBots [68] control bending and
inflation direction through patterned chambers and sealing. Recent
approaches, such as PneuSeries [5] and Reflatables [33], enable se-
lective inflation and shape redesign, but typically require iterative
inflation-deflation cycles or manual reconfiguration. These meth-
ods remain limited to preset shapes and depend on external pumps
for actuation. Thermally-driven materials harness phase transitions
and thermal softening for programmable deformation. Thermoplas-
tics in HotFlex [15] and MOR4R [67] can be manually folded above
glass transition temperatures and retain shapes upon cooling, while
LaserOrigami [32] uses gravity-driven folds from localized heating.
SMP-based approaches, such as ShrinkCell [30], combine polymers
with conductive fillers for reversible actuation, and SMAs deliver
higher force densities but require continuous current to maintain
deformed states [55]. Despite their versatility, these materials often
suffer from slow response times, low actuation forces, and the need
for sustained thermal input. Variable stiffness mechanisms enable
adjustable rigidity using jamming, mechanical reconfiguration, and
field-responsive materials. Vacuum jamming increases stiffness for
universal grippers [4] and particle UIs [11]. Mechanical approaches,
such as variable stiffness actuators [20, 61] and origami metamate-
rials [69], switch rigidity via structural changes. Field-responsive
materials like magnetorheological fluids [ 51] and dielectric elas-
tomers [60] offer fast stiffness control but require specialized power
sources. These methods provide versatile rigidity modulation but
add complexity.
To address these limitations, we propose Foldflatables: a novel
inflatable structure equipped with thermoplastic hinges that allow
programmable control of folding direction and angle. Our approach
uses heated wires for localized heating, selectively softening hinge
regions so the structure can be dynamically fold under pressure.
After cooling, the thermoplastic solidifies, maintaining the trans-
formed shape without sustained air supply. By reheating specific
hinges, users can redefine folding and obtain a variety of shapes
from a single inflatable structure.
Foldflatables advance the state of the art by decoupling shape
transformation from static design constraints, enabling on-demand,
repeatable, and portable reconfiguration. This paper details the
design and fabrication of thermoplastic hinges, evaluates angle
control and transformation speed, and demonstrates application
scenarios for programmable inflatable structures.
Our main contributions are as follows:
(1) Precise control of thermoplastic hinges for programmable
folding angles and directions.
(2) A system architecture with independent air supply and heat-
ing for deployable inflatable devices.
(3) An design and fabrication workflow for Foldflatables, sup-
porting reproducibility and scalability.
(4) Quantitative evaluation of hinge performance and transfor-
mation dynamics.
(5) Proposal of novel application scenarios enabled by pro-
grammable inflatable structures.
2 RelatedWork
2.1 Pneumatic Shape-changing
Pneumatic shape-changing interfaces have been widely explored
for their large deformation ratios and strong shape retention after
transformation. PneUI [66] demonstrated that shape changes can be
controlled by applying predetermined structures to soft composite
materials. aeroMorph [42] extended materials to paper and plastic,
proposing methods to control bending angles through different
heat-sealing techniques. PneuBots [68] controled bending direction
through sealing patterns, while SnapInflatables[64] introduced a
mechanism where two stable angle states switch with minimal
force. Heat-sealed sheet bladders have also been used as soft actua-
tors [38, 39] Pneumatic laser origami [40, 41] enabled bend angle
control by incorporating a zigzag arrangement within sealed blad-
ders. Other works combine multiple linearly extending actuators
to achieve both expansion and tilt motions [24, 34]. Some examples
create shape changes by applying external constraints to inflat-
able structures. Human-scale inflatable structures have also been

explored. Printflatables [ 46] and AirTied [ 45]developed custom
CNC machines capable of folding and sealing, successfully creating
large-scale inflatable structures through printing. TilePoP [56] and
LiftTiles [54] recreate chairs in VR space that users can actually sit
on. Poimo [37, 47] incorporates drop-stitch within inflatable struc-
tures to create human-rideable mobility solutions. Various control
modules for pneumatic shape-changing interfaces have been de-
veloped [9, 23, 50]. However, these studies are limited to only two
shape patterns - deflated and inflated - and cannot modify the in-
flated shape after construction. Research on inflatable structures
capable of multiple shape changes has emerged. PneuSeries [ 5]
proposes selective inflation through pressure-difference-activated
valves. Reflatables [33] enables shape redesign in large-scale objects
using hook-and-loop fasteners on tubes. InflatableMod [31] allows
different shapes by rearranging tubeless inflatable structures. How-
ever, even in these approaches, realizing the desired shape often
requires multiple cycles of air inflation and deflation, which can be
time-consuming, or involves manual work to rearrange patterns. As
such, they have yet to achieve a mechanism in which the structure
autonomously transforms into different shapes with each cycle of
air inflation.
Beyond these examples, programmable inflatables exploit struc-
tural nonlinearity and computational inverse design to realize tar-
geted morphing. Snap-through shells and bistable skins deliver
rapid, high-power transitions between discrete states [14], while
computational morphing shells and inverse-designed surface inflat-
ables algorithmically encode a target 3D shape into a flat precur-
sor that deploys under pressure [17, 44]. SnapInflatables integrate
snap-through instabilities for responsive interactions [65]. However,
these strategies typically fix the deformation program at fabrication
time or depend on discrete stable states. Achieving on-demand se-
lection of folding sequences and continuous bend angles—especially
with a single pneumatic inlet—remains challenging; Foldflatables
targets this gap via selective hinge heating and passive locking.
Therefore, Foldflatables proposes a mechanism that can program-
matically change inflatable structure shapes with each air inflation.
2.2 Thermally-driven Shape-changing Materials
Materials that deform in response to heat have been extensively in-
vestigated in the design of shape-changing interfaces. This category
includes thermoplastic materials, shape memory polymers (SMP),
shape memory alloys (SMA), and systems driven by Joule heating.
Thermoplastics are commonly used in fabrication processes such
as vacuum forming and 3D printing, and their thermal properties
enable them to soften above the glass transition temperature and
be reshaped. Systems such as HotFlex [15] and MOR4R [67] allow
manual bending of plastics, while LaserOrigami [32] uses gravity-
driven folding. Deformation via pneumatic actuation [32, 59, 63]
and vacuum-based methods like ProtoMold [ 62] have also been
demonstrated. Some thermoplastics contract when heated [8], and
4D printing techniques [ 16, 21, 35, 53, 58] leverage differential
shrinkage for programmable deformation, with Inkjet 4D Print [35]
achieving high-resolution results. However, thermoplastic methods
often require manual post-heating deformation and make selective
heating of specific regions difficult.
Shape memory polymers (SMPs) can be temporarily deformed
and then recover their original shapes when heated above a transi-
tion temperature [3, 10]. ShrinkCell [30] combines multiple SMPs
with conductive filaments to achieve directionally controlled bend-
ing. However, SMPs typically provide low actuation force, making
large or rapid deformations challenging.
Shape memory alloys (SMA) exhibit reversible deformation when
heated by electrical current and have been applied in various inter-
active systems [7, 13, 26, 48, 55]. Joule heating is also used to drive
shape change in systems such as Unimorph [ 19], Printed Paper
Actuator [57], and Liquid Pouch Motors [36]. However, SMA and
Joule-heating approaches often require continuous energy input to
maintain the transformed shape.
Thermally responsive approaches excel at encoding deforma-
tion into matter—e.g., via anisotropic fiber layouts in 4D print-
ing [12]—but often (i) require post-processing or global heating
that makes selective activation in assembled inflatables difficult,
(ii) fix the deformation field before use, or (iii) demand continu-
ous energy input to hold the transformed state (as with SMA or
actively heated unimorphs). In contrast, Foldflatables applies em-
bedded resistive heaters only to choose local hinge states during
inflation; once cooled, hinges re-stiffen and the shape is passively
maintained, enabling multi-state morphing across inflation cycles
without continuous heating.
Foldflatables addresses these limitations by combining thermally
driven actuation (e.g., embedded heating wires) with pneumatic
pressure, enabling programmable, multi-state shape changes that
can be maintained without continuous heating.
2.3 Variable Stiffness Mechanisms
In addition to shape-changing methods, research on variable-
stiffness mechanisms aims to enhance haptic feedback and enable
shape locking. Major approaches to varying stiffness include the
jamming phenomenon, mechanical reconfiguration, and the use of
field-responsive materials.
Jamming-based systems use vacuum pressure to compact par-
ticles or layers, increasing inter-particle friction and rigidity, as
reviewed in soft robotics [27]. This principle underlies universal
grippers [4], locomoting robots [52], and programmable particle
UIs [11, 43]. To achieve directional stiffness, square-fiber jamming
offers tunable anisotropy [1], while tendon-driven fiber jamming
integrates shape sensing and variable rigidity in a compact form
factor [22]. More recently, motor-tendon jamming mechanisms
provide fast, portable stiffness transitions without bulky vacuum
pumps [6]. Despite these advances, many systems still depend on
external vacuums or motors, increasing complexity and weight.
Mechanical reconfiguration provides precise stiffness control
by physically altering the structure. Variable Stiffness Actuators
(VSAs) adjust spring preload or lever arms, offering high force den-
sity for dynamic tasks like legged locomotion [20, 61]. Compact leaf-
spring joints achieve lightweight stiffness modulation in small-scale
mechanisms [28]. Origami-inspired metamaterials use controllable
crease patterns for multi-state stiffness [69], and 3D-printed liquid
crystal elastomer actuators enable reversible, high-work-capacity
changes without discrete linkages [25]. However, these mechani-
cal approaches often introduce additional system complexity and

bulk due to extra motors, linkages, and intricate manufacturing
requirements.
Field-responsive materials provide rapid stiffness changes with-
out large mechanical parts. For instance, Field-responsive materials
change stiffness under external fields without moving parts. Mag-
netorheological fluids in haptic arrays use permanent magnets for
high-resolution texture rendering [51]. Dielectric elastomer actu-
ators (DEAs) function as artificial muscles that stiffen under high
voltage [60]. Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) combine programma-
bility and large reversible contraction for modular soft robots with
tunable profiles [70]. However, heavy magnets, high-voltage re-
quirements, or complex electronics hinder their integration.
Each of these stiffness control methods presents trade-offs, such
as bulky equipment, mechanical complexity, or specialized power
needs. Against this backdrop Foldflatables achieves both actuation
selection and stiffness control using only metered heat in ther-
moplastic hinges: heating softens hinges to permit folding, while
cooling restores stiffness to lock the configuration. This minimizes
external hardware while preserving single-inlet, per-cycle repro-
grammability.
In Foldflatables, stiffness is controlled solely by adjusting the
heating level of embedded heating wires within the thermoplastic
resin; this change in stiffness both drives shape transformation and
locks the structure into its new form, enabling fully reconfigurable
architectures
3 Foldflatables
3.1 System Overview
Inflatable structures are highly valued for their large expansion
ratios and excellent shape retention. However, when a single air
inlet is used, the pouch layout, connections, and internal air path-
ways are fixed during the design and fabrication stages, effectively
making the obtainable shape unique. To realize multiple shapes,
multiple inlets or chambers are typically required. Therefore, a
method that enables diverse shape transformations while retaining
a single inlet is highly desirable.
We present Foldflatables, a system that enables switching of
folding behavior and overall shape using only one inlet by manipu-
lating the relationship between pouches—specifically, the folding
angle. Unlike conventional geometric constraints using rigid plates,
which control folding behavior in a fixed manner [63], our ap-
proach varies the stiffness of the plates (hinges) between pouches
to actively control both the folding direction and folding angle.
Even under identical pressurization, selective modulation of local
stiffness allows the system to generate multiple distinct shapes.
Fig. 2 illustrates representative shape transformations. The sheet-
like inflatable structure placed at the center is Foldflatables, which
consists of multiple pouches operated through a single air inlet.
When air is supplied, the structure deploys, and the overall shape
can be manipulated by controlling the folding angles of the hinges
located between the pouches. Venting the air restores the original
flat configuration, and reinflating with a different control command
allows the generation of an alternative shape.
Foldflatables is composed of a series of pouches connected by
thermoplastic hinges placed between them (Fig. 3A). A thermo-
plastic hinge governs the relative folding motion between adjacent

3.2 Folding Techniques for Foldflatables
3.2.1 Controlling the Folding Direction. Foldflatables steers folding
direction by creating a temporary stiffness asymmetry across the
hinge: heating only one side plate above𝑇𝑔 softens it, leaving the op-
posite plate stiffer. Upon pressurization, the structure folds toward
the stiffer side. Pre-pressurizing slightly before heating equalizes
pressure between pouches, ensuring uniform inflation even for
pouches distant from the inlet when strong pressurization follows
(Fig. 4).

3.3 Hinge Design
3.3.1 Hinge Geometry Optimization. To widen the maximum
achievable folding angle, we compared four geometries—line, rec-
tangle, diamond, and diamond+rectangle. The diamond+rectangle
leverages the adhesion of the rectangle and the large-angle benefit
of the diamond, achieving the widest range (Fig. 9).
Figure 9: Hinge geometries compared.
3.3.2 Guide to Mitigate Shear. If plate are fully bonded, folding-
induced shear concentrates at the edges, risking cracks or delam-
ination. Our guide-based hinge bonds only the plate center and
constrains ends with guides, allowing outward sliding during infla-
tion and guided return during deflation (Fig. 10). This reduces edge
shear and improves cycle reliability.

4 Implementation
4.1 Fabrication of Inflatable Structure
This section describes two complementary fabrication routes: a
lamination-based process for rapid, tabletop-scale iteration, and a
polytube-based process for larger assemblies. Both preserve internal
air paths to support uniform inflation/deflation during multi-shape
operation.

4.2 Fabrication of Thermoplastic Hinges
We describe material selection, thermal characterization, hinge
geometry, and the plate fabrication process, with attention to re-
producibility in angle control.
4.2.1 Thermoplastic Material Selection. We require (i) low𝑇𝑔 with
high flexibility when heated, (ii) adequate room-temperature stiff-
ness, and (iii) 3D-printing compatibility. TRF+H and PLA satisfy
these criteria.
TRF+H (UNITIKA) is a PET-based 3D-printing filament that
softens around 45 ◦C and re-stiffens below it, matching our safety
and actuation needs.
PLA has 𝑇𝑔 ≈ 60 ◦C and is widely available. We compared both
using printed coupons (10×30 mm; thickness 0.2–2.0 mm in 0.2 mm
steps; Bambu Lab A1, 0.4 mm nozzle, 220 ◦C, 100% infill). Under a
10 N load (MCT-1150; clamp 10 × 20 mm), we measured deflection
at room temperature and under hot-air heating (100 ◦C, GHG 23-
66). TRF+H exhibited slightly greater flexibility at equal thickness,
especially at ≥ 0.6 mm. A thickness of ∼ 0.8 mm provided the best
balance of room-temperature rigidity and heated flexibility, and is
used henceforth.

4.3 Air Valve for Foldflatables
To enable Foldflatables to retain its transformed shape even after
detaching the air module, we designed a thin 3D-printed connector
incorporating an internal TPU valve and an external lever/lock
mechanism (Figs. 19, 20). Lowering the lever opens the valve and
seals the airflow path through a TPU gasket; the lock prevents
accidental detachment. Raising the lever closes the valve and dis-
connects the connector. This enables hands-free inflation/deflation
and on-device air retention.

4.4 Fold-Angle Control Module
The hardware system consists of three main units (Fig. 21): (A) Pneu-
matic Unit — equipped with a diaphragm pump and dual solenoid
valves, it manages inflation, venting, and sealing; (B) Temperature
Control Unit — comprising a 16-channel PWM driver and MOSFETs,
it regulates heating for each thermoplastic plate; (C) Control Unit
— incorporating an MCU, TFT display, sensor ports, and wireless
communication, it executes transformation patterns and provides
real-time monitoring. This modular architecture enhances both
reliability and portability, enabling battery-powered autonomous
operation of the system.

4.5 User Interface
The GUI orchestrates the entire workflow—designing multi-shape
nets, validating each configuration, and dispatching command sets
to the control module for fabrication. The layout consists of three

5.3 Heating/Cooling Cycle and Minimum
Interval
Between two different shapes the structure must briefly return to
the flat baseline; during this phase the plate cools below𝑇𝑔. Because
warmed plates do not cool instantly, residual heat can accumulate
over consecutive transformations and degrade performance. We
therefore measured the minimum interval that preserves repeatable
operation.
A thermistor (MF55, 𝐵 = 3435) calibrated at 25 ◦C monitored
surface temperature. The heater drive was fixed at 40% PWM ( ≈
2.0 V) for all runs. Starting from a 5 min interval, we shortened the
interval in 1 min steps and ran four cycles per condition. Fig. 27
summarizes the maximum folding angle per interval; Fig. 26 shows
representative plate temperature traces.
For intervals ≥ 4 min (Fig. 26A), plates reliably returned near
room temperature by air cooling, and angles remained stable and
repeatable (average ∼75◦ across cycles; Fig. 27). At a 2 min interval
(Fig. 26B), a single flip from one maximum to the opposite maximum
was still achievable, but residual heat accumulated across cycles,
breaking repeatability. At 1 min (Fig. 26C), the plate temperature
never fell below 𝑇𝑔 before the next cycle; target angles were not
reached and transformations remained incomplete. Repeated 1 min
cycles also exposed the nichrome wire (Fig. 28), indicating material
degradation under excessive heat.
In practice, repeated angle changes require at least two cooling
phases of 4 min each (total 8 min). For a one-off change to a different
shape, two phases of 2 min each (total 4 min) suffice.

6 Application Scinarios
6.1 Large-scale Letters and Symbols
Our proposed method enables the formation of large letters and
symbols from a single polyethylene tube equipped with six hinges.
In this application scenario, a 2.4 m long tube with six hinges
placed at 40 cm intervals (where, to achieve a 180 ° fold, only the
center has two hinges positioned 5 cm off the center line) can be
reconfigured to form various large-scale characters—specifically
“T”, “E”, “I”, “2”, “6”, and “←”. Conventionally, constructing each
character required designing and fabricating a unique inflatable
tube, leading to repeated design efforts and high time and material
costs. In contrast, our approach uses a universal tube design; by
specifying the orientation and angle of each hinge, users can flexibly
reconfigure the same tube into different letters or symbols, as shown
in Fig. 32. Once the universal tube is fabricated, users do not need
to redesign or remake tubes for each new letter. Instead, the same
tube can be flexibly reconfigured into different shapes depending
on the situation and requirements. This dramatically improves the
adaptability and reusability of the inflatable system, enabling its
application across a wide range of use cases with minimal effort
and resources.

6.2 Adaptive Packaging Material
Our method inflates the sheet so that it expands and morphs into
the desired shape, and the post-inflation shape is retained. This
allows the boundaries of protected compartments or target objects

In this scenario, we demonstrate that a single inflatable packag-

geometry. This approach eliminates the need to prepare different
packaging materials for each item and allows the same sheet to be

6.4 Pneumatic Actuator
In our proposed method, repeated shape transformation requires
time, which is a limitation. However, because the angles of the in-
flatable structure can be arbitrarily controlled with high precision,
it is well-suited for actuator applications where frequent adjust-
ments are not needed but accuracy and repeatability are essential.
In scenario (Fig. 37A), we attached an inflatable structure composed
of three pouches to a webcam. Before a meeting starts, the camera
faces the ceiling for security reasons or to avoid accidental broad-
casting. When the meeting begins, the structure transforms to show
the user’s face. If the user wants to show their hands, the shape
changes again to adjust the camera angle. Such situations require
precise but infrequent adjustments. The USB webcam initially faces
the ceiling. The start of the meeting is detected by a meeting start
event from the host PC. The camera then tilts downward until
the user’s face is appropriately framed. For hand sharing, fingertip
tracking of the index finger using MediaPipe Hands determines the
camera’s movement direction. The host PC calculates the required
camera pitch angle and converts it into two hinge target angles
through a calibrated mapping. These two angles are sent once from
the PC to the microcontroller via Wi-Fi, driving the pump and two
hinges to reach the commanded posture.

7 Limitations & Future Work
7.1 Geometrical Constraints
The present design mounts the thermoplastic plates perpendicular
to the hinge line. As a result, the attainable bend is approximately
±90◦ and true 180◦ folding is not supported. Moreover, the hinge is
intended for planar bending; motions that include significant twist
are not directly achievable, which limits the diversity of realizable
geometries.
7.2 Electrical Constraints
Although each plate reaches its target angle with about 1.4 W of
heater power (e.g.,2.0 V at 0.7 A for a typical plate), driving multiple
hinges in parallel substantially increases the total electrical load
(e.g., 8 hinges ≈11.2 W in aggregate, implying 5.6 A on a 2.0 V rail).
This necessitates thicker wiring and shortens battery runtime. Cur-
rently, each hinge employs two plates; with four leads per plate the
wiring harness becomes bulky, leaving little margin to add angle
or temperature sensors near the hinge.
As short-term mitigation, we parameterize heater power by tar-
get angle in firmware to avoid overdrive and store per-hinge presets
to keep energy use predictable. For medium-term improvements,
we are exploring functional 3D-printing filaments to embed sens-
ing directly within the plates—for example, thermochromic fila-
ments for visual temperature feedback and conductive filaments for
resistance-based temperature or angle estimation. This approach
aims to reduce external wiring and enable basic self-sensing without
discrete sensors.
7.3 Scalability Constraints
The minimum practical hinge width in our prototypes is approx-
imately 5 cm. As hinge width increases, required thermoplastic
volume and heater wire length grow, which raises the necessary
heater voltage to reach the softening regime (for a roughly constant
target current density). For example, a 1 cm-wide plate reaches its
TEI ’26, March 08–11, 2026, Chicago, IL, USA Masanobu Kanazawa, Yuki Nakata, Kanako Toyoshima, and Yasuaki Kakehi
maximum deformation with ∼2.0 V (about 0.7 A, 1.4 W), whereas
a 10 cm-wide hinge requires roughly 10 V given the longer wire
path. With a single-cell LiPo supply, hinge widths around 6 cm are
a practical upper bound; wider hinges are feasible with a dedicated
higher-voltage source.
In terms of overall size, lamination is limited by the laminator
throat; commercially available units that accept up to A3 make A3 a
practical upper bound for a single sheet. With the polytube process,
length is not strongly bounded by tooling and can be extended, but
increased length entails longer wiring and higher pneumatic flow,
which can demand a larger pump or longer actuation times.
7.4 Safety Considerations
Stiffness modulation in Foldflatables is achieved through resistive
heating. If a heater lead is miswired or unpowered during inflation,
localized plates may remain rigid while pressure rises, risking plate
cracking or delamination at the film interface. As observed in stress
tests, repeated rapid actuation cycles without adequate cooldown
can also expose the nichrome wire at the plate surface.
To mitigate these risks, we (i) use crimped terminations and
strain relief to ensure reliable electrical contact, (ii) bound heater
power and duration per target angle in firmware to prevent over-
drive, and (iii) default to heating only during inflation. We also
selected TRF+H, which softens around 45 ◦ C; once a shape is set
and inflation stops, no further heating is required, reducing touch
risk during use.
7.5 Further Application Scenarios
The demonstrated applications—large-scale letters and symbols,
adaptive packaging, transformable household sheets, and pneu-
matic actuators—naturally extend to deploy-on-demand protection
systems and low-frequency personal actuation. Examples include
temporary partitions, outlet covers, and reconfigurable play areas
that inflate only when needed and fold flat for compact storage, as
well as slow, precise adjustments of objects such as desk lamps or
side mirrors driven by schedules or environmental triggers. Further-
more, incorporating Foldflatables sheets into luminaires or blinds
could enable minute-scale shape transformations that modulate
brightness and the atmosphere of a space.
7.6 Temporal Limitations
As quantified in our evaluation, repeated transformations require
cooldown (typically ≥4 min between actuations) to avoid residual
heat accumulation and loss of angle accuracy. Many target scenarios
do not require rapid continuous motion; rather, they benefit from
precise but infrequent adjustments with passive shape retention
between actuations.
7.7 Automated Design and Software Platform
Currently, multi-shape development diagrams are created through
manual or GUI-assisted iteration. A promising future direction is
the establishment of an integrated pipeline that (i) imports one or
more target shapes from CAD, (ii) jointly optimizes a 2D net layout
and hinge presets to realize those shapes under pneumatic and
thermal constraints, and (iii) automatically generates fabrication
masks and actuation schedules. We further envision a higher-level
interface in which desired shapes can be specified through natural
language or intuitive sketch-based input, which would then be
compiled into manufacturable nets and executable control profiles.
7.8 Durability
To preliminarily evaluate durability under repeated operation, we
reused the same inflatable structure and hinges across all multi-
hinge evaluations (identical/different target angles). Each hinge
underwent 20 shape changes and 20 returns to flat (i.e., two thermal
events per cycle), for a total of 40 heating/cooling events per hinge
under the common experimental conditions (Sec. 1).
We compared the folding angles from the first five trials and the
last five trials. For a representative hinge, the first five angles were
{86, 84, 89, 85, 86}◦ and the last five were {80, 85, 80, 86, 80}◦. The
mean angle decreased from 86.0◦ (SD 1.67◦) to 82.2◦ (SD 2.71◦),
indicating a modest drift of ≈ 3.8◦ over 40 thermal events.
These results reveal a small but measurable drift in achievable an-
gle after repeated actuation. We hypothesize contributions from (i)
gradual changes in local stiffness distribution due to repeated ther-
mal cycling, and/or (ii) minor seating changes at the guide/adhesive
interfaces. In future work we will (a) increase the number of cycles
to characterize drift trajectories and variance across hinges, (b) log
plate temperature/pressure histories to correlate drift with thermal
dose, and (c) explore mitigation strategies such as periodic auto-
recalibration (updating angle–power presets) and material/process
tweaks (e.g., guide tolerances, adhesive footprints).
8 Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed Foldflatables, a novel pneumatic shape-
changing interface that enables programmable control of folding
direction and angle using thermoplastic hinges. Foldflatables over-
comes the limitations of conventional pneumatic structures, which
are restricted to pre-designed shapes and cannot be easily reconfig-
ured or fine-tuned after transformation. From a design and fabrica-
tion perspective, we developed rapid prototyping methods for inflat-
able structures using lamination and polytube processes, thermo-
plastic plates with embedded heating wires via 3D printing, custom
air valves and connectors, and a modular system with multi-hinge,
multi-angle control and a dedicated GUI. This allows individual
control of multiple hinge angles with a single air source and heating
module, enabling repeatable transformation, shape retention, porta-
bility, and reusability. Our evaluation demonstrated precise control
of hinge bending angles through heating output and cooling time,
bi-directional transformation up to 80 degrees, simultaneous and
individual control of multiple hinges, and transformation times as
short as 2 minutes. Compared to prior approaches, Foldflatables
achieves higher flexibility, reconfigurability, and accuracy. Appli-
cation scenarios include large-scale information physicalization,
where a single polytube can be reconfigured into multiple letters
or symbols; adaptive packaging that automatically morphs to fit
objects; and multi-purpose sports rackets that can switch shapes for
different activities. These examples illustrate new use cases where
diverse shapes can be repeatedly generated and reused from a sin-
gle structure—an ability not possible with conventional methods.
We also identified several limitations: geometric constraints (e.g.,
difficulty with folding beyond 90 degrees or twisting), electrical

challenges (complex wiring, high current and battery consump-
tion, sensor integration), safety considerations (heating elements
and temperature management), and transformation speed (several
minutes required per change). Future work includes integrating
functional filaments for embedded sensing, automated develop-
ment diagram generation via AI and CAD, and developing faster
and more versatile transformation mechanisms. Foldflatables pro-
vides a new technical foundation for flexible, reconfigurable, and
persistent pneumatic structures, with promising applications in
HCI, robotics, information physicalization, packaging, sports, and
beyond.
