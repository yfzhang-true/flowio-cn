Abstract
A Cabinet of Affective Touches (ACAT) represents an approach to 
generate tacit design knowledge and build a repository for creating, 
experiencing and reflecting on designing affective touch with 
soft robotic technology. Despite being increasingly recognised as 
a somaesthetic material for affective touch interactions, knowledge 
on technical parameters and the experiential quality of touch with soft 
robotic technology is investigated fragmentally in separate disciplines. 
Sixty four haptics design students used the ACAT platform and created 
a repository of seventeen designs intended for all primary affective 
dimensions, covering both positive and negative affective experience. We 
illustrate how this process cultivated tacit design knowledge and fostered 
critical reflection on novel design space, in particular, on how to facilitate 
the creation and sharing the designs of uncomfortable touch.

1. INTRODUCTION 
The vital role of interpersonal touch in supporting 
social communication and well-being [12], together 
with ongoing advances in robotics and haptic 
technologies, are leading to increasing opportunities 
for technologies to be in physical contact with 
humans. The affective qualities of technology-
initiated touch thus play a critical role in shaping 
our future relationship with embodied technology 
and robots. Among a wide range of haptic actuating 
technologies, soft robotics actuators have been 
increasingly appreciated for their naturalistic sensory 
quality in haptic interaction. Such quality, affording 
a rich and nuanced experience, alludes to great 
potential in supporting a wide range of applications 
[2, 39, 50, 51]. Despite the promising potential, it has 
been noted that this field is currently in crisis [20] – 
reflected in the lack of sensorial-richness and social 
meaning in current haptic devices. Two barriers were 
identified: the first being that the knowledge on 
technical configuration and felt experiential quality 
is investigated fragmentarily in separate disciplines 
[52]; the second being the lack of design repository 
of different kinds of sensory feedback around 
the body [20, 40]. This gap in knowledge calls for 
an infrastructure that enables the exploration of 
sensory affordance, the sharing and accumulation 
of design knowledge, with a particular enabler being 
the association of the technical configuration with 
the felt experiential quality. Our work is a response 
to this need.
Drawing on lessons from Soma Design practice [17] 
– a first-person design method focussed on felt 
experience, we developed A Cabinet of Affective 
Touches (ACAT) which includes tools for both 
authoring and collecting feedback on soft robotic 
touch design. It was developed with two objectives 
in mind: the first is to link technical parameters with 
felt qualities and second to enable the sharing and 
registration of diversified sensory and social qualities 
felt by different individuals, thus to facilitate the 
accumulation of a design repository. The word 
“cabinet” reflects the features of the platform 
that transcend typical libraries or systems. Firstly, 
our platform includes a physical cabinet (Figure 
1) that contains a collection of design examples to 
complement the digitised on-line repository. The 
word touch is used here as a countable to refer 
to each specific instance of design. Secondly, it 
communicates the intention for the repository to 
be eventually tourable. Thirdly it articulates an 
epistemic stance toward knowledge of technological 
touch—the notion of a cabinet foregrounds material 
presence, situated encounter, and experiential 
engagement. While digital libraries may document 
sensory description and technical configurations of 
a haptic design, the design knowledge on shaping 
the sensory quality of haptic design cannot be fully 
fostered by digital resources and instructions alone. 
Research in design and HCI has consistently shown 
that design knowledge is fundamentally experiential, 
tacit, and developed through doing [14, 42, 49], and 
designers train their sensitivity to subtle experiential 
differences through hands-on exploration rather 
than symbolic representation [17].
Although touch can be used to communicate affective 
experience spanning a full spectrum of sensations 
covering negative, neutral, and positive valence, as 
well as low (calm) to high (exciting) arousal [28], the 
exploration of affective touch design within the field 
of HCI has predominately focused on positive valence 
[18, 33]. We were motivated for the ACAT platform 
to support the authoring of touch intended for the 
complete spectrum [36] including both positively 
and negatively valenced affective experiences, 
and covering low to high activation level. Our 
work contributes to a design infrastructure for the 
affective haptic design community, specifically, it 
contributes: 1) a live platform that can be used by 
designers to author touch and collect diversified 
feedback on its experiential qualities. 2) a design 
repository embodied in the physical collection of 
customised soft robotic touch artefacts and its 
associated documentation of the technical set-up 
linking with the affective quality. 3) Reflection on 
the considerations and scaffolding when facilitating 
authoring and sharing of uncomfortable touch. 
4) Subjective documentation of touch sensations 
created using current soft robotics that may be of 
value to the designers of future soft robotics.
2. RELEVANT WORK
2.1 The Challenges Towards an
Experience-driven Paradigm
Interpersonal affective touch supports social 
communication and helps maintain physical 
and emotional well-being [13, 31]. Novel haptic 
technologies and a proliferation of robotics are 
leading to an increasing number of contexts where 
technologies and humans physically touch. This 
has illuminated an immense design space for a new 
generation of touch-oriented technologies that 
enrich and support human life through physically 
contacting humans. It has recently been suggested 
that in some vulnerable situations, being touched 
by a machine might be preferred over being 
touched by an unknown person, such as when being 
washed [10]. A collective body of work in HCI has 
contributed towards opening up the design space 
of soft robotic design by establishing the potential 
of such materials to offer touch experiences as both 
physically and social-emotionally valuable, that 
might raise body awareness [23, 50], enable self-
care [46], support healthcare aims [34, 53] or social 
bonding [39], augment senses [1], and aid mobility 
[2]. Despite the success of haptics as a field and its 
recognised potential for social and affective touch, 
it has been pointed out that the field itself is in 
crisis [19]. This is reflected in that the promise of a 
haptic revolution in HCI is yet to be fulfilled [40], 
as current haptic devices are still very limited in the 
range of sensations that they can deliver, and there 
is still the bottleneck of developing richer and more 
fine-grained sensations [33]. To address this, a more 
designerly approach moving away from technology-
dominance towards an experience-driven design 
paradigm has been called for [20, 52]. Such a 
paradigm takes experiential quality as the goal of 
design, to engage the sensory and social aspects

What contributes to this bottleneck is a two-fold 
complexity. The first complexity comes from the 
nature of human affective touch—the perception 
of which is mediated by a mixture of our biological 
set-up [29], context and personal preference that 
is impacted by personal, social and cultural factors 
[37]. The second complexity comes from the business 
of designing technology-initiated affective touch 
[52]. Authoring touch is a laborious process that 
requires inter-disciplinary expertise and skills. In 
this emergent design space, knowledge of material 
design, experience evaluation, computational 
algorithms, sensing/actuation mechanisms, and the 
ethics of touch are often investigated separately and 
within different disciplines.
2.2 Towards Shared Repertoire for Designing 
Soft Robotic Affective Touch
Two barriers exist that hinder the advancement 
of an experience-driven paradigm. First, when 
authoring affective touch from technology, in order 
to achieve desirable affective qualities, haptic 
designers need knowledge of what affective quality 
is associated with what kind of design parameter 
[41]. Currently such knowledge is fragmentally 
investigated in separate disciplines, which hinders 
the reproducibility and comparability of the 
artefacts and the intended quality [52]. The tacit 
knowledge of designing and experiencing is rarely 
passed on beyond the local design team. Second, 
we point to the absence of a shared repository of 
material, design samples, and sensations for touch 
designers to draw on. Established design fields such 
as fashion and textiles are grounded in extensive, 
accumulated repositories of material knowledge, 
where designers develop an understanding of 
sensory and affective qualities through sustained 
engagement with material samples, precedents, 
and hands-on exploration [25]. This experiential 
foundation enables them to anticipate and compose 
creative designs by drawing on remembered tactile 
and embodied sensations, using the “felt repertoire” 
to imagine and shape future designs [35]. In contrast, 
when designing affective touch using soft robotic 
technologies, such repositories remain largely 
undeveloped. Due to the dual novelty of actuating 
soft robotics and its emergent sensory affordance 
[51], designers lack established samples, conventions, 
and shared experiential knowledge linking technical 
configurations to felt qualities. As a result, the 
affective dimensions of technological touch must 
often be discovered anew through prototyping 
from scratch. Advancing this emerging design field 
crucially depends on enabling designers to share, 
experience, critique and build on each other’s work.
Jewitt et al. [20] and Schneider [40] call for an 
infrastructure that enables designers to “creatively 
play” with digital social touch concepts and foster 
design. They further propose “sensory measurements” 
to collect data on people’s touch sensations at scale” , 
alongside “libraries of different kinds of sensory 
feedback around the body” . Schneider et al. [40] 
also advocate for the development of performance, 
technical or scientific software libraries. Our effort to 
build a repository for affective touch design responds 
directly to these calls, and specifically addresses the 
two barriers outlined above.
Two main approaches support the design of soft 
robotic haptics. First, open-access toolkits provide 
practical resources. For instances, the Soft Robotics 
Toolkit [16] provides detailed guidance on design, 
fabrication, and characterisation, while FlowIO [44] 
offers modular hardware for controlling inflation and 
deflation. Similarly, the Compressables Prototyping 
Toolkit (CPT) [11] enables compression-based haptic 
interaction design. However, these resources do 
not link affective qualities to manipulatable design 
parameters. In parallel, HCI designers who engage 
with the felt affective qualities of soft haptic 
stimulations explore through a soma design approach 
[2, 46], often employ low-fidelity prototypes or static 
materials (e.g., fabrics) to capture the qualitative 
richness of touch. Yet, the rich experiences felt by 
these researchers, communicated through literature, 
are difficult to access by designers beyond the original 
researchers. Moreover, low-fidelity prototyping 
through manual actuation limits reproducibility of 
dynamic touch pattern. These limitation hinders the 
sharing of physical sensations, the tacit knowledge 
and potential to build on each other’s work, which is 
essential to advance a design field [20].
Libraries can form a way to share and reuse haptic 
design ideas between designers and collaborate 
in a timely but asynchronous manner. Focusing 
more directly on the evaluation within haptic 
designs, Schneider et al. proposed crowdsourcing 
affective ratings for vibrotactile haptic designs [41]. 
Collaborative platforms have also been explored for 
both vibrotactile and shape-memory alloy (SMA) 
actuators; for example, CoTacs [30] supports the 
joint design, evaluation and refinement of haptic 
feedback using SMA actuation. Related systems 
have explored different facets of this space, yet 
each foregrounds a specific layer—tooling, signals, 
or prototyping—rather than the accumulation of 
embodied, shareable touch experiences. CuddleBits 
[22] supports expressive actuation through 
material and software exploration, but remains 
oriented toward individual prototyping rather 
than reusable artefacts. TactJam [43] enables the 
storage and exchange of vibrotactile patterns, yet 
these remain abstract signal representations rather 
than physically enacted touch. Feel-Play-Imagine 
[21] foregrounds lived tactile experience through a 
hardware toolkit, but does not support archiving or 
cross-designer accumulation of such experiences. 
Earlier methodological work such as Haptic Sketching 
by Camille Moussette [32] similarly emphasises 
embodied ideation without enabling systematic 
sharing or accumulation. Complementary work on 
documenting the diversity of social touch, such as 
the CoST (Corpus of Social Touch) [24], highlights the 
richness of touch modalities, though it is primarily 
oriented toward observation and annotation rather 
than design reuse.
Against this backdrop, our work explores soft 
robotic affective touch as a shareable experiential 
material. We foreground a repository of physical 
touch samples that can be revisited, compared,

2.3Towards Full Spectrum of Affective 
Dimensions
Touch can be used to communicate affective 
experience spanning a full spectrum of primary 
affective dimensions [38]. It evokes a full spectrum 
of emotions, from care, to alert, to threat to harm. 
These sensations cover negative, neutral, and positive 
valence, as well as low (calm) to high (exciting) arousal 
[28]. For example, Lee Masson and Op de Beeck [28] 
have profiled 13 socio-affective touch expressions 
that cover all four quadrants of the Russell Circumplex 
[36]. While much touch-oriented design focuses 
on positive valence interactions, Benford et al. [3] 
identified that uncomfortable interaction designs 
underpinned by positive cultural values can enable 
powerful experiences with benefits to participants 
of entertainment, enlightenment, and sociality.
Encouraged by this recognition of the potential 
value of the entirety of emotional response to 
touch, in our study, we deliberately included the 
exploration of designing for unpleasant touch 
experiences, as a conduit to reflect on the value 
and ethics of such activity. The ACAT platform thus 
supports creating touch that is intended for the full 
spectrum (both negative and positive, calm and 
exciting) of emotions. Soft robotics, being a machine 
which is inhuman, juxtaposed with organicness in its 
sensory experience, can at times provoke alienness 
and naturalness in contrast [26]. Exploring this 
ambiguous space may yield findings that define 
previously unknown experience that have value in 
the right context.
The ACAT platform is intended to be used for both 
prototyping and experiencing touch from soft robotic 
technology. It includes a pneumatic control kit (Figure 
2-A), an inflatable pad (Figure 2-B) a textile retention 
band (Figure 2-C) and a webApp (Figure 2-D) at the 
address: https://www.touchlibrary.com. A central 
objective for this platform is to enable the archival 
of both digital and material elements of touch design 
and to enable the sharing and comparing of design 
parameters associated to different affective affordance. 
The design of the platform has gone through two 
iterations. The pilot version had been introduced to two 
groups of HCI designers, practitioner and researchers. 
The current version is modified based on their feedback 
and our reflection.
Touch is nuanced, and touch design is highly sensitive. 
As identified in [50] and [53] the small variation in 
actuator properties (e.g. the sizes of the actuator, 
elasticity of the actuator, surface texture), location 
on the body, temporal behaviour of inflation and 
deflation can result in possibly completely different 
experiences. Prototyping these at the same time 
can be overwhelming. To ensure consistency, the kit 
provides standard actuator with fixed dimension (9cm 
in diameter) made from Smooth-On Dragon Skin™ 10 
Fast silicone. Although orchestrating multiple actuators 
affords more sophisticated experience such as in 
[50, 53], it involves moderating individual actuators 
separately before combining them into a symphony. 
We therefore treat a single actuator as a foundational 
unit [11] for building knowledge to compose experiences 
with multiple actuators.

4. WORKSHOP FOR AUTHORING 
AND EXPERIENCING TOUCH
We deployed the CAT platform v.2 in two design 
workshops in May 2025, as part of the educational 
programme for a Masters HCI course on haptics 
interaction design. Two weeks prior to the workshop, 
a lecture was given on affective haptic design in the 
field of HCI, in order to introduce the students to the 
design space of affective haptics and soft robotics 
technologies. 64 students participated. 7 physical 
prototyping kits were produced to facilitate up to 
7 design groups per workshop. The students were 
assigned to two workshops, and both workshops 
had identical procedures. The students formed their 
own groups of 3–5 people with a total of 10 design 
groups. Each workshop lasted 2 hours. Each group 
was provided with one physical design kit which 
included two plain actuator pads (Figure 3). We 
arranged for the workshop to take place in a maker 
space of the university, so that the students could 
access to a wide variety of materials for customising 
their actuators. The range of materials available to 
them included textiles, haberdashery, paper, foam 
and packaging materials, metal, wood, and other 
quick-prototyping materials. At the beginning of each 
session, participants were given a brief introduction 
and demonstration on how to use the platform. We 
also introduced the tool of the circumplex model of 
affect [36], for which we adapted the word ‘arousal’ 
to ‘activation’ following precedence [6, 53]. Valence 
was explained as how pleasant or unpleasant it 
feels, and activation was explained as how energised 
or calm it feels. Each group was given three tasks: 
to design at least two touch patterns (at least one 
for negative valence) and upload these to the ACAT 
website repository (Figure 4.); to experience and give 
feedback on at least one touch pattern from another 
group; and to reflect on a set of questions regarding 
the design process through written submission. Each 
workshop was facilitated by two members of the 
research team, one as moderator and one as technical 
support for using the ACAT platform..

4.1 Ethics
Within the country where this work was conducted 
a formal ethics review was not required. However, 
the research team followed best practice in terms 
of providing clear information to participants on 
the nature of the study and the ways in which data 
would be processed and stored, and written consent 
was collected. As part of the course, participation 
in the workshop and submitting design reflection 
was mandatory. Students who were willing for their 
design and design reflection to be included in the 
analysis of this research gave their informed consent. 
It was made clear that the students’ participation was 
entirely voluntary and that there was no connection 
to their grades if they choose not to consent. All 10 
design groups gave their consent for their designs 
and reflections to be included in this research.
As part of our design activity was experimenting 
with unpleasant touch experiences, we carefully 
considered the aspects of consent, withdrawal, 
privacy and risk [3]. We mitigated any risk of real 
physical harm or negative psychological impact 
through transparency, supervision, group work 
and free withdrawal. We fully briefed the student 
designers about the intended design activity, 
reminding them about choosing and creating 
materials that do not cause physical injury, and 
working in groups to provide an open space for 
discussion, reflection and for any concerns to be 
raised. Two workshop facilitators were present at all 
time providing additional safeguarding.
4.2 Data Analysis
Our data included the physical customised actuators, 
images and video clips of these actuators in motion 
on the targeted location of the body, intended 
quality description from the design team, feedback 
sheets from touch recipients; brief collective 
verbal reflections at the end of the workshop with 
reflections documented in notes, and finally the 
written reflections. The textual data were analysed 
applying a reflexive thematic analysis approach to 
code and develop patterns across the data [7].

6. DISCUSSION
6.1 Experience-led Design Process
Groups were able to explore ideas in a process led 
by the imagined experiential qualities of the touches 
they and their peers were designing. Although in the 
beginning, students found it challenging in “[...] just 
figuring out how to express something as abstract 
as “affect” through a very physical medium” , and 
they found the material of soft robotics rather novel 
experience: “translating emotion into inflation is 
a weird design process”  (Group 2), they found that 
prototyping with the physical design kit was enabling: 
“the hands-on process allowed us to witness the 
formation of the work. ”  (Group 6). While exploring 
the relationship between their idea and realising it 
the physical medium, groups found themselves deeply 
engaging with the materials in a somatic way: “we 
talked a lot about what different kinds of negative 
feelings feel like in the body, not just emotionally, but 
somatically, and tried to turn those conversations 
into mechanical designs. ”  (Group 2)
Students were able to probe subtle differences in 
nuance, even within the same affective quadrants. For 
example, Group 2 documented their probing into the 
quadrants of negative valence and high activation. In 
exploring a kind of unpleasant touch experience, Group 
2 documented two nuances of experience:
(Referring to Figure 8). (T)the affective quality 
we were aiming for was something that lives in 
the “panic, stress, anxiety” zone of the affective 
space. High arousal and negative valence. It’s 
a band around your neck that tightens when 
inflated, and while we were obviously careful 
not to actually hurt anyone, the physical 
sensation is meant to mimic the discomfort 
and tension that comes with stress or fear. That 
pressure around your neck immediately makes 
you hyper-aware of your body, breathing, 
and the lack of space, all of which match that 
upper-left quadrant (high activation, negative 
emotion). [...] this experience was very carefully 
designed not to be able to cause actual harm to 
its wearer.
“The Spikey-ish Fence (figure 7) lives in a similar 
zone but with a different quality. It’s worn 
on the palm, where [...] metal nails (blunted 
for safety) poke out from foam and inflate 
into your hand. This design leans more toward 
discomfort than pain, like a nagging, creeping 
feeling. It’s still negative valence and moderate 
to high activation, but perhaps less of a “fight or 
flight” and more of a “ugh, get this away from 
me” vibe. A bit more emotionally ambiguous 
than the neck wrap, which felt more direct. “
Reflecting on these processes, we see how important 
it is when designing touch interactions to have that 
first-person understanding of the holistic experience 
and to take time to deeply reflect on how it feels 
when you tweak it, vary it, change the materials or the 
behaviours. As noted in the soma design methodology 
[17], fundamental to understanding what you are 
designing. Without access to these sensations, design is 
not only limiting but potentially unsafe – particularly 
when designing negatively-valenced touch, where 
firsthand experience is necessary for controlling risk, 
and for identifying the balance and the edges of what 
is comfortable, desirable or acceptable. And of course, 
it’s equally necessary to hear others’ reflections on 
experiences you have designed – what may be on the 
edge for you, may be far over the edge for somebody 
else. It is for this reason that the inclusion of both the 
ability to replicate experiences, and a collection of 
different people’s reflections is critical to the value of 
ACAT.

Non-verbal language of affective  touch. While we 
were impressed by the rich imagination felt through 
the poetic names of the touches designed, we also 
noticed a lack of words for describing touch from the 
touch recipients. For example, it is not uncommon 
for the touch recipients to describe the felt sensation 
using a close word in quotation marks to refer to the 
indicated feeling: “’aww--’ feeling” , “inflating and 
deflating ‘slowly’  ” . In contrast, there was a richness in 
body language: “strong responses - nervous laughter, 
tensed shoulders, immediate pulling away. ”  (Group 2). 
This invites us to consider that describing touch is rarely 
exclusively verbal – and thus ACAT needs to be able 
to represent this, both by presenting video recordings 
of descriptions (to capture the body language people 
use to describe touches) and even more critically 
by including the ability to replicate those touches – 
after all “you had to be there” . Somatic experience is 
notoriously hard to articulate [45], so arguably the best 
articulation is first person experience.
Subjectivity . The individually different feedback from 
touch recipients is to be expected, given, as noted 
earlier, the personal, social and cultural individualities 
that impact touch experiences, however, what we 
found interesting was that the groups themselves were 
surprised by the fact that designers within the same 
team could have different ideas regarding the affective 
affordance of the same material: “When we discussed 
creating low [sic] affection and low valence, we noticed 
that everyone had different standards for these two 
dimensions. For example, we thought of using water 
to create a sense of coldness and indifference, which 
we associated with low [sic] affection. However, 
some people believed that the fluidity of water could 
actually feel quite affective. We found this clash of 
ideas to be very interesting. ”  (Group 6) (Figure 10). This 
suggests that designers may not inherently recognise 
the subjectivity of touch experiences. By actually 
experiencing these touches themselves, and comparing 
their own sensation to those reported by others, ACAT 
helps to foster an understanding of the subjectivity of 
touch experiences.
Temporality . It has been recognised that temporality 
of interactive materials [47] has a critical impact on the 
experiential quality of touch [53]. This has also been 
tacit knowledge that our design students acquired: 
“by watching these variations, we learn how subtle 
changes in timing, texture, and pressure can result in 
completely different emotional impressions. ”  What is 
more interesting and rather novel, is that some students 
discovered that there is a temporality in the felt quality 
too, that is, the felt quality, is not always consistent. 
Some particular sensations shift over time: “I found it 
interesting that the soft and pleasant feeling faded 
over time as my hand got used to it (Figure 11), so it 
became less noticeable. But with the metal thorns, 
it was kind of the opposite. The longer my hand 
stayed there, the more uncomfortable and tense 
it felt. The discomfort built up instead of fading”  
(Group 5) (Figure 12). This suggests that designers would 
benefit from recognising the specific temporality of 
individual touch experiences, and that ecologically 
valid experience durations are necessary to properly 
understand the sensation. In its descriptions and 
reflections, ACAT encourages designers to experience 
touches ‘as intended’ .
Students found that prototyping with the physical design kit enabled “the hands-on process (which) allowed us to witness the formation of the work. ”  They were 
able to experience tacitly that the materiality of the physical elements and the temporality of the dynamic behaviour of inflation and deflation are entangled with the 
subtlety and nuances of the felt qualities: “by watching these variations, we learn how subtle changes in timing, texture, and pressure can result in completely 
different emotional impressions”; “Just the smallest inflation at the right spot can trigger a whole-body response. ”  In addition, the process is infilled with 
negotiation around the non-verbal language of affective touch, subjectivity of experience, and the temporality of touch. We describe these separately in this page.

6.3 Universality Vs. Creativity
Some of our students assumed that “touch is such 
a universal experience that most people tend 
to approach it in similar ways. ”  and found it 
“challenging to come up with a unique idea” .  (Group 
5). Most other groups expected that if their design is 
successful, the affective response from people should 
be close to the intended affective qualities, yet 
encountered diverse interpretations from the touch 
recipients. Soma design, the HCI method we take as 
a key inspiration for our approach, explicitly rejects 
homogeneity of experience, instead recognising the 
plurality of individuals [17], which is why it is an 
inherently first-person method. Individual, social, 
cultural and even daily sensations such as mood can 
change the way we ‘feel’ an experience. Students’ 
surprise reflects the legacy of design approaches 
such as user personas or “representative users, ” 
which overlook this complexity.
Some groups in our workshop considered this 
discrepancy as indicating that their design needed 
improving. Some other groups found the opposing 
felt quality of their touch to be an “interesting 
learning” .  It is interesting to us that over half of 
groups considered the deviation of feedback from 
touch recipients as a limitation, as if this framed 
their design as flawed, and that misunderstanding is 
something that ACAT needs to address. This exposes 
a prevailing mode of thinking in the technology-
driven approach to haptic design which considers 
consistency in perceived quality across different 
people as a sign of successful design. An associated 
assumption is that this can also be mitigated by a 
scientific approach to affective touch – that the 
experiment set up needs to be managed to avoid 
variation. We certainly don’t disagree with this 
call for scientific rigour, but we don’t believe that 
managing experimental context could ever resolve 
these differences between individuals’ experience. 
We argue that there is much more to the sensation 
of touch than the environmental context, and that 
this assumption of consistency=correctness is a 
fundamental misconception. We suggest instead 
that the field would benefit from embracing the 
subjective nature of affective touch. We propose 
to flip this anxiety around managing difference, to 
celebrating the diversity and ambiguity in people’s 
interpretation: “we could know really diverse and 
interesting interpretations of what an “affective 
touch” could be. ”  (Group 6)
We suggest there may be value in ACAT for more 
engineering focussed soft robotics designers, who 
may be abstracted from the experiential qualities of 
their work – seeing a depth of subjective reflections 
on an experience created with the same materials 
may encourage deeper reflection on the soft robotics 
systems themselves. Engineers, designers and users 
make up a complex ecosystem which requires 
embodied communication to reflect on both enabling 
technologies and designed experiences.
Technology-initiated touch is such a new phenomenon 
and we are still in the early stages of exploring its 
affective affordances. As one group commented: 
“Just like how music or art hits people differently, 
haptic feedback can’t guarantee a universal 
response. ”  Technology-initiated touch is still a novel 
material for creativity. This echoes with [16] which 
advocates the playful engagement from the creative 
arts, for open-ended exploration towards novel 
qualities of experiences. To support this exploration, 
it is not enough to document designs; a pluralistic, 
subjective collection of reflections on felt experience 
must also be captured and shared as a resource 
for future designers. ACAT does exactly this. This 
infrastructure, enabled by ACAT, could additionally 
serve as a valuable database for reflection across 
disciplines such as social science and health research.
6.4 Designing  Uncomfortable Touch 
Experience 
The greatest number of designs (8 out of 17) created 
by our students were intended for the high activation 
– negative valence quadrant (Figure 5). This suggests 
an interest in this space – perhaps because it might 
be considered a design taboo. In [27], Marshall 
et al. provocatively reframes calculated ‘risk’ as 
adventure and empowerment. Giving our students 
the opportunity to risk sensing the unpleasant or 
uncomfortable, albeit in a safe and monitored context, 
speaks to the potential playfulness to be found in 
this quadrant. This may not be of great surprise 
given that one of the few forms of play often carried 
on into adulthood, as Callois notes in [9], is vertigo 
play – characterised by our predilection for terrifying 
ourselves on rollercoasters and the like. Sometimes 
negative and positive emotions can be intrinsically 
linked, for example fear and thrill, as noted by Ralf 
Buckley in [8] – the risk of the negative (fear) or 
indeed the overcoming of it, leads to the positive 
(thrill). Someathetics theory at the heart of soma 
design recognises that experiences are nuanced and 
inherently non-dualistic [17] – so to assume that the 
negative valence zone 1) does not have a fuzzy edge, 
and 2) should be some kind of design “restricted 
zone” , would be a failure to recognise its potential. 
By explicitly including and encouraging reflection on 
the full spectrum of experiences, ACAT empowers 
designers to engage with this under-explored space 
from a position of knowledge, safety and, since they 
can actually choose to try those designs, first person 
experience.
The students started from trying to simulate a 
stereotypical uncomfortable physical experience, 
what Benford et al. [3] calls “visceral discomfort” , 
without knowing what meaningful interaction 
could be elicited  from it, and through the process 
discovered nuances of discomfort and subsequently, 
through feedback towards such touches, some found 
that safe, nuanced discomfort can afford a rather 
poetic experience. As the teams articulated materials 
to evoke unpleasant experience such as discomfort, 
tension, stress and fear, within safety measures to 
avoid causing real harm, they found challenge in 
balancing safety and the physical intensity of the 
sensation, “We had to tinker a lot with materials 
and inflation levels to get something that felt 
affective without crossing a line… ”  (Group 2). This 
prompted the students to reflect more on the ethics

“Interestingly, the thorn-like texture also

or bring a user to engagement through this is 
“really cool” as it “presses your buttons in a 
more poetic way” . 
Returning then to the value of ACAT for soft roboticists, 
we invite the community to reflect on discomfort 
and safety in a somatic way. In [5], Benford et al. 
argue that people develop somatic understandings 
of how to interact safely with robots, and that 
some of the hard lines of how to manage risk might 
break down opportunity. Assuming that any pain/
discomfort is undesirable, neglects the possibility of 
some potentially powerful or interesting experiences
 – and ACAT includes examples of where pain/
discomfort are aesthetically interesting, suggesting 
they need (careful) enabling. This isn’t a call to make 
unsafe soft robotics – but simply to recognise that 
safety ≠ comfort.
Although this student design journey attests to 
how our toolkit contributes by enabling physical 
engagement and cultivating tacit knowledge. This 
process also helped surface a series of important 
questions regarding deploying the cabinet. 
Because the design was undertaken by students, 
whose professional and ethical judgement is still 
developing, they tended to pursue ideas intuitively 
and without restraint. In some instances, this 
resulted in concepts—such as a prototype termed 
“asphyxiation”—that, while exploratory in intent, 
could be perceived as suggesting self-harming 
actions if disseminated without appropriate framing. 
To mitigate such risk, the research team has modified 
the name with a more neutral sounding one. This 
may suggest the explanation of the full context 
or necessary curation of the design samples when 
introducing them to audience beyond the original 
design group.
7. Limitations and Future Work
The work presented here on ACAT represents a first 
foray into recording and articulating tacit design 
knowledge about affective touch experiences. It 
represents only a scratch of the surface – with a few 
hours worth of a small sample of students’ designs. 
To truly fulfil its potential, ACAT needs to collect 
far more feedback in order to map the emotional 
footprint of each design [4]. And with rigorous 
analysis, it may surface the association between 
actuation and material elements and affective 
affordance. Such will be a real potential of ACAT, 
which contributes to a valuable infrastructure for 
technological touch design. However, this prompts 
the reflection on the curation of the cabinet 
collection, as the database of designs and reflections 
may be more or less infinitely expandable. One key 
limitation of a physical cabinet is that it will reach 
far fewer designers than a digital repository. Clear 
documentation can make it possible to replicate the 
touches, but that lacks the rapid exploration that is 
possible when the prototype is in a box in front of 
you. Earlier in the paper, we criticised the manual 
activation methods often used in Soma Design 
workshops for being hard to replicate outside the 
initial group, and while ACAT has a standard set of 
equipment by which many of the touches can indeed 
be replicated, there is the limitation that the form 
of actuators has to remain the same, which could 
not compare to more refined and unique prototypes 
such as the pelvic chair [50]. And finally, given the 
important questions raised regarding deploying and 
sharing the designs, especially on uncomfortable 
touches, the design team will have to research into 
appropriate guidance for deploying and sharing the 
physical design examples, before the ACAT can be 
made tourable, either in the form of accompanying 
researchers or clearly printed guidance.
8. Conclusion
In this paper we have introduced A Cabinet of 
Affective Touches (ACAT), a collection of materials 
for creating affective touch experiences, with 
an embedded process of recording subjective 
reflections on the felt experience of those touches. 
We have demonstrated how this can be used by 
designers through a series of workshops with design 
students, eliciting reflections on the language used 
to articulate touch, the complexities of temporality 
in affective touch, and the plurality of individual 
experiences. These factors validate the need for 
reflections on affective touches to be bundled 
with the touches themselves, so that designers and 
engineers can build the tacit knowledge necessary to 
be effective at creating new touches – building on 
past experience, both of others and of themselves, 
rather than creating every new touch in isolation.
