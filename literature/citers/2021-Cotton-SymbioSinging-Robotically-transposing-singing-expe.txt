ABSTRACT
In this paper we present our late-breaking work in leveraging a
soft robotic fiber-based wearable system for the transposition of so-
matic knowledge and experience within the context of singing. We
examine how the transposition of the physical nuances of singing
from one body to another, or multiple other bodies, is possible by
engaging with a soma design process. We share our findings in
the context of experience transposition, resulting in a preliminary
prototype: a pneumatically controlled soft robotic garment—called
ADA (short for air-driven actuator) for re-enacting felt experiences
of singing onto the human body. We contribute with 1) our initial
findings in transposing singing experiences between and across
bodies, and 2) a preliminary wearable robotic garment to mediate
intersomatic experiences of singing.

1 INTRODUCTION
In our late-breaking work, SymbioSing, we examine how the so-
matic1 experience of singing and the bodily knowledge of the singer
can be intersomatically2 experienced by another person—or multi-
ple people—mediated by haptic input and output. The act of ‘experi-
ence transposition’ mediates the audience’s experience of symbiotic
engagement with the singer’s body. This transposition allows them
to experience the movements that the singer generates in real-time,
translating her changing physiology to another body. We plan to
apply this form of physical transposition with a performance of
a musical composition that features 3 simultaneously performing
voices; 2 of which are pre-recorded and 1 performed live. This
performance will be for an audience consisting of members of the
general public, interaction design practitioners and HCI researchers,
who presumably are not trained singers. The motivation of this
work is to examine how the biomechanical complexities of a trained
vocalist’s body while singing, can be intersomatically experienced
by a non-singing body. We hypothesise, this will render a novel
listening experience where the audience may feel themselves as
the ones singing, even if momentarily.
Throughout the evolution of our research, we address HCI ex-
plorations of kinaesthetic empathy and telepresence addressing the
communication of bodily knowledge between bodies, as well as
across time and space. For transposing the experiential components
of singing we started with exploring the biomechanical changes of
our body, such as torso expansion and contraction during breath-
ing and micro skin vibrations around the larynx and skull, while
singing. This resulted in an initial implementation of an upper
body garment embedded with a soft haptic I/O mechanism. This
first prototype of an air-driven actuating garment, abbreviated to
ADA3, features an integrated system for playback of biomechanical
changes during singing4. This current system is specifically focused
on breathing as the core movement material, therefore targeting

an interactive upper-body garment for haptic simulation of breath-
ing and torso movements by the integration of fluid-actuated soft
materials with textiles. This work intersects with how creativity can
be elicited, supported and augmented by novel methods, materials,
and tools.
2 RELATED WORK
The aesthetics of touch has drawn interest in recent years [3] [15]
[8] whether it is through touching with one’s hands [7] [22] [30]
[12], or being touched [ 25]. The majority of research addresses
touch on and through the hands or arms, but some explore digital
touch to the torso [ 2] [5] [1]. For example, Delazio et al. ’s Force
Jacket [5] uses an array of pneumatic actuators on the torso to
produce a variety of experiences, from a light, gentle touch to a
punch-like sensation. Similarly, Arafsha et al. ’s EmoJacket which
conveys emotions through vibrotactile and heat feedback [1]. Addi-
tionally, digital touch on the torso has also been used to examine
breathing [11] [10] and to communicate touch such as hugging
[6] [24]. In the context of musical performance, digital touch and
telepresence is notably explored by Turchet et al. ’sMusical Haptic
Wearable for Audience [26] and Xiao Xiao’sMirrorFugue [27] [28]
[29]. We further draw connections to research in somatosensory
knowledge, kinesthetic awareness [23] [14] [4] and empathy [13]
[16].
3 TRANSPOSING SINGING EXPERIENCE
BETWEEN BODIES
In our examination of touch to transpose the experience of singing
between bodies with the ADA garment, we have engaged with
a soma design process [ 18]. This method focuses on designing
with and for the body [9]. The approach is based on somaesthetics
[20] and emphasises the designer’s first person perspective at the
meeting between technologies and bodies [ 18]. Commonly used
soma design tactics include body mapping and facilitation of the
movement practice by a somatic connoisseur [17]. The soma design
method supported us in attuning our understanding of singing
and vocal physiology [ 21], and informed the zoning of actuated
output in the ADA garment. Further, this methodology assisted
with uncovering how the experience of singing can be transposed
to another person’s body through external haptic feedback.
In a series of 6 bodywork sessions, which guided the design of
the ADA garment, we explored intersomatic components of singing
experiences. One of the exercises we discuss here— Singing Body,
Listening Body (or SBLB)—examines touch as transposer of expe-
rience. Some of the tactile outputs that we focused on in SBLB
included skin vibrations around the laryngeal area and head (felt by
the hands), as well as deformation and expansion of the thorax and
abdominals. In this exercise, the Listening Body (LB) wears foam
earplugs and noise cancelling headphones (playing white noise).
At a start signal from the LB, the Singing Body (SB) performs a
sung pattern comprising 3 - 5 vocal gestures. 5 The sung pattern
is repeated for as many times as the LB requires to “hear-feel” us-
ing their hands placed on specific regions on the SB during the
sung pattern (Figure 1a). When the LB determines they have inter-
nalised—or “heard-felt”—the sung pattern, they then sing it back to
the SB.
Reflections on Singing Body, Listening Body
Our engagement with SBLB yielded a range of tactile findings,
namely in regards to the regions of the body we determined were
more “conductive” for the LB. Touching these regions with the
hands made SB’s movements more easily understandable with re-
spect to the type of motion occurring in the body (ie. skin vibrations
from humming ascending and descending glissandi)6. Based on our
findings regarding expansion, contraction and vibration during
singing we identified the type and location of these conductive
zones (see Figure 1b below). The first prototype of ADA focuses on
movement of the torso during singing, based on the torso and ab-
dominal expansion and contraction zones and their corresponding
somatic output. (see Figure 1b).

P2 mused that “we are discovering at different sorts of rates where

this notion of transposing another person’s actions into one’s own
body.
Another notable phenomenon occurred throughout the LB trans-
position process, where the LB would place their own hand upon
their “conductive” body regions after a repetition of the gesture.
This emerged spontaneously when immediately after feeling par-
ticipant 3’s (P3) sung gesture, participant 4 (P4) instinctively placed
her hands on the same “conductive” regions on her own body. Dur-
ing P4’s singing back of P3’s phrase, she kept her hands upon these
conductive regions. P4’s gesture can be seen as an attempt to trans-
pose lingering sensations she experienced from P3’s moving and
oscillating larynx, and P4 singing those back to P3 through touching
her own larynx. As P3 observed when he was a LB, “can I feel the
same movement, can I do the same movement and maybe produce
the same sounds?”. Our experiences with the SBLB exercise indi-
cate how touch can be leveraged as a means of transposing somatic
experiences of singing from one body to another. We used these
findings from the SBLB exercise conducted during our bodywork
sessions to inform the preliminary design of our first prototype,
the ADA garment.
4 TECHNOLOGY AND SYSTEM OVERVIEW
4.1 Wearable Interface: Fabric I/O
To facilitate the transposition of the somatic experience of singing
observed in our bodywork sessions, we fabricated a novel sensing
and actuation system composed of strain-programmable microflu-
idic fibers and fabrics. These fluid actuated fibers—developed by
P1—are an emerging soft robotic technology providing a modular
and reconfigurable system for the fabrication, programming and
control of high resolution shape-changing interfaces. The thin fiber
actuators have strain programming capabilities. Further, they have
unique multimodal haptic feedback capabilities, and can thus be
configured for a highly expressive bandwidth of input and output
motions. Their versatile I/O configurations offer an ideal system for
intimate biosensing and haptic feedback. ADA is our first prototype
of an actuated garment for physically simulating and transposing
singing experiences onto another body, utilising the pneumatic
fibrous materials. Our first iteration physically plays back breath-
ing and torso movement onto the wearer’s body by sequenced
compression and expansion movements. We utilised fluid actuated
fibers, integrated within a corset-style upper body garment with 3
separate inlay-knitted panels layered by a smocked panel and an
active corset-cording on the back that enables robotic fitting onto
different body shapes (Figure 2b).
In the under bust panel (Figure 2e), an actuated fiber with con-
traction modality is inlaid into the knitted structure. In the currently
passive smocked panel (Figure 2c), we are anticipating to utilise

the same fluidic fiber substrate.
4.2 System Architecture
The system control of the ADA garment is built on the open source
electro-pneumatic platform FlowIO [19], to help us create a portable,
compact and unobtrusive method for on-the-fly control of multiple
garments (Figure 3). FlowIO is a miniature integrated hardware
and software development platform for driving soft pneumatically-
actuated devices such as the fluidic fibers. We adapted this platform
to achieve our specific system requirements such as; (1) yielding
high pressures (>300kPa) for actuating multiple and up to 10 meters
long fluidic fibers, and (2) communication via Bluetooth Low Energy
(BLE) protocol for closed-loop bidirectional wireless interaction
between multiple integrated garments. Although we currently use
the 5 port configuration of FlowIO, we will increase the valve termi-
nal up to 20 ports to afford precise control of localised movements
within the multipartite robotic garment.

5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this paper we have discussed our preliminary explorations in
leveraging actuated textile-based haptic feedback to transpose singing
experiences onto another person’s body. We presented our first
prototype, ADA, that utilises fluid actuated robotic fibers to reen-
act the experience of a singer’s biomechanical movements on the
listener’s body. The axially morphing sections in the garment re-
flect the localisation of breathing and muscular engagement which
occurs during singing, forming the first layer of our explorations.
Through ADA, we examine how touch can be utilised to transpose
and transform experiential features of a vocal movement material
from one person’s body to another. In future work, we will integrate
our findings on vibration within an interactive laryngeal-wear. Fur-
ther, we will conduct case studies with larger groups of people,
specifically with non-singers. As the singer’s input gestures will be
directly reenacted as actuation on the audiences’ bodies in real-time,
we will explore complex mappings through machine learning to
develop a more expressive range of intersomatic singing experience.
Currently, we use machine learning algorithms to train a computer
to recognise input gestures and map them to a predefined actuation.
During the performance, the trained model will be fed with the
singer’s input data and send appropriate actuations to the listening
garments. Additional applications of AI may include the adaptation
of output gestures to the audience garments, taking into account
factors such as variations in body size and shape as well as the
stages of the breathing cycle7
