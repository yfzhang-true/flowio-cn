SOFT OBJECTS
Prototyping for Tactile Interpersonal Communication
ABSTRACT
Sensory perception plays a fundamental role in how 
we interpret and relate to people and our environment. 
While verbal communication dominates much of our 
interaction, nonverbal forms, such as haptics, present 
alternatives for connection. This pictorial investigates 
the potential of haptic interaction through an iterative 
prototyping process, culminating in SYMBIONT—a pair 
of bioinspired soft robotic objects designed for affective 
tactile communication across distances or in shared 
spaces. 
SYMBIONT emerges from three prototypes—HiPalm, 
EmoLink, and OctoTouch—each exploring different 
dimensions of tactile expression. The final design 
enables users to engage in tactile communication 
through embedded bend and pressure sensors paired 
with actuators that pulse, vibrate, and curl. 
The prototyping approach integrates metaphorical 
frameworks, biomorphic aesthetics, and biomimetic 
behavior. Methods include sketch-based ideation, 
annotated portfolios, and Research through Design, all 
contributing to developing a responsive tactile system. 
This research explores how such mediums might 
enhance physical presence and open new possibilities 
for tactile, bioinspired, and sensory communication 
systems.

1 INTRODUCTION
This research aims to develop a bioinspired soft robotic 
object to augment tactile interpersonal communication. 
Focusing on design process and implementation, this 
pictorial explores how a soft robot  might facilitate 
tactile, metaphorical interpersonal communication 
and emotional sharing between distant or co-located 
individuals.
SYMBIONT emerges from an iterative prototyping 
process involving three earlier prototypes: HiPalm, 
EmoLink, and OctoTouch. Each contribute uniquely—
HiPalm explores soft robotic actuation for affective 
feedback [55], EmoLink establishes interaction logic and 
component selection, and OctoTouch refines biomorphic 
aesthetics, sensory elements, and the communication 
protocol later implemented in SYMBIONT. This design-
oriented process emphasizes tactile experiences with 
SYMBIONT’s soft body, with metaphor playing a key role 
in shaping touch patterns and design’s name. 
SYMBIONT references the concept of symbiosis—a 
prolonged coexistence between organisms [70]—and 
aligns with the metaphor of commensalism, where one 
entity benefits without harming the other. In this sense, 
SYMBIONT serves as a physical medium that augments 
human-to-human communication through touch.
The following questions guide this research:
1. What design elements contribute to developing
soft robotic objects for affective interpersonal
communication?
2. How can iterative prototyping and interdisciplinary
methods, including Research through Design (RtD) and
Annotated Portfolio, contribute to developing object-
based tactile systems for affective interpersonal
communication?

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED STUDIES
2.1. Emotions through Haptics and Tactile Interaction
In human social communication and interaction, 
emotions play a crucial role [36], and touch alone, which 
is an essential component of the human haptic system, 
can effectively convey distinct emotions such as anger, 
fear, and disgust [28, 27, 17].
Substantial research has been conducted on how touch 
communicates affection. Because touch is our most 
emotional channel [59] and it plays an important role in 
communication [29], it is capable of conveying a wide 
range of emotions including love, gratitude, anger, and 
sympathy [53].
Many literature reviews focus on specific affective 
purposes, such as affective health or self-reporting of 
emotions [68]. Much of haptics and tactile platform 
research focuses on market-driven or academic 
research tailored for hapticians [56], which improves 
task efficiency [32], distinguishes shapes and textures 
[10], grasps virtual objects [2], augments auditory and 
visual feedback [58], and allows sensory substitution 
[66].
In contrast, this pictorial diverges from this line by 
exploring object-based tactile systems, where physical 
form, texture, motion, and metaphor contribute to a more 
open-ended, interpretive affective communication.
Haptic feedback is essential for mediating touch in the 
absence of physical proximity. Its emotional and sensory 
qualities contribute to creating experiences that deeply 
engage and captivate audiences [67]. 
Research on the emotional benefits of haptic 
technology—such as regulating heart rate with tactile 
biofeedback [8], breathing guidance device [3], tangible 
affective interaction for self-report of emotions [22], and 
interactive technologies for emotion regulation training 
[54]—demonstrate its potential to enhance emotional 
well-being. Expanding on these, this work explores 
object-based interpersonal communication through 
tactile interaction without physical interaction between 
users.
2.2. From Wearable to Object in Affective Communica-
tion
It has been widely explored how affective haptic 
communication can be achieved using wearable 
technologies focusing on modalities such as vibration 
[47], compression [21], warmth [44], and breathing 
rhythms [39] in order to simulate social touch. They 
are commonly embedded in garments [45], wristbands 
[25], and other wearables and accessories and serve 
as a means of enhancing emotional expression and 
presence.
By evoking specific sensory and affective responses, 
this rapid progress in haptic technology has been able to 
provide richer, more effective, and more organic tactile 
feedback [48]. Studies have shown that certain types of 
actuation can influence feelings of relaxation and safety, 
such as gentle pressure [6, 26, 35] or synchronized 
breathing movements [3, 4, 9] .
Although these affective systems have offered valuable 
insight into embodied emotion and tactile interaction 
(some related works mentioned earlier), they are 
most commonly realized through body-mounted 
configurations—numerous examples are detailed 
in [64]—especially in the context of interpersonal 
communication, emotional regulation, and remote 
presence.
However, the present research explores an object-
based soft robotic medium that aims to evoke affect 
through metaphor, dynamic motion, bioinspiration, and 
sensory design. This approach explores how tactile 
communication can emerge through interaction with a 
shared object—shifting the site of affective engagement 
from the body to a pair of objects and offering new 
possibilities for mediating interpersonal communication.

Soft robotics consists of compliant materials with 
elastic moduli similar to soft biological tissues [7, 
50], is defined by its morphology, material properties, 
control systems, and interactions with the environment 
[37]. This study uses soft robotics to explore tangible 
interfaces for human interaction, augmenting tactile 
communication through lifelike interaction.

2.3.1. Aesthetics: Biomorphic in Morphology
In interaction design, aesthetics aim to explain users’ 
aesthetic perception through feelings, cognitive 
processes, and physical design feature [40]. They often 
convey social and cultural meanings through materials 
and forms [51].
Devices influence human responses, as noted by 
Verbeek [65], with aesthetic interactions stimulating 
imagination and rethinking interactive systems to 
accomplish different goals [44, 42].
According to Wilson’s biophilia hypothesis [24], humans 
are inclined to focus on life and lifelike processes due to 
evolution, and it has also been shown that biomorphic 
design can positively influence health and well-being 
[33].
In the context of affective haptic devices, form 
factor and materiality contribute significantly to their 
effectiveness [64]. In addition to stimulus type (e.g., 
thermal, vibrotactile, or pressure), tactile sensations are 
also influenced by the device’s physical characteristics, 
such as texture, shape, and softness [62]. Furthermore, 
devices with human-like or organic forms, such as 
abstract hands or soft breathing robots, were perceived 
as more intuitive and natural to use [11].
In accordance with this notion, aesthetic qualities are 
strongly associated with the human experience and 
it profoundly influences our perceptions of trust [46]. 
According to the researchers [11], soft biomorphic 
prototypes are more appealing for emotional and 
physical health, balancing organic and geometric 
elements to meet specific needs.
2.3.2. Behavior: Biomimetic and Metaphor
In soft robotics, natural organisms’ adaptability and 
efficiency are utilized, using and mimicking their 
morphology and biomechanical characteristics to 
create new dynamic behaviors [38], aiming to enhance 
human-robot interaction [45]. Using such designs, 
robots can move, respond, and interact in more lifelike 
ways, enhancing the adaptability, robustness, and safety 
of robots designed for human interaction [38], fostering 
more user engagement.
In design metaphors, unfamiliar objects are associated 
with familiar objects with similar characteristics 
to explain complex ideas [13] concisely. Sensory 
experiences can metaphorically help us understand 
many abstract concepts [14], allowing us to interact 
effectively with an object [42]. Thus, by integrating 
sensory experiences into design, we can forge more 
profound and emotional connections between user and 
object [1], resulting in more immersive and emotional 
experiences. 
2.3.3. Sensory Design and Material Choice
In soft robotics, silicone is valued for its flexibility, 
stretchability, and lifelike properties, enabling tactile, 
sensory, and sensory interactions [5]. As a result of 
these qualities, robots can engage users emotionally 
and physically, fostering attachment and trust during 
interaction [30].
Silicone’s perceptibility aligns with Strawson’s 
interpretation of objects as tangible, sensory entities 
designed for human interaction [60]. Soft robots made 
from silicone are not abstract tools, but objects meant 
to be held, touched, and felt, facilitating rich, non-
verbal communication through their physical properties 
[50]. As Pallasmaa observed, touch unconsciously 
shapes our visual perception, with textures and forms 
determining how we connect with objects emotionally 
and physically [41]. 
In sensory design, tactile feedback transcends 
functionality [30], redefining how objects are experienced 
and interacted. Through touch, sound, and movement, 
sensory design can also facilitate accessibility and 
emotional connections [30]. With silicone’s sensory 
and material properties, this study aimed to create 
bioinspired soft tactile objects that provided a more 
sensory and emotional connection between the user 
and embedded circuit components.
3 METHODOLOGIES AND DESIGN PROCESS
Designers play a critical role in Human-Computer 
Interaction (HCI) research by addressing complex, 
under-constrained problems with unique 
perspectives [69]. This pictorial adopts a design-
oriented approach combining Research through 
Design (RtD) and Annotated Portfolio to support 
both practical exploration and interdisciplinary 
objectives [18]. These methods facilitate the 
development of prototypes as essential tools for 
refining design goals and evaluating functional and 
aesthetic outcomes.  
RtD emphasizes that uncertainty is not an obstacle, 
but as a generative resource for design exploration 
[16]. Hence, this work embraces ambiguity to 
investigate interaction possibilities through sensory 
exploration, iterative prototyping and metaphor. 
RtD was used to generate knowledge by testing 
interaction patterns inspired by human touch, 
metaphorical meaning, and biomimetic behavior. 
Rapid prototyping enabled subjective evaluation 
of components, forms, textures, sensors, and 
actuators, informing iterative refinements [61]. 
Annotated Portfolio documents the design process, 
inspirations, and iterative process in relation to 
research questions. This approach retained a close 
connection with the artifacts while addressing 
broader concerns within the objectives [23] to 
create a unique concept.
This approach is further demonstrated through 
iterative prototyping and the use of metaphor and 
bioinspiration to shape the design.

3.1. Research through Prototyping
Throughout this pictorial, prototyping serves as a mode of inquiry rather than merely a validation step within a 
Research through Design (RtD) approach. Iterative making and reflective refinement enable insight into form, 
movement, and interaction. Subjective decisions and contextual constraints shape each prototype during 
the prototyping process. The goal is to optimize functionality and explore further possibilities of soft robotic 
interactions to augment interpersonal communication and evoke emotion by emphasizing metaphor, tactility, 
and sensory feedback. Ambiguity and uncertainty guide decisions around materials, actuation, sensory systems, 
and interaction timing. In the evolution from HiPalm to EmoLink, OctoTouch, and finally, SYMBIONT, insights 
gained through embodied experimentation are gradually layered over time.
3.2. Metaphor and Bioinspiration
Each prototype integrates biomimetic behavior and biomorphic aesthetics not as surface decoration but as tools 
for affective engagement. Prior research shows that shape-changing interfaces [19] and lifelike movements [49] 
can facilitate emotional responses [20] and contribute to designs that feel agreeable and sensorially appealing 
[34]. These qualities also make soft robots with biomorphic traits more appropriate for human interaction [12]. 
While marine organisms are not directly related to communication, their ambiguous and lifelike qualities inspire 
forms and interactions that encourage users to assign emotional meaning, supporting more flexible, open-
ended human-robot interaction [11].

4.1. Prototype I: HiPalm
HiPalm served as a starting point 
for design exploration and iterative 
prototyping during this research. To 
understand pneumatic actuation, air 
control mechanisms, and the fabrication 
of inflatable structures, early prototyping 
employed open-source hardware 
platforms like the Programmable Air Kit 
[72] and Soft Robotics Toolkit [31, 72]. As 
exploration progressed, another open-
source pneumatic toolkit, FlowIO [73,
55], was used. FlowIO’s documentation
provided insight into scalable system
design, real-time actuation control, and
soft robotics.
Following that, a custom pneumatic 
system is designed to align with the 
research objectives. This was followed 
by incorporating a range of sensors 
and components to experiment with 
affective interpersonal communication 
on a low-fidelity and simplified level. 
This phase aims to develop a wearable 
device capable of augmenting affective 
interpersonal communication, even 
though it is not intended to enable full 
bidirectional communication. The design 
evolved gradually, starting with a more 
achievable and technically manageable 
prototype that laid the foundation 
for more complex systems later on, 
encompassing aesthetics, control logic, 
components, and interactions.
As a wearable soft robotic device, HiPalm simulates heartbeat-like pulsing when another hand is detected nearby through 
an embodied, rhythm-based tactile medium. This concept aims at amplifying subtle emotional cues by mimicking lifelike 
inflation patterns.
CONCEPT
FABRICATION AND AESTHETICS
The actuator is designed based on the pufferfish, 
which is characterized by its biomorphic form, 
which inflates upon external stimulus. Using 
a laser-cut acrylic mold, the top and bottom 
parts are assembled. Later, skin-safe silicone is 
used to cast the actuator, which is sealed with 
room-temperature-vulcanizing silicone (RTV) 
adhesive. It is placed on the palm to leverage the 
sensitivity of the glabrous skin and its symbolic 
association with gestures such as handshakes.
SYSTEM DESIGN
The design of HiPalm’s control logic is based 
on synchronizing the inflation and deflation 
cycles with the wearer’s heart rate. 
This device features three heart rate 
zones: Calm, Normal, and Elevated, each with 
specific inflation and deflation timings that 
correspond to the physiological rhythm of the 
human heart. The heart rate and time-of-flight 
(ToF) sensors provided real-time feedback, 
which was utilized to maintain control.

CONCEPT
EmoLink explores how tactile feedback 
can send and receive signals by leveraging 
the distributed nature of somatosensory 
receptors (table 1) across the body. In 
its original form, it was imagined as two 
separate parts that could be attached 
to different body areas for personal 
interaction. Later, however, the focus 
moved away from aesthetic refinement 
toward establishing a reliable circuit and 
optimizing its functionality.
EmoLink is developed using two silicone 
layers, each containing a variety of 
components arranged in modular 
layouts and tested for responsiveness 
and signal clarity. Differences in skin 
texture and thickness are also evaluated 
to assess sensor performance. In this 
step, sensory-actuator responses can be 
compared directly, and tactile mappings 
can be refined without excessive 
attention to aesthetics.

SYSTEM DESIGN
The system employs a feedback loop 
in which sensors detect tactile inputs, 
including pressure and touch, and 
actuators generate corresponding 
tactile outputs, including vibration, 
temperature, or the sensation of being 
touched. A single microcontroller and 
a single set of sensors and actuators 
enable more efficient prototyping, a 
better understanding of component 
functionality, and data transmission 
between them.

4.4. Findings from Initial Prototypes
HiPalm demonstrates the potential of a pneumatic
system for rhythmic, affective feedback in this 
research, but its actuation and control system 
proved bulky, noisy, and visually dominant. The 
next prototype is developed focusing on compact 
components that are flexible in form and behavior to 
reduce distraction and explore more subtle (in this 
case, life-like) interaction patterns.
Hand-Based Interaction
Through using the hand and being worn on the 
palm, stronger emotional symbolism and tactile 
sensitivity can be achieved, promoting embodied 
interaction.
System Complexity vs. Emotional Subtlety
The pneumatic actuation enables expressive 
pulsing patterns. However, the noise and 
bulk of the system distract from subtle 
emotional cues.
EmoLink explores a few tactile  modalities,
sensors, and  actuators, but  it lacks aesthetic 
integration, metaphorical expression, and a complete 
circuit with all components incorporated.
Component Evaluation through Low-Fidelity Testing
Through prototyping and testing, parameters such as tactile 
clarity and responsiveness are evaluated, which leads to the 
exclusion of some components (e.g., TRILL, heating pad).
Modality and Emotional Mapping
Emotional resonance is tested for pressure, 
vibration, and temperature.
OctoTouch creates a framework for
metaphorically driven tactile states that integrates 
sensory and communication logic, and explores how 
form, texture, and movement could be combined 
to create more lifelike and emotionally resonant 
interactions. However, aligning these interactions 
within a coherent system requires further 
refinement.
Metaphorical Touch Patterns
Vibration, crawling, and pulse are mapped to distinct 
emotional tones to provide a framework for metaphor-
driven interaction.
Balancing Realism and Responsiveness
SMA springs and flex sensors enable smooth, 
lifelike feedback while supporting responsive 
control. SMA also introduces subtle warmth 
into the interaction.
Embodied Aesthetics through 
Biomorphic Form
This prototype’s marine-inspired 
forms and silicone skins show 
how form, texture, and motion 
work together to create an 
engaging experience.

5.1. DESIGN PROCESS
The design process for SYMBIONT 
revolves around integrating sensory 
design, biomorphic aesthetics, and 
biomimetic behavior into a soft 
robotic object for affective tactile 
communication. Iterative prototyping 
refines SYMBIONT through multiple 
stages of technical exploration, realistic 
interaction mapping, and emotional 
resonance.
5.2. MATERIAL AND TACTILE AESTHETICS
The silicone used in SYMBIONT supports 
lifelike (soft) tactile interaction while 
protecting embedded electronics. The 
material is flexible while durable, allowing 
sensors and actuators to be integrated 
safely. In order to optimize sensory 
responsiveness and reinforce emotional 
resonance through tactile engagement, 
silicone skin has been tested in varying 
thicknesses and textures.
Marine life forms inspire SYMBIONT’s 
biomorphic design.  For example, 
creatures with long  tentacles guided 
the placement of flex sensors due 
to  their  length, while centralized 
bulges helped frame the core section 
in which the microcontroller was 
embedded. Based on these studies, 
structural layouts are developed that 
are both aesthetically and functionally 
aligned with the circuit structure.

6 CONTRIBUTIONS
This pictorial presents a research-through-design 
(RtD) process exploring how soft robotic objects 
can facilitate affective, tactile interpersonal 
communication. Through four iterative 
prototypes—HiPalm, EmoLink, OctoTouch, and 
SYMBIONT—the research investigates how 
metaphor, sensory  modalities,  and bioinspired 
design can be  involved  in designing objects 
for  mediated  tactile  interaction in shared or 
remote contexts.
The contribution lies in articulating a prototyping 
process that integrates haptic interaction 
mapping, component testing, metaphor, and 
bioinspired design. Rather than presenting a 
single outcome, each prototype builds toward 
SYMBIONT, which combines biomorphic 
aesthetics, biomimetic behavior, and 
metaphorical tactile expressions.
This approach offers practical insights into 
designing soft robotic systems that convey 
emotion through iterative, embodied design.
7 DISCUSSION AND 
FURTHER DEVELOPMENTS
The research demonstrates the application 
of iterative prototyping to developing soft 
robotic objects that facilitate affective 
tactile interpersonal communication. A 
variety of interaction modalities and system 
configurations were explored in each 
prototype, building toward technical and 
aesthetic refinements. While SYMBIONT 
presents a cohesive system, the broader 
process demonstrates how affective tactile 
interactions can emerge through exploration 
rather than predefined solutions.
Future work in the form of user studies to 
examine how metaphorical tactile cues 
are  perceived is also possible, as is  the 
potential for quieter, more adaptive actuations. 
This approach also suggests new opportunities 
for soft robotics to be applied to assistive 
technologies, media presence, and sensory 
augmentation.
