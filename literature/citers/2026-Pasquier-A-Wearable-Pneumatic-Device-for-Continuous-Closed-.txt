SUMMARY
We present a system of two wearable pneumatic haptic devices that supports continuous, closed-
loop, bidirectional tactile interaction at perceptually relevant force and temporal scales. A single
device can contain up to twelve pressure sensing channels connected to textile-based pneumatic
pouches. Each channel in a device can be used as a sensor, an actuator, or both. As an actuator
with integrated sensing, the channel generates stable skin indentation through local closed-loop
control. As a sensor, a channel can be mounted (or worn) on any surface, including on a robot
gripper or on the human body, and used to measure touch interactions with the environment
or a human user. A distributed architecture supports sustained pressure output, rapid dynamic
response, and wireless pairing of identical devices in a system to transmit and reproduce tactile
pressure signals in real time.
Device-level characterization demonstrates force bandwidth exceeding 30 Hz, rapid and well-
damped step responses, and extended pressure retention compared to prior compact pneumatic
platforms. Human studies show that pressure-based fingertip feedback enables discrimination
of force and stiffness, improves teleoperated manipulation by reducing applied pressures by up
to 23.1% and task duration by up to 27.4%, and lowers subjective mental workload by 18.8%,
particularly under visually constrained conditions. By unifying tactile sensing and haptic feedback
within a single pneumatic modality, the device provides a practical foundation for bidirectional
touch interaction in teleoperation.

Soft Robotics, Haptics, Teleoperation
INTRODUCTION
The human sense of touch is inherently bidirectional: we respond physically to what we feel in
real time. Applying this two-way exchange to teleoperation improves performance on several
levels: haptic feedback lowers task completion time 1,2, improves manipulation accuracy 3, and
decreases both peak and average forces applied during interaction 4. Y et in practice the two
directions are engineered separately: haptic displays are designed for humans and tactile sen-
sors are developed for robots, and the mismatch between what the robot records and what the
operator feels limits both2. Here, we present a wearable pneumatic device in which sensing and
display use the same mechanism: a textile pouch whose internal pressure is simultaneously the
sensed and the actuated variable. Paired wirelessly, two such devices reproduce in real time
the pressure sensed at each gripper finger on the corresponding fingertip of the robot operator
(Fig. 1).

As teleoperation is increasingly used not only for control but also as a source of demonstra-
tions for imitation learning, continuous, interpretable, bidirectional tactile feedback has become
essential. To understand why it has remained rare requires examining both ends of the connec-
tion: how touch is displayed at the hand, and how it is sensed at the robot.
On the operator, haptic feedback is conventionally divided into two types: kinesthetic and cu-
taneous. Kinesthetic feedback conveys force to the muscles and joints, typically through world-
grounded interfaces. For teleoperation, this relies on bilateral position-exchange control or on
expensive, fragile force sensors 3, and bilateral control loops are destabilized by hard contacts,
stiff settings, and communication latency 5,6; stability is recovered only by scaling down force,
which sacrifices the transparency ideally provided by kinesthetic feedback 7. In contrast, cuta-
neous feedback is delivered directly to the skin and underlying tissues. Cutaneous stimulation
at the fingerpad can be ungrounded, such that forces are reacted by the user’s own finger. Un-
grounded, wearable cutaneous feedback for teleoperation has two advantages: the device is
wearable and free of workspace constraints, and keeps the teleoperation loop intrinsically sta-
ble even under hard contacts or communication delay 8,9. How that cutaneous information is
delivered then determines its interpretability. The dominant approach,sensory substitution, en-
codes the missing force into an unrelated channel such as abstract vibration, an auditory beep,
or brightness 10. Because the cue bears no natural relationship to the interaction, the human
operator must learn the mapping, and such cues poorly encode continuous quantities like force
magnitude or compliance 11,12, increasing cognitive load 13. Moreover, when teleoperation with
sensory substitution is used to collect data for imitation and diffusion learning, the training data
is noisy and degrades learned policies2,14. For the low-force interactions characteristic of dexter-
ous manipulation, local skin deformation feedback is better suited because it provides cutaneous
stimulation akin to real-world contact15,16, rather than a cue the operator must decode.
2
At the robot, tactile sensing measures the state of contact, potentially including contact geom-
etry, force, texture, and slip17, most commonly through vision-based sensors that image the de-
formation of an illuminated elastomer 18–20 or through dense electronic arrays 21–24. The defining
tension is a reciprocal one between spatial resolution and speed: denser sensing captures finer
contact geometry but produces high-bandwidth data streams that impose substantial sensing,
transmission, and processing overhead, while faster sampling can be achieved only by coars-
ening spatial resolution 25–27. In practice, the tactile stream is distilled during teleoperation to a
single contact scalar, typically normal force amplitude or incipient slip 28–31, and mapped to fin-
gertip vibration, or preserved spatially and mapped to a tactile array 32, vibration across multiple
fingers33, or directional rotation of a vibration motor 34. In every case, the robot records one
signal modality and the operator feels another.
Pneumatics is unusual in being able to serve both ends at once. The same inflatable tex-
tile pouch that displays normal force through direct skin indentation 10,35,36 can measure it, be-
cause the pressure inside the pouch is simultaneously the actuation variable and the sensed
one (Fig. 1A and B). Beyond conveying normal force, an inflated contact reproduces the nat-
ural growth of contact area with force that the fingerpad experiences against a real compliant
object (Fig. 1D), long understood as a primary cue for softness discrimination 37. What has
stood between pneumatics and this promise is control: existing pneumatic controllers are bulky,
due to external compressors and valves 38,39, confining them to open-loop control in untethered
settings12,40,41, while the compressibility of air makes stable, precise control difficult without feed-
back42. This absence of closed-loop control is a significant hurdle, since stable, sustained feed-
back is essential both for realistic perception and for reliable data collection.
Our device overcomes this hurdle with a compact, closed-loop controller that removes the
compressor and valve burden that has historically tethered pneumatic devices, regulating each
pouch locally so that pressure cues remain stable over extended interaction. In the twin architec-
ture introduced above, sensed pressure is transmitted wirelessly with latency below the threshold
at which tactile feedback is perceived as immediate, without intermediate modality change. We
validate the design through device-level characterization and user studies, demonstrating reliable
discrimination of transmitted force, improved teleoperation performance, and reduced subjective
workload, establishing a practical foundation for bidirectional tactile communication that supports
both human-in-the-loop control and data collection for learning-based robotic systems.
RESULTS
The device is a wearable, untethered pneumatic system in which the same textile pouch can
serve as a sensor or an actuator (Fig. 1A and B). Three choices distinguish it from prior com-
pact pneumatic haptic devices. Each pouch is paired with a proportional piezoelectric valve, a
pressure sensor, and a dedicated microcontroller, so pressure is regulated in a local closed loop
rather than by centralized open-loop solenoid switching (Table S2). The valve and air supply are
integrated on the body of the device, removing the compressor and tether that have confined
closed-loop pneumatic haptics to benchtop use. And because sensing and display share one
physical modality, two devices can be paired so that pressure measured on one is reproduced
on the other with no intermediate encoding other than amplification (Fig. 1C).
We evaluate these choices at three levels. At the device level, the device holds regulated
pressure for 30 min without a supply, settles to a new setpoint in 39 ms, delivers a -3 dB force
response to 34.2 Hz and a peak force of 76.2 N, and mirrors pressure between paired devices in
64 ms. At the perceptual level, participants discriminated force and stiffness through the device
as accurately as by hand, while weight discrimination degraded once three levels had to be
ordered under teleoperated control. In teleoperated pick-and-place, feedback reduced applied
3
pressure by up to 23.1%, task duration by up to 27.4%, and mental demand by 18.1%, with the
largest gains under visual occlusion.
Device airtightness was evaluated through pressure retention tests in which the external pres-
sure supply was disconnected after full inflation. Across n = 5 trials, the device retained pressure
for extended durations, with internal pressure decreasing from 2650 mbar to 2300 mbar after 5
min and remaining above 1700 mbar after 16 min before fully depressurizing after 30 min. In
contrast, comparable compact pneumatic systems exhibited rapid pressure loss within seconds
under identical conditions.
Operating at perceptually relevant force and temporal scales
To assess whether the device operates at force and temporal scales relevant for tactile percep-
tion, we characterize its flow rate, force bandwidth, and maximum output force. These metrics
determine the device’s ability to generate perceptually meaningful, time-varying normal forces at
the fingertip.
Flow rate
Inlet and outlet flow rates were estimated by recording the pressure change in a fixed volume
chamber while running the device withP inlet = 1.4bar and venting toP outlet =−0.8bar, with the
inlet and outlet valves respectively fully open, until the pressure reached a steady state. Wall
air and vacuum were used in this experiment, though alternative air sources would yield similar
results for the given inlet and outlet pressures. The detailed experimental procedure is provided
in the Supplemental Information. A schematic of the system architecture is shown in Fig. 5.
The average inlet flow rate was 10.8 L/min, and the average outlet flow rate was 13.03 L/min,
four to five times higher than other reported pneumatic controller flow rates (see Table S2). Given
that the internal volume of the textile sensors and actuators used in this work is approximately
4·10 −3 L, these flow rates support rapid pressurization and depressurization, enabling high-
frequency closed-loop control.
Bandwidth
The force bandwidth of the textile actuator was characterized by measuring its frequency-depen-
dent force response under blocked conditions. For n = 3 actuators, the device maintained a -3
dB force response up to 34.2±1.2 Hz. This bandwidth exceeds that of many other untethered
pneumatic haptic systems, which exhibit force roll-off below 15 Hz 35,43 (see SI Table S2); for a
broader survey of soft actuation for wearable haptics, see44,45.
From a perceptual standpoint, this bandwidth spans the range relevant for conveying dynamic
tactile cues such as pulsing and force modulation, especially in the low-frequency regimes where
conventional vibration-based approaches struggle to deliver strong forces. Human sensitivity to
normal indentation and low-frequency force changes primarily involves the Meissner corpuscles
and ranges from approximately 5 Hz to 50 Hz 46,47, making the measured response suitable for
pressure-based tactile feedback.
Force
Maximum actuator force was evaluated under blocked conditions at an input pressure of 2650
mbar. Across tested actuators, the device produced an average peak force of 76.2 ± 2.8 N.
This force range exceeds the minimum thresholds required for reliable normal-force perception

Because force output is regulated via closed-loop pressure control, the device enables con-
tinuous modulation of contact force rather than binary actuation. Prior work in haptics has shown
that cutaneous force and skin deformation feedback can convey graded magnitude information
such as stiffness and friction, whereas vibrotactile feedback is most commonly used to encode
events or temporal patterns and has limited ability to represent force magnitude 47. Prior pneu-
matic haptic systems have predominantly relied on open-loop control, which limits feedback to
transient pressure or force pulses rather than the sustained and continuously regulated contact
forces required to replicate touch feedback during teleoperation35,36.
System responsiveness for bidirectional tactile communication
To evaluate the suitability of the system for bidirectional tactile communication, we characterized
both its local pressure response dynamics and its end-to-end latency.
System responsiveness was first assessed by measuring the intrinsic step response of the
controller, isolating local actuation dynamics from communication effects. When subjected to a
step input from a 1050 mbar baseline to 2000 mbar, the controller exhibited a rapid and well
damped response, with an average 10–90% rise time of 29 ms and a settling time of 39 ms (see
Fig. S4).
End to end latency was then evaluated between two wirelessly connected devices by measur-
ing the delay between a pressure event sensed on one device and the corresponding actuation
5
response on a second device. Across repeated trials (n= 10), the average sensing to actuation
latency was 64 ms (Fig. 2), with the dominant contribution arising from wireless communication
via the host computer.
Taken together, these measurements place overall system responsiveness well below com-
monly cited perceptual thresholds for tactile feedback to be perceived as immediate (80–100
ms)49. This performance supports real time bilateral and cooperative haptic interactions between
distributed devices, including mirrored feedback, shared tactile experiences, and teleoperation
scenarios requiring tight temporal coupling.
Haptic-enabled discrimination of force, stiffness, and weight in manual and
mediated conditions.
To evaluate the interpretability of the haptic feedback provided by the device, we conducted a
series of perceptual discrimination tasks probing force, stiffness, and weight withn= 25partici-
pants. Participants were recruited through university email lists and Slack channels. Participants
provided informed consent, and the protocol received approval from the Stanford University In-
stitutional Review board (#65022). These tasks were designed to progressively increase in com-
plexity, moving from direct haptic feedback alone to mediated interaction through teleoperation.
Teleoperation was performed using a Kinova Gen3 7 DoF arm and a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper with
a textile actuator on the inside of each finger for sensing, and a corresponding textile actuator
on the index and thumb pads of the participants for haptic feedback. The robot position was
controlled using wrist and finger (thumb and index) tracking on the right or left arm through a
Microsoft HoloLens 2 worn by the participant (see Methods section and Fig. 6 for more detail).
The results for the five tasks are illustrated with confusion matrices in Fig. 3.
Force
We first assessed force discrimination using the haptic device alone, without involvement of
the robotic manipulator. Participants were asked to discriminate between discrete force levels
delivered directly to the index and thumb fingertips via the haptic device. The three levels of
force were 150, 300, and 450 mbar.
Participants demonstrated consistently high accuracy in this task. In the 2-element force
discrimination condition (Fig. 3A1), participants correctly identified whether the second force was
stronger, weaker, or equal to the first with an accuracy of 92.2%. Performance remained stable
when the task was extended to 3-element comparisons, with an accuracy of 92.1% (Fig. 3A1).
These results indicate that the pressure-based fingertip feedback enabled reliable perception of
force magnitude even as comparison complexity increased.
Stiffness
We next evaluated stiffness discrimination under two conditions: direct manual interaction with
three soft cubes, and haptic feedback mediated by the robotic gripper. The three cube stiffnesses
were 0.630, 1.77, and 3.35 N/mm. In the mediated condition, the gripper closed onto each cube
with a fixed closing motion while participants faced away from the setup to prevent visual cues,
and the gripper sensors drove the fingertip pouches directly.
Participants performed comparably in the two conditions (Fig. 3B1 and B2). In the 2-element
task, accuracy was 90.1% by hand and 91.4% through the device. In the 3-element task, it was
96.7% by hand and 90.8% through the device, a difference of 5.9 percentage points. That me-
diated feedback matched direct touch on the 2-element task and came within six points on the

Weight
Finally, we examined weight discrimination, again comparing direct manual interaction to haptic
feedback mediated through teleoperation. The three weights were rigid blocks of 150, 300, and
450 g. In the teleoperated condition, participants actively controlled robot position and gripper
opening by moving their right wrist and their index finger and thumb, respectively, and were asked
to pick up each weight with the gripper and judge it from the haptic feedback at their fingertips.
Weight discrimination diverged sharply between the two conditions (Fig. 3C1 and C2). In the
2-element task, accuracy was 95.7% by hand and 87.5% through teleoperation, a difference of
8.2 percentage points. In the 3-element task, it was 100% by hand and 53.8% through teleop-
eration. Mediated feedback therefore tracked direct lifting closely when only two weights had to
be ordered, but lost most of that performance once a third was added. The added cognitive load
and motor complexity of the weight discrimination task appeared to challenge participants’ abil-
ity to reliably encode and recall the associated haptic sensations across multiple comparisons,
unlike the force and stiffness discrimination tasks.
The order in which force, stiffness, and weight were displayed in the 2- and 3-element condi-
tions was randomly generated in a test protocol sheet for each participant.
Improved teleoperation performance with reduced mental load
We next evaluated whether the haptic feedback provided by the device translated into measur-
able improvements during teleoperated manipulation tasks. Participants performed three pick-
and-place (PnP) tasks under visual-only and haptic feedback conditions: PnP1 in which they
had direct view of the task, PnP2 in which they had an obstructed view of the task, and PnP3 in
which they viewed only the camera feed from the robot (see Fig. 4E). For all tasks, participants
were asked to pick up a hackey sack from a starting position and place it at another position
marked by tape. Participants were encouraged during practice trials prior to PnP1 to use the
minimum grip force required to reliably grip the object. Within each task, the order of haptics
vs no-haptics conditions were pseudo-randomized for each participant. The quantitative results
are shown in Fig. 4A, B, and C respectively in the form of violin plots. Teleoperation results are
shown forn= 19participants due to exclusions stemming from device malfunctions during the
study.
Across all three tasks, participants consistently applied less pressure when fingertip haptic
feedback was available. In PnP1, average applied pressure decreased by 19.2% relative to the
visual only condition. In PnP2, pressure decreased by 23.1%, and in PnP3 by 7.2%. These re-
ductions indicate that haptic feedback enabled gentler interaction with the delicate object across
task conditions, even when visual information was present.
Task efficiency also improved under haptic feedback in two of the three conditions. Par-
ticipants completed PnP1 and PnP3 21.8% and 25.6% faster, respectively, while following tra-
jectories that were 14.6% and 28.9% shorter. Speed improvements in PnP1 and PnP3 were
attributed to faster object transport, whereas speed of initiating a grasp remained unchanged
(see Fig. S8 for PnP time allocation by condition). In contrast, overall improvements in PnP2
were minimal, with path length and average completion time remaining largely unchanged. In
PnP2, transport performance gains were washed out by slower grasp initiation. Despite being
rated by participants as the most challenging task, performance gains in PnP2 may have been
limited by increased familiarity with the teleoperation interface by this stage of the study.

Subjective workload assessments further reflected the impact of haptic feedback. After the
pick-and-place tasks in each condition, participants also completed the NASA Task Load Index
(TLX), which rates perceived workload across six subscales from 0 (none) to 100 (extreme).
As shown in Fig. 4D, TLX scores decreased across four of the six subscales when haptic feed-
back was provided, including Mental Demand, Temporal Demand, Effort, and Frustration. Mean
Mental Demand decreased from 47.6 to 39.0 (-8.7 points, 18.1%), and Frustration decreased
from 52.9 to 45.3 (-7.6 points, 14.4%), the latter being statistically significant. Reductions in

Because perceived workload is often correlated with task duration, we additionally highlight
the three participants with the longest task completion times; shown in bold in Fig. 4D (additional
individual participant level results can be found in Figs. S5 to S7). For these participants, reduc-
tions in subjective workload under the haptic condition were more pronounced than the group
average: Mean Mental Demand decreased from 63.3 to 41.7 (-21.7 points, 34.3%), Performance
improved from 58.3 to 36.7 (-21.7 points, 37.2%), and Frustration decreased from 61.7 to 43.3
(-18.3 points, 29.7%). These results suggest that haptic feedback is particularly beneficial for
longer or more complex tasks.
After completing the pick-and-place tasks in each condition, participants rated the system us-
ing the standardized System Usability Scale (SUS), a ten-item questionnaire that yields a single
usability score from 0 to 100. Their responses suggested improved perceived usability when
haptic feedback was provided. Mean SUS scores increased from 61.1±14.6 in the No Haptics
condition to 64.9±16.3 in the Haptics condition, corresponding to a 3.8-point improvement. Al-
though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p= 0.090, d z =−0.41), the observed
trend indicates that haptic feedback may enhance users’ overall perception of system usability.
In open-ended comments, several participants also reported perceiving delay during operation,
attributing latency of the robotic arm to the haptic feedback.

Discussion
The central contribution of this work is a design in which tactile sensing and haptic display use
the same physical mechanism, regulated in closed loop and carried entirely on the body. Prior
wearable pneumatic devices have had to choose between these properties: those achieving sta-
ble, sustained pressure rely on external compressors and valve banks that confine them to fixed
setups, while untethered designs rely on open-loop solenoid actuation, which limits feedback
to transient pulses rather than the regulated, continuously varying contact forces teleoperation
requires. Distributing a proportional valve, pressure sensor, and microcontroller to each channel
resolves that tradeoff: regulation happens locally, so the device scales across channels without
a central bottleneck and stays light enough to wear. And because the sensing and actuation use
the same modality, no information is lost between the robot and the operator.
The device characterization validates this design. Closed-loop pressure regulation, high flow
capacity, and a rapid, well-damped step response enable stable, continuous fingertip indenta-
tion at perceptually relevant force and temporal scales. Unlike many prior pneumatic haptic
systems that rely on open-loop or transient actuation, our device maintains regulated pressure
over extended periods without rapid decay. The distributed control architecture allows individ-
ual channels to be regulated locally while remaining synchronized across the device, supporting
low-latency response and sustained interaction. These characteristics are critical for tasks in-
volving prolonged contact and fine force regulation, particularly in teleoperation settings where
10
force cues must remain stable and consistent over time.
Across force and stiffness discrimination tasks, participants demonstrated high accuracy
when using the haptic feedback device. Stiffness discrimination performance in particular was
close to that of direct manual interaction, indicating that the device preserves compliance infor-
mation with high fidelity. These findings support the use of indentation-based cues for conveying
continuous contact information, such as force magnitude and material properties, and suggest
that such cues can be interpreted reliably without extensive training. In contrast to vibration-
based feedback, pressure-based indentation provides a direct mechanical mapping to contact
forces, which likely contributes to its perceptual robustness and interpretability.
Weight discrimination revealed a more nuanced picture. While participants were able to dis-
criminate object weight with near-perfect accuracy during direct manual interaction, performance
declined in the harder teleoperated condition, particularly for 3-element comparisons. This re-
duction highlights the role of task-level cognitive demands in shaping perceptual performance.
In the teleoperated setting, participants were required to integrate fingertip pressure cues with
simultaneous control of arm motion and grip force, increasing memory and coordination require-
ments. These results emphasize that perceptual accuracy depends not only on the fidelity of the
pressure signal, but also on how directly that signal corresponds to the property being judged
while the participant is simultaneously controlling the robot.
The twin-system configuration further extends the capabilities of the device by enabling bidi-
rectional tactile communication. By pairing two identical devices, sensed pressure signals can
be transmitted and reproduced in real time without intermediate abstraction. Beyond enabling
haptic mirroring, this architecture allows tactile interaction data to be recorded using the same
sensing modality under both haptic and non-haptic conditions. In the present study, this made
it possible to capture synchronized tactile pressure data during teleoperated manipulation re-
gardless of feedback condition. Such paired datasets provide a foundation for treating tactile
pressure as a first-class modality in learning from demonstration, where policies can be trained
to incorporate contact information alongside vision and proprioception.
When deployed in teleoperated manipulation tasks as a validation context, the device led
to consistent reductions in applied force and improvements in task efficiency, particularly under
visually constrained conditions. Participants also reported lower mental demand and frustration
when haptic feedback was available, indicating that tactile cues reduced the cognitive effort
required to infer contact state and regulate interaction forces. Consistent with the framing in
the introduction, teleoperation is not presented here as the primary application of the device.
Rather, it serves as a demonstrative use case illustrating how the device facilitates perceptual
interpretability and bidirectional feedback within a human-robot interaction context.
Prior work has shown that haptic feedback can reduce average task completion time and
substantially increase the number of usable demonstrations collected during teleoperation. In
particular, vibrotactile feedback has been reported to improve efficiency in aggregate, although
these time advantages largely diminish when analysis is restricted to curated successful demon-
strations2. In contrast, our results show that pressure-based fingertip feedback yields persistent
improvements in task efficiency and force regulation even under visually constrained conditions,
alongside significant reductions in subjective mental demand. This suggests that closed-loop,
indentation-based feedback may not only increase data yield, but also improve the quality and
interpretability of individual demonstrations.
This study has several limitations. The experimental setup relied on multiple subsystems
for robot control, visual feedback, and haptic feedback, which occasionally resulted in technical
interruptions and participant exclusions. Some participants also conflated latency in the robotic
system with latency in the haptic device, underscoring the need for tighter system integration
in future studies. From a hardware perspective, further reductions in size, improvements in
force resolution, and continued refinement of control strategies remain important directions for
11
development.
Overall, this work demonstrates that closed-loop, pressure-based fingertip haptic feedback
can support reliable tactile discrimination while reducing cognitive load during interaction. By
unifying tactile sensing and haptic feedback within a single pneumatic platform and enabling
bidirectional tactile communication, the device provides a practical foundation for future research
in tactile interaction, teleoperation, and data-driven learning from touch.

Hardware Architecture
The pneumatic controller was designed as a compact, untethered system capable of closed-loop
pressure regulation for multiple soft actuators. As shown in Fig. 5, each actuator channel con-
sists of a proportional piezoelectric valve, an pressure sensor, and a dedicated microcontroller,
forming a local actuator–sensor–controller unit. This distributed architecture allows each chan-
nel to regulate pressure independently while remaining synchronized with the rest of the device.
The base weight of the device is 204 g, with an additional 52g per channel (so for a two-channel
device, 308 g total). For the study, we use two paired devices: one connected to the robot, and
one to the human operator.
Compressed air is supplied either by an external source or an onboard reservoir and routed
through the piezoelectric valves to the textile actuators. Pressure sensors are placed proximal

to each actuator to enable accurate local feedback. The controller electronics are housed in
a lightweight enclosure and include power regulation, wireless communication hardware, and
safety circuitry. The device was designed to minimize pneumatic dead volume and leakage in
order to support sustained pressure output during prolonged interaction.
Software Architecture
Closed-loop pressure control is implemented locally on each microcontroller using proportional-
integral control. Pressure sensor readings are sampled continuously and used to regulate valve
opening in real time, allowing each channel to track desired pressure setpoints with low latency
and minimal overshoot. Control parameters are identical across channels, enabling scalable
operation with multiple actuators.
A central communication layer coordinates setpoint updates and data logging across chan-
nels. Wireless communication is used to transmit pressure commands and sensor data between
paired devices in the twin-system configuration. This architecture supports both local pressure
regulation and bidirectional tactile communication while avoiding centralized control bottlenecks.
All firmware was developed to prioritize deterministic timing and robustness under continuous
operation.
Teleoperation System Overview
Haptic Feedback System
Haptic feedback was delivered to the user through textile-based pneumatic actuators mounted on
the thumb and index fingertips, replicating pressure variations corresponding to contact forces on
either side of the parallel-jaw gripper (see Fig. 6). The actuators were independently regulated
by the closed-loop pneumatic controller described above, allowing pressure to be modulated
13
continuously during interaction. Actuators were positioned such that applied pressure produced
normal indentation at the fingertip pad, directly conveying contact force information. The ac-
tuators were fabricated from two layers of 70 Denier TPU coated Ripstop nylon, heat-pressed
using a stencil, and a barb was then inserted and secured with glue. The detailed manufacturing
method is reported by Coram et al. 50.
During teleoperation, pressure commands for the haptic actuators were generated from force
measurements acquired at the gripper. Normal forces measured at the gripper were mapped
to actuator pressure setpoints using a linear scaling factor of four, determined empirically to
ensure perceptible yet comfortable indentation. Pressure setpoints were updated continuously
and tracked locally by the controller, enabling sustained force feedback without perceptible drift
or decay.
Robot Control
Participants controlled the Kinova Gen3 7 DoF arm using direct hand tracking through a Microsoft
HoloLens 2, without holding or manipulating any physical input devices. This design kept the
hands and fingers fully unencumbered, allowing haptic feedback to be delivered directly to the
fingertips. Wrist motion was mapped to Cartesian velocity commands, while thumb-index finger
distance controlled the opening of the parallel-jaw gripper. This approach enabled continuous,
intuitive control of both arm motion and grasp without introducing additional physical interfaces
that could interfere with tactile perception. HoloLens 2 hand tracking has been independently
characterized against a Vicon motion capture reference, with mean fingertip position errors of
2 to 4 mm and thumb-index pinch span errors below 1 mm 51, sufficient for the velocity-based
control mapping used here. The Gen3 has a manufacturer-specified position repeatability of 1.0
mm (2σ) in its 7 DoF configuration, valid for calibrated robots in a fixed-base installation such as
the one used here52. Full implementation details are provided in the Supplemental Information.
Hypotheses
Based on the design and capabilities of the device, we tested the following hypotheses:
1. The pneumatic fingertip haptic feedback system enables discrimination of force and object
properties, including stiffness and weight.
2. During teleoperation, the inclusion of haptic feedback reduces the forces transmitted to the
manipulated object.
3. During teleoperation, haptic feedback improves task performance, reflected in reduced
completion time and increased efficiency.
4. Haptic feedback lowers cognitive load during teleoperation.
5. The use of textile-based sensors supports comfort and overall usability.
User Study Procedure
Participants were recruited to evaluate the system under conditions with and without haptic feed-
back. The study protocol was approved by the Stanford Institutional Review Board (Protocol
65022). Twenty-five able-bodied participants completed the study (10 female, 14 male, 1 non-
binary), ranging in age from 19 to 67 years (M = 31, SD = 11.7). Twenty-two participants were
14
right-handed and three were left-handed. All participants provided informed consent prior to par-
ticipation. The study lasted approximately 1.5 hours per participant and consisted of two phases:
Perception and Teleoperation.
Perception Tasks
The perception phase consisted of five tasks designed to assess participants’ ability to interpret
fingertip haptic feedback across increasing levels of complexity. Tasks progressed from direct
haptic stimulation alone to robot-mediated interaction and teleoperation. The five tasks were:
1.Direct force discrimination: Participants discriminated between discrete levels of fingertip
pressure delivered directly by the haptic device, without involvement of the robotic manip-
ulator.
2.Direct stiffness discrimination: Participants manually squeezed physical objects of dif-
fering stiffness to establish a baseline for human stiffness perception.
3.Haptic-mediated stiffness discrimination: The robotic gripper interacted with objects of
differing stiffness using a fixed grasp motion, and the resulting contact forces were con-
veyed to participants via fingertip haptic feedback.
4.Direct weight discrimination: Participants lifted visually identical objects of different mass-
es by hand to establish a baseline for weight perception.
5.Teleoperated weight discrimination: Participants controlled the robotic arm and gripper
to lift objects of different masses, relying on fingertip haptic feedback to discriminate weight
during teleoperation.
Across all five tasks, participants were seated to eliminate whole-body motion cues, and
visual information was restricted as appropriate to isolate haptic perception. Full task parameters
and trial structures are provided in the Supplemental Information.
Teleoperation Training
Before formal evaluation, participants completed a five-minute training session to familiarize
themselves with the teleoperation interface and haptic feedback. The training duration was se-
lected in accordance with the researchers’ domain expertise and established practices from prior
teleoperation studies. During training, participants practiced manipulating an empty aluminum
soda can, a deliberately fragile object that encouraged careful force regulation and precise mo-
tion. Participants were instructed to move the can around the workspace without deforming it.
Half of the training period was performed with haptic feedback enabled and half without haptic
feedback, with the order randomized across participants.
Teleoperation Evaluation Tasks
The teleoperation phase evaluated whether fingertip haptic feedback improved manipulation per-
formance, force regulation, and perceived workload under varying visual conditions. Participants
completed a series of pick-and-place tasks of increasing visual difficulty while controlling the
robotic arm and gripper. In all tasks, participants were instructed to pick up a soft object from a
fixed starting location and place it at a fixed goal location marked with tape on the table.
15
A hackey sack (round, sand-filled cloth bag) was selected as the target object because its
compliant structure makes improper grasping difficult to assess visually, while remaining suffi-
ciently stiff to generate reliable force signals within the sensitivity range of the pneumatic tactile
sensors. This choice ensured that successful manipulation depended primarily on force regula-
tion rather than visual deformation cues.
The three teleoperation tasks were:
1.Pick-and-place with direct view (PnP1): Participants performed the task while standing
behind the robot with an unobstructed view of the workspace, gripper, and object.
2.Pick-and-place with obstructed view (PnP2): Participants performed the same task
while standing farther from the robot, where portions of the workspace were intermittently
occluded by the robot itself.
3.Pick-and-place with robot-mounted camera view (PnP3): Participants relied solely on a
wrist-mounted camera feed displayed on a monitor, with no direct line of sight to the robot
or object.
As shown in Fig 4E, each task was performed under both visual-only and haptic-feedback
conditions in a counterbalanced order. PnP1 included a larger number of repetitions to capture
steady-state performance, while PnP2 and PnP3 emphasized increased visual uncertainty. The
tasks were intentionally simple to isolate the effects of haptic feedback on applied force, task
efficiency, and perceived workload rather than on task planning complexity. Detailed trial counts,
training procedures, and performance metrics are provided in the Supplemental Information.
Metrics
Both quantitative and qualitative metrics were collected. Quantitative measures included fin-
gertip pressure signals, task completion time, and gripper path length. Qualitative measures
included the System Usability Scale (SUS) and the NASA Task Load Index (TLX), which as-
sessed perceived usability and workload, respectively. Detailed definitions of all metrics and
data processing procedures are provided in the Supplemental Information.
Statistical Analyses
Statistical analyses were performed in Python using the SciPy statistics package. Paired t-tests
were used to compare conditions with and without haptic feedback for all measured variables.
Trials with missing data due to logging errors were excluded symmetrically across conditions.

Each device is organized as a set of up to twelve identical actuator channels coordinated by a
system microcontroller. Each channel comprises a proportional piezo valve, a pressure sensor, and
a channel microcontroller that runs local closed loop pressure control (performing proportional-
integral control in software). Two identical devices can be paired so that one device senses pressure
signals and transmits them wirelessly to the other device, which reproduces the received pressure
signals in real time.
Electronics and sensingEach channel uses a Nordic Semiconductor nRF5340 microcontroller
and a TE Connectivity pressure sensor (MS583702BA). The pneumatic control valves used in this
study are Festo VEMP piezo proportional valves, which support continuous actuation over the full
pressure range (0–1.7 bar) and nominal flow rates up to 28 L/min. These valves use integrated
piezo actuators for proportional control, enabling graded pressure modulation rather than simple
digital switching [1]. The schematic architecture includes a Leader PCB and a Control PCB. The
device uses BLE for wireless communication. The architecture includes DC to DC converters and
two DAC outputs per channel. The DAC outputs provide 0 to 10 V signals that drive a high voltage
stage (0 to 310 V) used for piezo actuation. The valve and pneumatic pathway are rated for 0 to
2.7 bar.
Power and railsThe schematic indicates 24 V and 3.3 V system rails, plus intermediate rails
(12 V and 16 V) used for analog and high voltage generation. The DAC and sensor communicate
over I2C. The device is powered by a OVONIC 130C 6S 1100mah 22.2 V Lipo Battery.
PneumaticsCompressed air is supplied either by wall air or by an onboard CO2 cartridge reg-
ulator. In the airtightness protocol, the inlet port of the VEMP valve is fully opened to allow
1
unrestricted airflow from the CO2 cartridge regulator to the actuators, then the regulator is closed
to isolate the device and prevent additional air intake. We use 2 mm ID, 4 mm OD silicone tubing
and plastic barbed fitting connectors.
1.2 Local closed loop control and software architecture
Each channel microcontroller runs closed loop pressure control using the collocated pressure sensor.
This distributed control approach avoids centralized control bottlenecks and supports scaling to
multiple channels.
Controller implementationThe closed-loop pressure controller runs on the nRF5340 micro-
controller of each Control PCB under the Zephyr RTOS. The pressure sensor thread samples at
approximately 500Hz (1ms sleep interval plus two 500µs ADC conversions per reading). The con-
trol loop runs free-running with no fixed period; elapsed timedtis computed dynamically from the
Zephyr system clock (k_uptime_get()), and the effective control update rate is bounded in practice
by the sensor update rate.
Feedback is computed from a rolling mean of the 8 most recent pressure readings, which attenu-
ates high-frequency sensor noise. The error signal is defined asϵ(t) =P0(t)−Prolling window(t), where
P0(t)is the pressure setpoint provided by the Leader PCB. A symmetric deadband of±2mbar sup-
presses actuation for small errors; when|ϵ(t)|<2mbar the controller output is forced to zero and
no DAC write is issued.
Due to the asymmetric dynamic response of the inlet and exhaust piezo valve channels, separate
controllers are tuned for each path. Whenϵ(t)≥2mbar, a PI controller (Kp = 0.6,K i = 0.25,
Kd = 0) drives the inlet DAC; whenϵ(t)≤ −2mbar, a PID controller (Kp = 0.4,K i = 0.5,
Kd = 0.5) drives the exhaust DAC. Both controllers were tuned manually by iterative adjustment
on the physical device. DAC output voltages are clamped to the 0–10V rated input range of the
Festo VEMP piezo valve driver.
Anti-windup is implemented through four combined mechanisms: (1) the integral term is zeroed
whenever the error crosses zero (sign-change reset); (2) the integral is hard-clamped to±400; (3)
a per-cycle decay factor of 0.9999 is applied as a soft leak to prevent slow accumulation; and (4)
when the setpoint changes by more than 50mbar the integral is hard-reset to zero, while smaller
setpoint changes scale the integral by 0.95.
Host GUI role in characterization and twin-system testingIn the twin-system delay test,
pressure data are transmitted via Bluetooth from the Leader PCB of the sensing device to a host
computer, through which the signal is logged and forwarded using a python script to the second
device. The second device actuates the corresponding pressure response and reports status back to
the computer, enabling timing measurement.
1.3 Actuator characterization protocols
1.3.1 Blocked force and force bandwidth
The objective of this test was to quantify two critical performance metrics of the textile actuator: its
maximum output force and its frequency-dependent force response, known as the force bandwidth.
These parameters directly influence the actuator’s effectiveness in haptic feedback applications. The
maximum output force determines the strength of the tactile sensations that the actuator can de-
liver, which is essential for perceptibility through the skin. The force bandwidth reflects how quickly
and accurately the actuator can respond to dynamic input signals, an important requirement for
2
conveying realistic, time-varying haptic cues.
MethodsThe test setup shown in Fig. S1a and S1b, consists of a custom-built force measurement
platform. A 3D printed frame similar to the one use by du Pasquier et al. [2] was mounted on a
rigid steel base to ensure stability. A circular cutout in the frame houses the textile actuator. Below
the actuator, a calibrated force sensor (ATI Nano-17) [3] measured the downward force generated
during actuation.
To constrain the actuator and direct its expansion force toward the sensor, a rigid Plexiglass plate
was fixed on top of the actuator. This blocked force configuration prevented vertical displacement
and ensured that the pressurization force was directed into the force sensor.
To assess the actuators’ peak force at 2600 mbar input pressure, static force tests were conducted
on (n= 3) textile actuators.
In the second phase of the test, the actuators were repeatedly fully pressurized (opening the propor-
tional valve completely) and fully depressurized (closing the valve completely) at using a frequency
sweep from 1 to 100 Hz.

1.3.2 Vertical displacement under load
Tocharacterizehowthetextileactuatorinteractswithloadedsurfaces, anexperimentwasconducted
to measure its deformation under varying pressures and applied loads. The relationship between
force and displacement provides insight into how the textile actuators interface with human skin.
MethodsA vertical test stand similar to the one used in the force measurement tests is illustrated
in Fig. S1c. Instead of a flat Plexiglas plate, a transparent Plexiglas tube was used to constrain
the vertical motion of the load and ensure proper alignment with the actuator. A digital sliding
caliper was mounted above the weight to measure vertical displacement with high precision. As the
actuator was gradually pressurized to various pressure levels. As it inflated and lifted the weight
upward, the displacement readings were recorded in real time. For each load condition, n = 2 tests
were conducted to ensure consistency. The measurements were performed with n = 3 actuators
(n= 6tests totals) to account for actuator variability.
ResultsAs shown in Fig. S3, actuator displacement increases non-linearly with applied pressure
and is inversely proportional to the external load. The displacement generally decreases as the
load increases. At lower loads, the displacement curve saturates more quickly, resulting in greater
overall displacement and more pronounced nonlinearity. Conversely, higher loads tend to flatten
and linearize the pressure–displacement response. Crucially, even under the highest tested load, the
actuator achieves displacements exceeding 5 mm, enough to generate perceptible haptic feedback.
1.3.3 Airtightness and pressure retention
Maintaining airtightness is essential for pneumatic systems to ensure efficient operation, minimize
air and energy consumption, and enable reliable actuator control. To validate the airtightness of
the platform, we tested the device’s air retention systematically.
MethodsIn this experiment, three textile actuators were connected to a single device. Airtight-
ness was then measured as follows:
1. The inlet port of the proportional valve was fully opened to allow unrestricted airflow from
the CO2 cartridge regulator to the actuators.

2. A pressure regulator was adjusted to supply the standard operating pressure of 2665mbar,
fully inflating all actuators.
3. Once pressurized, the regulator was closed to isolate the device from the CO2 source and
prevent additional air intake.
4. The pressure gauge on the regulator was used to monitor internal pressure over time. The

1.3.4 Step response characterization
This experiment was used to characterize the transient behavior of the device by measuring its
response time to a step input, which provided insight into its dynamic performance and control
response.
MethodsThe device was initially stabilized at a baseline pressure of 1050mbar. Then, a step
pressure increase of 2000mbar was applied. The resulting actuator response was recorded using the
device’s integrated pressure sensor, with time-resolved data collected via the GUI. This data was
then analyzed using a custom Python script to extract key performance metrics such as rise time,
settle time, and overshoot. To evaluate performance and repeatability, a total of 20 step response
(n= 40) trials are conducted using two different valve–actuator pairs.
ResultsThe resulting actuator response is shown in Fig. S4. The system exhibits an average rise
time (10%–90%) of 29ms (ranging from 5ms to 34ms), with a total settling time of 39ms.
These results indicate that the controller achieved rapid and stable pressure control despite the
inherent limitations of pneumatic systems, which are typically slower than vibrotactile actuators
due to the compressibility of air and mechanical valve delays [5].
5
For wearable haptics, response times below 50ms are generally sufficient [6] to render clear and
timely tactile feedback. Thus, the device’s responsiveness is well within the acceptable range for
interactive applications and supports a variety of dynamic haptic effects such as buzzing, pulsing,
tapping and squeezing.

1.4 Twin-system tactile communication evaluation
1.4.1 Wireless delay evaluation protocol
TestsetupandprocedureOnedeviceoperatedinsensing-onlymode, monitoringpressureinput
from a pre-inflated actuator (1100mbar) without actuation. The second device operated in listening
mode and mirrored the sensed pressure data received wirelessly. A clear event was generated by
rapidly compressing the sensing actuator in a single motion lasting approximately 80ms, producing
a sharp pressure spike. Pressure data from both devices were logged to CSV via the host GUI, with
a shared wall-clock timestamp appended to each BLE packet, providing a common time reference
across devices. The test was repeated ten times (n= 10). The process was additionally captured
using an iPhone at 120FPS to independently verify timing.
Event onset detection and latency computationLatency was computed from the synchro-
nized CSV logs using a 3% step threshold method, consistent with the latency definition used in
step response characterization (Section 1.3.4). Event onset on the sensing device was defined as
the time at which the logged pressure trace first exceeded 3% of the peak pressure above the base-
line sensing pressure of 1100mbar, marking the onset of actuator compression. Event onset on the
listening device was defined as the time at which the reproduced pressure trace first exceeded the
same 3% threshold above its baseline. Latency per trial was computed as the difference between the
two onset timestamps using the shared wall-clock reference. The reported mean latency of 64ms
represents the mean acrossn= 10trials. The iPhone footage at 120FPS was used to verify the
plausibility of the computed values but was not used in the primary latency calculation.
1.4.2 Exploratory human communication task
Participants and setupFive participants (2 female, 3 male) took part in the bidirectional
haptic communication task, forming user pairs for testing. In each session, one participant served
as the sender (User S) and the other as the receiver (User R). The experiment was bidirectional:
participants alternated roles so that both acted as sender and receiver during the session, requiring
6
no hardware changes between role switches. Each participant wore a device equipped with three
textile-based actuators positioned along the forearm (labeled 1 to 3 from wrist to elbow). To
eliminate visual bias, User R was blindfolded during all trials.
Task conditions and trialsThree task conditions were tested. In the single actuator condition,
oneactuatorwasinflatedtosimulatealocalizedtouch. Inthesimultaneousactivationcondition, two
or more actuators were inflated together to simulate multi-point contact. In the intensity variation
condition, a single actuator was inflated at varying pressure levels to simulate light, medium, and
strong touch.
User R received 10 trials per condition (n= 10), for a total of 30 trials per participant pair.
In each trial, User R was asked to identify the location, number of active actuators, or stimulus
intensity depending on condition.
Signal amplificationTo improve perceptibility, transmitted signals were amplified by a fixed
gain of4×at the GUI forwarding stage. For example, a pressure input increasing from 1100mbar
to 1200mbar on the sender side produced a corresponding increase from 1100mbar to 1500mbar
on the receiver’s actuator.
ResultsUser R identified the location and number of active actuators with 100% accuracy across
all trials and conditions. Dynamic haptic patterns such as repeated pulses and oscillations were
reliably perceived and correctly interpreted. Intensity classification across three levels achieved ap-
proximately 90% accuracy; the majority of errors occurred between the light and medium categories,
which were occasionally perceived as similar in strength.

3 Teleoperation Control Implementation
This section provides full implementation details for the teleoperation system used during the user
studies.
3.1 Hand and Wrist Pose Tracking
Human hand and wrist poses were tracked using a Microsoft HoloLens 2 mixed-reality headset. The
headset provides six-degree-of-freedom tracking and real-time hand pose estimation derived from a
combination of a time-of-flight depth sensor, two infrared cameras, and an onboard inertial mea-
surement unit. These sensing modalities enable robust estimation of wrist position and orientation,
as well as fingertip locations, without the need for external cameras or additional calibration.
The see-through optical display allows users to view the physical environment directly, avoiding
reliance on video passthrough, which has been shown to contribute to discomfort and fatigue in
immersive teleoperation systems. The head-mounted form factor also allows users to move freely
within the workspace while maintaining consistent tracking performance.
3.2 Arm Control Mapping
The robotic platform used in this study was a Kinova Gen3 manipulator controlled through Kinova’s
ROS driver. Cartesian velocity commands were sent to the robot end-effector, and joint velocities
were computed internally using Kinova’s proprietary inverse kinematics solver.
Upon launching the teleoperation application on the HoloLens 2, the device’s world coordinate
frame is initialized. To approximately align the device world frame with the robot base coordinate
frame, the application is started while standing directly behind the robot and looking forward. Be-
cause control commands are based on pose deltas rather than absolute positions, precise collocation
of the coordinate frames is not required. Empirical testing indicated that this alignment convention
is sufficient for stable control. Equations S6 and S7 describe the velocity relationships between
the two stationary frames for the default control mode and when participants view the workspace
through the wrist-mounted camera feed.
To map human wrist motion to robot motion, linear velocities were computed from the change
in wrist position(xwrist,y wrist,z wrist)expressed in a stationary HoloLens world frame and scaled by
the elapsed time between detections. Angular velocities were computed by taking the difference
between consecutive wrist orientations represented as quaternions, converting this difference to a
rotation vector, and scaling it by the same time interval. The calculations are detailed in Equations
S1–S8.
During all teleoperation tasks, the gripper orientation was constrained to a top-down grasp
configuration. As a result, only angular velocities about the gripper’sZaxis were permitted. This
constraint reduced task complexity and ensured consistent contact geometry during manipulation.

3.3 Gripper Control and Hand Calibration
Control of the parallel-jaw gripper was achieved by mapping the distance between the user’s thumb
and index finger to the gripper opening width. Because hand size varies across users, an initial
calibration step was performed at the beginning of each session.
During calibration, participants were instructed to open their thumb and index finger to a
maximally comfortable distance, denotedd fingers,max. This value was mapped to the fully open
state of the gripper. During teleoperation, the instantaneous distance between the detected thumb
and index finger positions,dfingers, was continuously computed. The normalized ratio was then used
to generate the gripper command:
gcmd = 1−dfingers
dfingers,max
,
whereg cmd = 0corresponds to a fully open gripper andg cmd = 1corresponds to a fully closed
gripper.
To ensure smooth and stable gripper motion, gripper commands were updated only when three
conditions were simultaneously satisfied: (1) the absolute change from the previous command ex-
ceeded 0.05, (2) at least 0.01 s had elapsed since the last update, and (3) the translational velocity
of the robot end-effector was below 0.05 m/s. Condition (1) was determined empirically to prevent
unintended gripper activation by filtering out natural, unintentional finger movements or oscilla-
tions during arm motion. Condition (2) was also determined empirically to filter noise and ensure
10
intentional commands at a consistent rate. Finally, condition (3) was defined a priori to restrict
finger-based commands to moments when the arm was near rest, thereby reducing false gripper
activations caused by finger-tracking errors while the participant’s hand or the robotic arm was in
motion.
4 Detailed User Study Procedures and Additional Results
4.1 Participant Instructions and Task Flow
N= 25participants were recruited through university email lists and Slack channels. Partici-
pants provided informed consent, and the protocol received approval from the Stanford University
Institutional Review board (#65022).
Participants completed the study in two phases: Perception and Teleoperation. Prior to begin-
ningeachphase, participantsreceivedstandardizedverbalinstructionsexplainingthetaskobjectives
and response format. Participants were encouraged to ask clarification questions before each task
began.
During perception tasks, participants were seated facing away from the robot to eliminate visual
cues. During teleoperation tasks, participants stood in designated positions corresponding to each
visual condition.
4.2 Perception Task Parameters
4.2.1 Force Discrimination
Three discrete pressure levels (150, 300, and 450 mbar) were delivered to the fingertip actuators in
randomized order. Participants performed both 2-element and 3-element comparison tasks, report-
ing whether the second stimulus was greater than, smaller than, or equal to the reference.
4.2.2 Stiffness Discrimination
Three visually identical lattice cubes of increasing stiffness were fabricated using a Formlabs Form 3
printer and Elastic resin. Stiffness was measured in compression using an Instron 68TM-50 equipped
with a 5 kN static load cell. The cubes were compressed to 10% of their original height at a rate
of 0.6 mm/min. The measured stiffness values were 0.630 N/mm, 1.77 N/mm, and 3.35 N/mm.
Participants completed both direct manual discrimination and robot-mediated discrimination under
fixed gripper closure conditions.
4.2.3 Weight Discrimination
Three visually identical weights (150 g, 300 g, and 450 g) were used. In the teleoperated condition,
participants controlled both the robot arm and gripper while attempting to grasp and lift each
object with minimal applied force.
4.3 Teleoperation Task Parameters
Participants completed three pick-and-place tasks under different visual conditions. For PnP1,
participants had an unobstructed view of the robot and workspace. For PnP2, visual occlusions
were introduced due to robot self-occlusion. For PnP3, participants relied exclusively on the wrist-
mounted camera feed displayed on an external monitor.

4.4 Metrics and Data Processing
AccuracyDuring perception tasks, true and predicted stimulus sequences were recorded and
compared using confusion matrices. Accuracy was computed as the percentage of correctly identified
relationships across all trials.
Task Completion TimeCompletion time was defined as the interval between initial contact
with the object at the start location and release of the object at the goal location. Trials exceeding
5 minutes were marked as failures.
Path LengthGripper position(x,y,z)was logged continuously, and total path length was com-
puted as the sum of Euclidean distances between consecutive samples.
Perceived UsabilitySystem usability was assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS).
Perceived Task LoadPerceived workload was assessed using the NASA Task Load Index (TLX),
including all six subscales.

4.6 Additional Results
Per participant Teleoperation resultsHere, we report per-participant mean applied pressure,
task duration, and gripper path length for each of the three teleoperation tasks. Participants were
randomly assigned to begin each task either with or without haptic feedback. These results are
shown in Figs. S5, S6, and S7 for the Direct View (PnP1), Obstructed View (PnP2), and Camera-
Only View (PnP3) conditions, respectively.
As reported in the main text, the addition of haptic feedback led to overall improvements
across all three performance metrics when averaged across participants. Examination of the per-
participant results reveals additional structure. In PnP1 and PnP2, the order in which participants
experienced the conditions influenced task duration and path length: participants tended to take
longer and follow longer trajectories in the condition they encountered first, regardless of whether
haptic feedback was present. This ordering effect was not observed in PnP3 (Camera-Only View),
where visual uncertainty dominated performance.
Across all tasks and conditions, the applied pressure was consistently lower or comparable when
haptic feedback was provided, indicating that haptic cues supported force regulation independently
of task order effects.

SUS ResultsHere, we report the average SUS scores for each condition (Figure S9). The SUS is
a standardized 10-item Likert-scale questionnaire used to evaluate perceived system usability. Odd-
numbered items are positively framed, for which higher scores indicate better usability, whereas
even-numbered items are negatively framed, for which lower scores indicate better usability.
As discussed in the main text, participants perceived the system with haptic feedback as more
usable overall, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. This trend nevertheless
suggests that haptic feedback may positively influence users’ perceptions of system usability and
interaction quality. The absence of statistical significance is likely attributable, in part, to the
additional complexity introduced by integrating the haptic feedback subsystem with the robotic
platform, which may have increased the overall cognitive and operational demands of the system.
Future work will focus on streamlining the system architecture and improving integration robustness
to better realize the usability benefits of haptic feedback.
