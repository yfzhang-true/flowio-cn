Abstract 
Vibrotactile feedback has become a popular and accessible means of 
conveying haptic information, with applications spanning virtual 
reality, robotics, healthcare, and accessibility. As interest grows in 
extending vibrotactile feedback beyond the hands to the full body, 
researchers face increasing challenges in designing scalable, per-
ceptually grounded systems. Despite advances in haptic hardware 
and perception research, existing tools often lack the flexibility, 
scalability, and application focus needed to support body-worn 
multi-actuator systems. This disconnect between perceptual un-
derstanding, hardware development, and real-world applications 
limits the broader impact of haptics research.

My research aim to bridge these gaps by introducing a human-
centered approach to close the design loop of vibrotactile systems. 
Full-body vibrotactile applications serve as "practical lenses" to 
identify key research questions in tactile perception and study them 
using psychophysics methods. Findings from the perceptual studies 
serve as "design guidelines" that inform the scalable and intuitive 
design of the VibraForge toolkit. The toolkit empowers both experts 
and non-experts to build full-body vibrotactile systems, leading 
to novel applications of tactile feedback in broader interaction 
scenarios such as human-robot interaction.

1 Introduction 
Haptics research studies the sense of touch, one of the essential 
human sensory systems that enable humans to perceive and interact 
with the world. In the past decades, haptic technologies that are 
used to simulate haptic stimuli have witnessed remarkable progress. 
From the early von Frey hairs [20] that only exert static tactile 
stimuli on the skin, to modern smartphone vibrations from built-
in tactors [4], various haptic technologies have been proposed to 
convey different types of haptic sensations, including vibrotactile 
actuators [16], mechanical indentations [51], peltier modules [31], 
pneumatic / microfluidic chambers [40], electrical stimulation [27], 
exoskeleton [38], and grounded force feedback controllers [41]. 
Among these technologies, vibrotactile systems, which produce 
tactile sensations via actuators vibrating on the skin, have gained 
popularity because they are compact, affordable and accessible [4]. 
Vibrotactile systems have been widely adopted in various domains, 
such as virtual reality (VR) [21, 34, 44], robotics [7, 12, 32, 47], health-
care [6, 23, 48], and accessibility [26, 42, 46]. Early work focused 
on providing vibrotactile feedback to the hand via gloves [1, 33] 
or handheld objects [11, 50]. In recent years, the focus of vibro-
tactile systems has expanded from the hand to the body. This is 
because full-body haptic feedback can significantly enhance im-
mersion [39] for applications such as virtual reality and multimedia 
entertainment. Moreover, the human body with its dense arrays 
of mechanoreceptors, offers great potential of an alternative in-
formation transmission channel, enabling potential applications 
such as assistive technology [26, 37], multimodal learning [3], and 
human-robot interaction [2, 17]. 
Despite the potential of body-worn vibrotactile systems, re-
searchers and designers often find it difficult to build such systems 
due to lack of proper design guidelines and tools. While a significant 
amount of work has been done to investigate vibrotactile perception, 
research findings are often tied to specific experiment hardware 
and cannot generalize to design guidelines [15]. Various toolkits 
have been proposed to assist with the design process [9, 30, 35, 45], 
but they cannot support a large number of actuators and fail to 
meet the scalability requirements of body-worn vibrotactile sys-
tems. More fundamentally, lack of focus on end-user applications 
has led to research effort being invested in the indefinite explo-
ration of nuanced perception problems and the development of 
impractical hardware solutions. 
As a human-computer interaction (HCI) researcher, I argue that 
these gaps in haptic research can be bridged by HCI research meth-
ods [24] (Figure 1). In HCI research, researchers first identify the 
target application, then study human perception problems related 
to the application, and finally develop novel hardware that satis-
fies perception requirements. Metaphorically, applications serve as 
"practical lenses" that help researchers locate key research ques-
tions in the enormous space of human perception, and perception 
findings serve as "design guidelines" that help researchers evaluate 
hardware solutions. 
Therefore, the research objective of my dissertation is: 
To develop scalable, intuitive haptic design tools that em-
power both haptic experts and non-experts to create inno-
vative on-body haptic systems. By aligning haptic design 
with human perceptual principles, these tools aim to support 
full-body user experiences and enhance task performance 
in novel applications. 
In my PhD, I published three papers to fulfill the research objec-
tive and contributed new insights to the haptics research commu-
nity. 
2 Vibrotactile Perception (ToH’24) 
Figure 2: The circuit design, experimental scene, and results 
of the vibrotactile spatial acuity study. 
The applications of full-body vibrotactile systems serve as "practi-
cal lenses" that help identify key perception problems. For example, 
to understand how much information can be delivered through vi-
brotactile feedback on the body, researchers measure spatial acuity, 
which is the ability to distinguish between vibrotactile stimuli at 
different locations [14, 19, 43]. In prior work, spatial acuity has been 
measured at multiple body sites with various types of actuators and 
psychophysics methods [5, 8, 10, 22]. However, the complex vibra-
tion characteristics of vibrotactile actuators make it challenging for 
researchers to reference and compare previous study results. This 
is because the effects of typical characteristics, such as intensity 
and frequency, are not well understood. Consequently, researchers 
often need to replicate previous experiments using their own ex-
perimental setup before performing new experiments. 
In this work, we investigated the effects of intensity and fre-
quency on vibrotactile spatial acuity (Figure 2). Using Linear Res-
onant Actuators (LRAs), we conducted relative point localization 
experiments to measure spatial acuity under different conditions. In 
the first experiment, we found that intensity had a significant effect 
on spatial acuity, with higher intensity leading to better acuity. In 
the second experiment, using a carefully designed intensity cali-
bration procedure, we did not find a significant effect of frequency 
on spatial acuity. These findings provide a better understanding 
of vibrotactile spatial acuity, allow for comparisons to previous re-
search, and provide insights into the design of future tactile devices.

3 Vibrotactile Hardware (CHI’25) 
Results from perceptual studies provide guidelines to inform the 
design of novel hardware. For example, spatial acuity measure-
ments help determine the layout of spatialized vibrotactile feedback 
systems, which deliver tactile information by placing multiple vi-
brotactile actuators on the body. These systems are used in battle 
simulation suits [25], directed movement sleeves [28], VR guid-
ance [44], and robot teleoperation [47]. Despite the potential of 
spatialized vibrotactile feedback systems, haptic designers face chal-
lenges creating such systems because increasingly large numbers of 
actuators are needed to convey information in complex applications, 
but existing toolkits offer limited scalability. 
In this work, we introduce VibraForge, an open-source vibrotac-
tile toolkit that supports up to 128 vibrotactile actuators (Figure 3). 
Each actuator is encapsulated within a self-contained vibration 
unit and driven by its own microcontroller. By leveraging a chain-
connection method, each unit receives independent vibration com-
mands from a control unit, with fine-grained control over intensity 
and frequency. We also designed a GUI Editor to expedite the au-
thoring of spatial vibrotactile patterns. 
The technical evaluation of VibraForge revealed that the toolkit 
has a high bandwidth (200 Hz), low latency (16 ms), and can re-
liably render complex audio signals in real time. Through three 
case studies, we demonstrate that VibraForge not only supports 
the efficient replication of existing research prototypes such as 
phonemic displays [37], but also improves user performance in 
various applications such as virtual reality fitness gaming and robot 
teleoperation. In a usability study, ten participants with diverse 
technical backgrounds learned to use the toolkit in 15 minutes and 
completed design tasks and a free exploration activity. The findings 
from this study reaffirmed VibraForge’s low technical barrier and 
high potential for customizability. 
4 Vibrotactile Application (RA-L’25) 
Novel hardware toolkits, such as VibraForge, enable full-body tac-
tile applications that were previously only envisioned. In unmanned 
aerial vehicle (UAV) teleoperation, haptic feedback has been shown 
effective in reducing collisions by providing directional obstacle 
information to operators [36, 49]. However, previously such feed-
back is rendered via haptic joysticks [18, 41], which are unfamiliar 
to UAV operators and limited to single-direction force feedback. 
Additionally, the direct coupling between the input device and the 
feedback method diminishes operators’ sense of control and induces 
oscillatory movements [13, 36]. 
To overcome these limitations, we propose AeroHaptix, a wear-
able haptic feedback system (Figure 4) that uses spatial vibrations 
to simultaneously communicate multiple obstacle directions to op-
erators, without interfering with their input control. The layout 
of vibrotactile actuators was optimized via a perceptual study to 
eliminate perceptual biases and achieve uniform spatial coverage. 
Our novel rendering algorithm, MultiCBF, extends control barrier 
functions to support multi-directional feedback. Our system eval-
uation showed that AeroHaptix effectively reduced the number 
of collisions and input disagreement compared to a no-feedback 
condition. Furthermore, operators reported that AeroHaptix was 
more helpful than force feedback, with improved situational aware-
ness and comparable workload. This work is published in the IEEE 
Robotics and Automation Letters journal as a regular paper. 
5 Discussion and Future Work 
In summary, these three projects show the potential of applying 
HCI methods to answer significant research questions for the HCI 
and broader research communities. I believe that this methodology 
of using applications as "practical lenses" and perception as "design 
guidelines" will be more widely adopted by researchers, helping 
bridge the gap between multiple research streams and making 
haptic systems more focused on human needs in the future. 
While my thesis mainly uses vibrotactile interaction as an ex-
ample to showcase the benefits of human-centered approach, this 
approach can be applied to other haptic technologies and broader 
research contexts as well. In my ongoing work, I am targeting the 
application of haptic feedback in neural prosthetics. Current design 
of upper limb prosthetics leverages electromyography (EMG) sens-
ing to detect neural signals and produce corresponding robotic hand 
movement. However, the lack of haptic feedback from hand-object 
interaction makes it difficult for amputees to perform dexterous 
manipulations. Therefore, we first delve into understanding what 
haptic sensations are delivered to human hands when interacting 
with objects. Then we develop our custom peripheral magnetic neu-
ral stimulation device to generate the corresponding kinesthetic 
and tactile feedback. Compared to industry standard desktop stim-
ulation devices like MagStim, our custom built device comes with 
a compact wearable form factor as an armband, and can be easily 
adapted to individual users’ needs. This device is expected to pro-
vide amputee users with a closed-loop neural interface, enabling 
them to exert precise control over prosthetics to perform dexterous 
manipulation tasks in daily life. 
6 Research and Dissertation Status 
I have just completed my fifth year as a PhD candidate in the 
Dynamic Graphics Project (DGP) Lab at the University of Toronto, 
under the supervision of Prof. Daniel Wigdor. I have published the 
aforementioned three papers on my dissertation topic, all as the 
first author. I have also mentored undergraduate research assistants 
in our lab and completed a Student Innovation Contest project at 
UIST’24 [29]. Besides my publications, I spend significant amount 
of effort making the VibraForge toolkit more accessible to general 
users. Researchers interested in the toolkit can submit research 
proposals describing their ideas. Once approved, they will get a 
pre-assembled development kit. Currently the toolkit is actively 
used by researchers from multiple institutions (HKUST, MIT, EPFL, 
Kookmin University, UW, University of Bath, etc) to integrate tactile 
feedback into various applications, such as accessibility, healthcare, 
human-robot interaction, etc. Receiving critique and engaging in 
discussions with the doctoral symposium panel would assist me in 
better framing my research objective, thinking about broader social 
impact of HCI research, and getting prepared for future research 
presentations.
