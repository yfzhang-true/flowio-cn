ABSTRACT The control of soft elastomeric actuators via pneumatic systems presents challenges due
to system nonlinearities and oscillatory behavior. The control of soft robotics is an underexplored field
compared to the control of traditional robotics. This study explores evolutionary algorithms for auto-
tuning Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controllers in pneumatic soft robotics. Four optimization
algorithms, Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Simulated Annealing (SA), and
Surrogate-Based Optimization (SBO), were employed to optimize PID parameters for pneumatic pressure
control. Each algorithm was tested with four objective functions: Integral Absolute Error (IAE), Integral
Time Absolute Error (ITAE), Integral Square Error (ISE), and Integral Time Square Error (ITSE). Both
simulated and experimental studies are conducted to evaluate these algorithms using a pneumatic system
designed with affordable on/off valves controlled by Pulse Width Modulation (PWM). Key performance
metrics were analyzed: rise time, settling time, overshoot, peak value, and peak time. Results indicate that
PSO and GA offer faster response times and moderate overshoot, while SA provides minimal overshoot
at the cost of slower response times. These findings can significantly contribute to the practical control of
pneumatic systems in soft robotics, offering insights for future optimization and application development.

due to their adaptability, safety, and affordability. They can
have high rates of deformability since they are fabricated
from compliant materials [1], making them suitable for sensi-
tive physical human-robot interaction applications [2]. Most
soft robots are actuated by pneumatic systems that consist of
affordable components such as solenoid valves, diaphragm
pumps, and pressure sensors. The benefits of pneumatics

control platform. Several pneumatic control stations are

PneuSoRD [6], OpenPneu [7], and programmable air [8].
Although many toolkits are available for pneumatic control
of soft actuators, there is a shortage of research on feedback
control for these systems. Only two of the aforementioned
kits present closed-loop control as part of the open-source
package. These include PneuSoRD [6] and OpenPneu [7],
which used PID to control the pressure. Other authors have
extended the functionality of these open-source packages to
implement closed-loop control. Elgeneidy [9] used the SRT
with PID to control an angle for a soft pneumatic actuator;
the PID was tuned first using the Ziegler-Nichols method
and then trial and error for fine-tuning. Khan [10] used the
SRT with an inverse dynamics controller to control the angle
of a soft finger actuator. Xavier [11] simulated a pneumatic
control system similar to these common toolkits and analyzed
the effect of a PI controller with anti-windup for the on/off
valve, using trial and error for PI tuning. In all of these cases
where closed-loop control is presented, the physical system
uses mechanical PWM with on/off solenoid valves to regulate
airflow. The on/off valves are the most common choice for
these kits because they are lightweight and affordable.
From the aforementioned papers that present closed-loop
control for pneumatic systems with PWM of on/off valves,
PI or PID algorithms are used to control the system; thus,
it can be concluded that PID control is an acceptable
choice for this type of system. Different methods have been
devised to adjust PID-controlled systems’ gains, including
trial-and-error, D-partitioning, Ziegler-Nichols, and pole
placement [12]. Despite their broad adoption in the sector,
traditional PID controllers have a reputation for underper-
forming in the presence of disruptions [13]. Additionally,
the practical application of these direct techniques demands
an in-depth comprehension of the system and its frequency
response, as they were designed with linear, time-invariant
systems in mind. This necessity has prompted the adoption of
numerous evolutionary optimization methods for fine-tuning
the gains of PID controllers, especially in nonlinear systems
such as those found in robotics [14], [15].
The choice of the PID controller in this study is based
on several vital considerations specific to the pneumatic soft
robotic system. Despite its known disadvantages compared
to advanced algorithms, the PID controller offers a balance
of simplicity, ease of implementation, and sufficient control
performance for many practical applications. PID controllers
are extensively used in industry due to their well-understood
design, tuning simplicity, and computational efficiency [16].
For pneumatic soft robotic systems exhibiting inherent
nonlinearities, compliance, and time-varying dynamics, the
PID controller provides a practical first-line approach to
achieving desired control objectives, such as minimizing
rise time, settling time, and overshooting. More advanced
algorithms, such as model predictive control (MPC) or
adaptive control, while potentially offering superior perfor-
mance, often require higher computational resources and
more complex implementation, which may not be practical
for real-time embedded control of pneumatic systems.
Evolutionary algorithms are stochastic search methods
that imitate natural biological evolution [17]. Ants, for
example, use the quickest path to a food source, while
birds use migration to reach their destinations. Researchers
have devised computational systems to emulate the efficient
actions of these species. These systems aim to find reliable
solutions to intricate optimization challenges. There are sev-
eral advantages to using evolutionary algorithms compared to
traditional optimization algorithms. Evolutionary algorithms
use a population of solutions instead of single-point solutions
and use a parallelized method by simultaneously evaluating
multiple points in the parameter space. This approach
increases the likelihood of finding the global optimum,
reducing the chances of getting stuck at a local optimum [18].
Evolutionary algorithms can also handle significant opti-
mization problems with multiple variables and resolve issues
independently without human expertise; nonetheless, they
may rely on human expertise when accessible [19]. Some of
the most common evolutionary algorithms employed across
many applications include the Genetic Algorithm (GA) [20],
Simulated Annealing (SA) [21], Ant Colony Optimization
(ACO) [22], Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) [23], and
Surrogate-Based Optimization (SBO) [24].
This study focuses on applying evolutionary algorithms
for tuning PID controllers in pneumatic soft robotic sys-
tems. These systems have several challenges, such as
inherent nonlinearities, compliance, time-varying dynamics,
and time-delay due to air compressibility and actuator
deformation. While traditional methods like Ziegler-Nichols
and pole placement offer computational simplicity, they are
primarily designed for linear, time-invariant systems and
often fail to account for the nonlinearities, compliance, and
time-varying dynamics inherent in pneumatic soft robotics.
Evolutionary algorithms, by contrast, are well-suited for
optimizing complex, nonlinear systems due to their ability to
explore a broad solution space and avoid local optima. This
advantage is particularly relevant in pneumatic soft robotic
systems, where achieving optimal control requires handling
multiple variables and constraints that evolve dynamically
over time. Therefore, while more time-consuming, evolution-
ary algorithms provide a more robust and flexible approach
to tuning PID parameters in such contexts, as demonstrated
in this study’s simulation and experimental results.
Apart from pneumatic applications, PID parameters are
often tuned using evolutionary algorithms. Abdelbar [12]
tuned a cascaded PI position controller using Surrogate
Based Optimization (SBO), Particle Swarm Optimization
(PSO), Hybrid Genetic Pattern Search Algorithm (HGSPA),
and Simulated Annealing (SA) algorithms. Parnianifard [25]
proposed a surrogate-based robust simulation-optimization

In summary, the control of pneumatic soft robotic systems
presents significant challenges due to inherent nonlinearities,
compliance, and time-varying dynamics. Although widely
used, traditional PID controllers often underperform in
nonlinear systems when tuned using conventional methods
like Ziegler-Nichols or trial and error. There is a lack of
robust, automated tuning approaches that can effectively
optimize PID parameters for these pneumatic systems. This
study addresses the gap by exploring the application of evo-
lutionary algorithms to optimize PID controllers specifically
for pneumatic soft robotic systems, aiming to enhance control
performance under simulated and experimental conditions.
The novelty of this study lies in its application and
comprehensive comparison of four distinct evolutionary
algorithms, GA, PSO, SA, and SBO, for PID controller
optimization in pneumatic soft robotic systems, a relatively
unexplored area in the field of control engineering. Unlike
previous studies that have primarily focused on traditional
or more rigid robotic systems, this research addresses the
challenges of pneumatic soft robotics, such as nonlin-
earities, time-varying dynamics, compliance, and actuator
deformations. This work also contributes a dual evaluation
approach by integrating both simulation and experimental
results to validate the effectiveness of these algorithms
under real-world conditions, which is rarely done in existing
literature. Additionally, a systematic analysis of how different
objective functions (IAE, ITAE, ISE, ITSE) impact the tuning
results is presented, offering deeper insights into optimizing
control performance for pneumatic soft robotic systems.
By providing a comparative analysis and highlighting the
strengths and limitations of each evolutionary algorithm
in this specific context, this study sets a new benchmark
for selecting and implementing optimization techniques in
pneumatic soft robotics. This contribution is significant for
advancing practical applications, from medical devices to
assistive technologies, where soft robotics offers unique
advantages.
II. MECHATRONICS DESIGN OF THE PNEUMATIC SYSTEM
The proposed pneumatic system is a modified version of the
well-known Soft Robotics Toolkit (SRT). Key modifications
include:
• Reduction of electrical noise: The buck converters are
replaced with individual linear power supplies because
the switching frequency in the buck converters adds
noise to the pressure sensor readings.
• Reduction of mechanical oscillations: A pneumatic tank
is added to reduce oscillations from the high-frequency
switching of the on/off valves.
• Motor protection: A relief valve is added to protect the
pump from failure during over-pressurization.

6) Pressure Sensor, 7) Actuator Outputs, 8) on/off Power Switches, and 9)

The main components of the proposed pneumatic system
are a set of on/off pneumatic valves, a relief valve, a DC
pump, MOSFET switches, and an A TMega2560 microcon-
troller, as shown in figure 1.
The pressure in the system is modulated by a Pulse Width
Modulated (PWM) signal applied to a high-speed on/off
pneumatic valve. The system has 3/2 solenoid valves (SMC-
VQ110U-5M), which control the pressure by high-frequency
switching between ON and OFF states to achieve the desired
pressure. Additionally, each valve has a pressure sensor
(Honeywell-ASDXA VX100PGAA5) that monitors the actu-
ator pressure. An Arduino Mega board is programmed to
apply PID control for the pneumatic system. To protect
the pump from failure, a relief valve, which consists of an
on/off valve and pressure sensor, is used to open the valve
to the atmosphere when the pressure reaches a threshold.
The PWM signal at 50Hz from the Arduino board is used
to change the pressure level of the actuator by changing the
PWM duty cycle. This mechanical PWM operation of on/off
valves provides an affordable and straightforward method
for controlling the soft actuator pressure. Despite these
advantages, there is the disadvantage of pressure fluctuations
around the desired valve due to mechanical switching of the
valves. This fluctuation happens because the valve modulates
output pressure by pumping or releasing high-pressure air at
the switching frequency of the PWM. As a result, a pneumatic
tank is used to overcome this problem; a 12ml syringe is used
as a tank in this system [9], [27]. Additionally, the average
filter is used to smooth the oscillations.

III. OPTIMIZATION ALGORITHMS
Four evolutionary algorithms are employed for auto-tuning
the PID parameters: the Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle
Swarm Optimization (PSO), Simulated Annealing (SA), and
Surrogate-Based Optimization (SBO). Each of these four
optimization algorithms is implemented with four different
objective functions to identify the optimal set of parameters
that optimize pressure control performance. The following
are the explanations of each optimization algorithm. The
optimization algorithm parameters were chosen based on trial
and error within the boundary constraints of the hardware.
A. GENETIC ALGORITHM (GA)
Holland et al. [20] introduced the GA in 1975. Because
it is so effective at exploring, this metaheuristic has been
widely used in many combinatorial optimization problems.
The objective function within the algorithm may be used with
various heuristics to gain problem-specific insights. In the
GA, the parameters (the inputs to the objective function) are
encoded within a string of genes or chromosomes. These
chromosomes then define the solution space [28], [29]. The
GA mimics the course of natural evolution. In biology,
the principle of the survival of the fittest states that better
solutions may be attained by recombination. Similarly, in the
GA, the parameters within each chromosome are swapped
with partner chromosomes to create offspring.

B. PARTICLE SWARM OPTIMIZATION (PSO)
Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is a metaheuristic
method for optimization problems. Russell Eberhart and
James Kennedy initially presented PSO in 1995 [31]. The
algorithm is predicated on social behavior, in which particles,
or possible solutions, interact with other particles in the
search space to move closer to the ideal answer. Because of
PSO’s ease of use, dependability, and efficiency, it has been
extensively utilized in a variety of industries.
In the natural world, many examples of swarming behavior
include packs of hunting wolves or flocks of migrating birds.
The members of these swarms, known as particles in PSO
terminology, start by randomly exploring their surroundings
before beginning to gravitate toward the discoveries made
by other members of the swarm. These particles advance
toward their goal by learning from their findings and the
discoveries of the swarm while simultaneously seeking
randomly in space and looking for signals of sustenance.
The PSO starts with a set (or swarm) of decision variables.
Each decision variable is a vector of the parameters that
are being optimized. The decision variable is denoted as
a position vector and updated using a velocity vector. The
velocity vector is calculated based on the personal best,
the global best, and some randomness. The personal best
is the best value that a specific particle has reached over
the iterations thus far. The global best is the best of all the
personal bests across the entire swarm. In this way, each
position vector moves in the direction of its velocity vector
at each iteration update, gravitating towards the optimum.
By gradual movement toward the swarm’s optimal outcome,
the function’s global optimum is achieved [32], [33].

For the implementation of an SBO algorithm, this paper
uses MA TLAB’s Surrogate optimization algorithm. The SBO
consists of two steps. The first is a surrogate construction,
which uses random points within the bounds and then
evaluates the objective function at these points to construct a
surrogate of the objective function by interpolating a radial
basis function [36]. The key characteristic of an RBF is
that its value depends only on the distance from a central
point; hence, its calculation is fast. The solver starts from
the smallest objective function value since the last surrogate
reset in the construction stage. The solver searches for a
minimum merit function that relates to the surrogate and the
distance from existing search points to balance minimizing
the surrogate and searching the space. The merit function
is a weighted combination of the scaled surrogate and the
scaled distance. The scaled surrogate is a normalized measure
used in optimization algorithms. It typically transforms
the surrogate function values into a standard range [0, 1].
This scaling helps in comparing different surrogate values
consistently. On the other hand, The scaled distance is used
to measure the distance of a point x from evaluated points
in the optimization space, normalized similarly to the scaled
surrogate. This measure helps explore the search space more
effectively by prioritizing points far from those already
evaluated.

Due to the larger square of a critical error, the ISE
(Integrated Square Error) penalizes substantial errors more
than lesser ones. Significant errors are often eliminated fast
by control systems designed to minimize ISE, while minor
errors may remain for a long time. This objective function
frequently results in quick rise times but with prolonged
low-amplitude oscillation. Moreover, squaring errors may
increase the effect of outliers, producing biased optimization
outcomes. The IAE (Integrated Absolute Error), on the other
hand, does not give any system response faults more weight.
It often results in less prolonged oscillation and a slower
response than ISE systems.
The ITAE (Integrated Time Absolute error) is the integral
of the absolute error multiplied by the time. Errors that occur
later in the response are given a higher weight than those
that occur initially. Compared to the previous two tuning
techniques, ITAE tuning systems may settle considerably
faster. The drawback of ITAE is that it results in systems
with a slow response, which is required to prevent prolonged
oscillation. By combining information from both ISE and
ITAE, the Integral Time Square Error (ITSE) penalizes
significant mistakes in the first transient stage. This measure
may result in more oscillations as it balances response time
and settling time. Squaring errors, however, can increase the
influence of outliers, leading to skewed optimization results.
The optimal choice for objective function varies depending
on the application. The performance metrics discussed high-
light distinct control characteristics; for instance, a square
error term penalizes significant control errors more rig-
orously than absolute error methods, prioritizing precise,
steady-state error-free control. Thus, as a control engineer,
selecting the most suitable metric depends on the primary
requirements of the control problem at hand. This decision
often prompts lively discussions, as identifying the ‘‘best
choice’’ for a particular application is seldom straightfor-
ward. Therefore, most researchers typically compare the four
objective functions and choose the most suitable one for their
specific application.

VII. DISCUSSION
There are some differences between the simulation and
experimental results for the four optimization algorithms
(Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization
(PSO), Simulated Annealing (SA), and Surrogate-Based
Optimization (SBO)) with different objective functions (IAE,
ITAE, ISE, and ITSE). For example, most experiments’ rising
and settling times are longer than in the simulations, and the
overshoot percentages are more significant. Many causes lead
to these differences, including unmodeled dynamics, environ-
mental variation, nonlinearities, unmodeled disturbances, and
parameter uncertainties [42], [43], [44], [45]. These causes
are enumerated below in more detail.
1) Model Simplifications: Simulating physical systems
using simpler models reduces computing complexity.
These models may not represent all the nonlinearities,
delays, and disturbances in the real system, resulting
in differences between simulated and experimental
results.
2) Parameter V ariations: Modeling characteristics, includ-
ing friction, air leakage, and valve dynamics, can be
challenging due to their variability. These variations
can affect the system’s rising time, settling time, and
overshoot.
3) Measurement Noise: Sensor and measurement device
faults and noise are common in experimental settings.
This noise can change observed performance and
introduce deviations the models did not anticipate.
For instance, the error of the pressure sensor in this
pneumatic system, which includes all errors due to
offset, full-scale span, pressure non-linearity, pressure
hysteresis, repeatability, thermal effect on offset, ther-
mal effect on span, and thermal hysteresis, can reach
maximum until 2% of Full-Scale Span (FSS). As a

VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
The comparative study of the PID controller for a pneumatic
toolkit used for general soft robotics is presented. Four
evolutionary algorithms (GA, PSO, SA, and SBO) across
multiple objective functions (IAE, ITAE, ISE, and ITSE) are
used for the comparison, identifying each approach’s main
strengths and limitations in both simulated and real-world
scenarios.
The paper’s outcome is that GA and PSO consistently
outperform other algorithms in balancing quick response
times with minimal overshoot, making them particularly
suitable for applications requiring speed and precision.
Although the SA is slower in rise time, it showed a much
lower overshoot with the ISE objective function in both soft
actuator and blocked outlet real-world experiments. This is
ideal for systems where stability is a higher priority. The
SBO showed higher overshoots and peak pressures in most
tests. The discrepancies observed between simulation and
experimental results showed the obstacle of moving from
the simulation to the real-world application; there are many
factors for these discrepancies, such as model simplifications,
parameter variations, measurement noise, and environmental
conditions. Furthermore, there is a notable difference in
the response of rigid versus soft actuators, particularly in

settling time, which resulted from many causes, such as
energy dissipation, control challenges, material properties,

This research provides valuable guidance for selecting and
implementing optimization algorithms in pneumatic control
systems for soft robotics. It can be considered a benchmark
for future studies, such as applying ranking algorithms
to choose the best optimization algorithms [47] using
different evolution optimization algorithms and applying a

help design more efficient, responsive, and flexible control
systems, eventually enhancing the capabilities of soft robotic
applications across various domains.
