Abstract
Ultrasonic mist-making devices are commonly deployed as closed,
appliance-level systems with limited programmability. At the same
time, recent multimodal large language models (LLMs) have ex-
panded interaction beyond text to visual and auditory modalities,
while environmental and atmospheric outputs remain largely un-
explored. This paper introduces MistMaker, an open-source, pro-
grammable ultrasonic mist platform, and Large Language Model
MistMaker (LLMMM), a system that connects LLMs to mist gen-
eration through an API-mediated, interpretive control pipeline.
Through two hardware pilots, we examine how mist is perceived,

1 Introduction
Environmental outputs such as airflow, temperature changes, and
mist have long served as sites of exploration for engineers, artists,
makers, and creative practitioners. Prior work has examined how
air and atmospheric forces can be harnessed as interaction media,
ranging from perceptual interfaces that make invisible airflow legi-
ble, to expressive systems that leverage environmental dynamics
for embodied and affective experiences. For example, Suzuki et al.
explore airflow as a tangible interaction material that can shape
bodily perception and engagement [22]. Broscheit et al. and Min et
al. investigate how environmental phenomena can be experienced
as interfaces instead of ambient background conditions[2, 11]. More
recent work such as AirCraft further demonstrates how air-based
actuation can support creative and exploratory interaction in both
technical and artistic contexts[24].
Despite this sustained interest, mist occupies an uneasy position
within this body of work. Unlike airflow or temperature, mist is most
often encountered through closed, appliance-level systems—such
as humidifiers or fog machines—that offer limited programmability
and obscure their underlying control mechanisms. As a result, mist
is frequently treated as a decorative or atmospheric effect instead of
programmable interaction or prototyping materials. Practitioners
who wish to work with mist in interactive or creative contexts of-
ten rely on pre-existing commercial device and reverse-engineered
circuits, or bespoke one-off implementations, which limits repro-
ducibility, iteration, and broader participation.
In parallel, recent work on multimodal large language models
(LLMs) has primarily focused on extending language-based interac-
tion through textual, visual, and auditory modalities. Survey work
by Wang et al. characterizes the dominant trajectory of multimodal
LLM research as the integration of text with images, video, and
speech, emphasizing tasks such as visual question answering, im-
age captioning, audio-visual reasoning, and multimodal content
generation [23]. Within HCI contexts, this orientation has trans-
lated into interactive systems where LLMs mediate user interaction
through screen-based representations, spoken language, or struc-
tured symbolic inputs and outputs.
More recent creative and interactive applications further rein-
force this focus. Sayel et al. explore multimodal LLM interfaces
that combine visual perception and linguistic reasoning to support
interactive control and interpretation in virtual environments [18].
Sorokin et al. examine multimodal LLM interaction through voice
and graphical interfaces.[21]. Across these works, multimodality is
largely framed in terms of representational media that can be view,
labeled, and rendered legible to both users and models.
Phenomena such as mist, humidity, scents, and airflow are dif-
fuse, transient, and difficult to discretize, resisting the representa-
tional and vectorizing logic that underpins most multimodal LLM
pipelines. This marginalization of environmental media stands in
contrast to a long tradition of ambient and multisensory interaction
research within HCI. Ambient feedback systems emerged as an
alternative to interaction models centered on explicit commands,
notifications, or task-driven interfaces, instead embedding infor-
mation into the surrounding environment so that it is perceived
peripherally and over time [5, 6, 9, 10]. This disconnect highlights
a gap between how interaction has been conceptualized in ambi-
ent HCI and how multimodality is currently operationalized in
LLM-mediated systems.
Overall, environmental outputs remain largely absent from LLM-
mediated interaction systems. Part of the reason lies at the lack
of programmable prototyping tools, while prevailing multimodal
frameworks privilege symbolic and legible perception and genera-
tion over material, experiential, and subtle modulation. MistMaker
is designed with the gap in mind. Large Language Model MistMaker
(LLMMM) is developed to explore how LLMs might participate in
environmental interaction.
2 System Overview
2.1 MistMaker
MistMaker is an Open Source Hardware–certified ultrasonic mist
platform (OSHWA US002742)1. It is a microcontroller-controlled
ultrasonic mist system where a network-capable programmable
controller drives a piezoelectric disc at resonance to generate mist,
while integrated battery charging, power regulation, and protection
circuitry support portable and reusable deployments.
A central design decision in MistMaker is the embedded pro-
grammable microcontroller in the XIAO form factor, specifically
the XIAO ESP32-C6[19]. The footprint selection is to maintain a
small board size while ensure adaptivity for other micorcontroller
model. The board layout is compatible with commonly used XIAO-
format microcontrollers, including Seeed Studio’s XIAO series and
Adafruit’s QT Py boards, allowing designers to swap controllers
depending on application needs, supported protocols, or power
constraints. The choice of choice XIAO ESP32-C6 specifically is to
enable Wi-Fi connectivity and lightweight network communica-
tion.
To drive the ultrasonic transducer, MistMaker implements a
high-voltage step-up circuit based on a tapped inductor configured
as a resonant LC circuit. A dedicated driver IC feeds the inductor,
stepping up the input voltage to approximately 130 Vpp to excite the
ultrasonic disc. A MOSFET-based switching stage sets the operating
frequency at 108.7 kHz, matching the resonant frequency of the
selected commercial ultrasonic mist disc. We selected a perforated
ultrasonic disc designed for efficient aerosolization, drawing on
prior work that demonstrates how disc geometry and resonance
characteristics influence mist generation efficiency and stability[4].
To best support creative uses of mist, the board includes battery
charging circuitry, support for multiple power input sources, and
current sensing to monitor system load. Brownout detection is used
to protect the system from unstable operating conditions, helping
to ensure reliable mist generation and extend hardware longevity
during prolonged or experimental use. These mechanisms allow the
system to detect unstable operating conditions caused by battery
voltage sag and fluctuating loads from inconsistent water draw. This

Taken together, these design choices position MistMaker as a
flexible hardware substrate for environmental interaction. By com-
bining network-capable microcontroller, high-voltage ultrasonic
actuation, and robust power management in an open and modular
form, the system enables mist to be addressed programmatically
and integrated into higher-level control pipelines, including API-
mediated interaction with large language models.
2.2 LLM Implementation
LLMMM integrates large language models through an API-mediated
listening and interpretation pipeline rather than continuous au-
tonomous control. The system is designed to periodically listen
to its surrounding context, capture spoken or ambient verbal in-
put, and translate this input into text before engaging an LLM for
interpretation.
Audio input is first processed through a speech-to-text model
(e.g., Whisper), which converts spoken language into textual de-
scriptions suitable for downstream interpretation. This transcrip-
tion step functions as a boundary between the physical environ-
ment and the language model, allowing environmental context to
be represented in a form that can be selectively filtered, summa-
rized, or structured before being passed to the LLM via API calls.
The transcribed text, along with additional system context such as
recent mist behavior or temporal cues, is incorporated into prompts
sent to the LLM. Prompt design emphasizes contextual reading
command execution, guiding the model to interpret the situation
and generate qualitative response intents.
Different interaction behaviors (or system “personalities”) are
achieved by varying prompt structure and instruction framing. Be-
cause the underlying system architecture separates interpretation
from mist events, the same MistMaker hardware can support mul-
tiple behavioral configurations without changes to firmware or
circuitry. In early iterations, LLMMM was configured to introduce
mist in response to conversational contexts described as sparse
or “dry, ” using mist as a way to enrich the ambient atmosphere.
In later iterations, the system was instead designed to respond to
conversational content indicating heightened tension or conflict
by modulating mist output, introducing a physical atmospheric
presence as a nonverbal response.
3 Hardware Pilots
3.1 Workshop at Open Hardware Summit
The first pilot took place as a hands-on workshop at the Open
Hardware Summit 2025 in Edinburgh, Uk[12], where participants
assembled MistMaker devices and experimented with mist genera-
tion in a short, guided session 2. This was the firs time MistMaker is
presented to the public. The workshop specifically emphasized leg-
ibility of the circuit, accessibility of the components, and hands-on
understanding of how ultrasonic mist is generated and controlled.
This deployment served two roles in relation to LLMMM. First,
it validated MistMaker as a reusable and teachable hardware sub-
strate, capable of supporting diverse experimental use without
requiring bespoke engineering expertise. This infrastructural ro-
bustness is a prerequisite for later LLM-mediated interaction, which

relies on stable and predictable actuation. Second, participants’ ex-
ploratory use of mist—treating it alternately as decoration, signal,
atmosphere, or performative element—highlighted the ambiguity
and interpretive openness of mist as a medium. These observations
directly informed LLMMM’s design choice to treat LLM output not
as explicit commands, but as qualitative intents that modulate mist
behavior.
3.2 Holiday Market at NYC Resistor
The second pilot occurred in NYC Resistor’s public-facing holiday
market on Dec 14, 2025 in Brooklyn New York. For the market, Mist-
Maker was developed as a standalone ornament where the package
serves as the water reservoir. The goal is to have MistMaker en-
countered by a broader audience with or without technical framing.
This deployment exposed how mist operates in socially shared en-
vironments, where interpretation is distributed across observers
but not a single user. Visitors often speculated about the meaning,
cause, or intention behind the mist without explicit explanations.
Many expressed interests in the novelty form of ornament as well
as the MistMaker itself.
One maker, Xiaotian Zhang, who encountered and acquired the
MistMaker at the holiday market, designed a classical Diskobólos
figure with MistMaker that would produce a brief puff of mist from
behind2 and envisioned a version that can do so in response to
specific conversational cues. The project illustrates a humorous,
bodily metaphor for environmental interaction.
For the MistMaker, these reactions and responses from the hol-
iday market further underscores mist’s capacity to function as
a nonverbal, non-instructional medium—an insight that directly
shaped LLMMM’s interaction framing. We took the inspiration
from the holiday market, and, as a result, we designed LLMMM
as an interpretive mediator that produces atmospheric modulation
suited to shared, ambiguous contexts.

4 Discussion
4.1 MistMaker As Community-developed
Hardware Infrastructure
Unlike commercial mist modules that usually conceal mist-making
mechanisms within sealed appliance architectures, open-sourcing
MistMaker exposes all mechanisms at the board level with a sup-
porting Arduino library. We do not claim that MistMaker has the
most novel technical contribution but aim to close the gap of in
fine-grained programmability and reproducibility in mist-making
hardware across existing deployments that center creative commu-
nities.
Our work on MistMaker builds on a lineage of community-
developed hardware toolkits that expose environmental phenom-
ena as programmable interaction materials. Notable examples in-
clude Programmable Air [13] and FlowIO [20], both of which have
been widely adopted across HCI, design, and embodied interac-
tion research. These toolkits have enabled a range of interactive
and creative systems that employ airflow and pneumatic actuation
as expressive media [15–17, 25]. Their impact lies in their ability
to lower the technical and conceptual barriers to working with
otherwise inaccessible physical phenomena.platform.
We argue that such toolkits function not only as technical in-
frastructure, but as epistemic infrastructure: they shape what kinds
of interactions can be imagined, prototyped, and studied within
the field. By making environmental actuation legible and reusable,
Programmable Air and FlowIO have enabled air and pressure to
become recognized interaction media in HCI. MistMaker occupies
a parallel role for ultrasonic mist, a medium that has remained
largely confined to appliance-level use despite its expressive po-
tential. In doing so, MistMaker challenges the implicit boundary
between functional environmental control and interaction design,
positioning mist as a first-class material for inquiry rather than a
background effect. This reframing is not only technical but con-
ceptual: by stabilizing mist as an interaction medium, MistMaker
creates the conditions under which computational systems (human
or artificial) can participate in atmospheric modulation.
4.2 LLMMM As Bridge for LLM and
Environmental Interaction
While simple rule-based mappings (e.g., keyword-to-mist triggers)
could produce comparable low-resolution actuation, LLMMM in-
tentionally explores interpretive indirection rather than determin-
istic control. The contribution of the LLM is not about providing
fine-grained precision in mist output, but AI’s agentic ability to in-
terpret ambiguous, evolving, and socially contextual inputs without
predefined symbolic mappings. LLMMM treats mist as an inter-
pretive compression of high-dimensional language contexts into
low-resolution atmospheric modulation. This asymmetry is inten-
tional: it foregrounds ambiguity and shared interpretation rather
than explicit semantic encoding.
Prior work on abstract, ambient, and environmental interaction
demonstrates how non-symbolic outputs can support interpreta-
tion and experience without relying on discrete mappings or legible
representations [2, 7, 14]. Building on MistMaker’s role as environ-
mental infrastructure, LLMMM explores what it means for large
language models to participate in atmospheric interaction. In do-
ing so, LLMs in LLMMM operate as interpretive mediators that
translate contextual inputs into high-level control intents that is ne-
gotiated by more abstract and ambigous human intentions, such as
tone, context, and emotions. This indirection positioning of LLMs as
interpretive layers between ambiance sensing and physical changes
is particularly appropriate for atmospheric media[1, 3, 8].
By connecting LLMs to atmospheric outputs such as mist, our
work moves multimodal interaction beyond representational modal-
ities and into environmental and material domains that are diffuse,
transient, and difficult to discretize. This shift challenges prevailing
notions of multimodality that privilege legibility and symbolic map-
ping, and instead foregrounds experiential modulation as a valid
form of AI-mediated interaction. In this framing, the contribution
of LLMs is not precision or autonomy, but their capacity to inter-
pret ambiguous contexts and produce qualitative environmental
responses.
We argue that environmental media offer a productive site for
rethinking AI agency in HCI precisely because they add interpretive
layers to physical interaction. When control outcomes are atmo-
spheric, questions of authorship, intent, and responsibility become
more about how meaning is constructed through perception and in-
terpretation. Here, MistMaker or any physical platform that provide
access to LLM layer access to the physical world serves as a critical
probe into how LLMs might participate in shared environments by
introducing new interpretive modalities.
5 Challenges and Limitations
MistMaker face constraints directly relate to component sourcing
and long-term reliability. Specifically, the current design leverages
a three-legged tapped inductor with different coil counts3, while
common, lacks unified code and global standards. Not only does
the unique components choice creates problem for accessible and

sustainable development, but the unstandardized hardware is un-
stable for future developments and iterations. We are still actively
seeking for solutions for this issue.
From an interaction standpoint, LLM-guided environmental con-
trol introduces ambiguity around system agency and intent. Be-
cause mist output is atmospheric and non-symbolic, users may find
it difficult to infer why particular behaviors occur or what contex-
tual factors triggered them. While this ambiguity aligns with mist’s
role as a peripheral and interpretive medium, it also challenges
conventional expectations of legibility and feedback in interactive
systems.
Although the resolution difference in high-cost language models
and low-cost mist output is intentional, the lack of alternative
contextual information is a limitation that cannot be understated.
LLMMM’s reliance on transcribed language as a the only input
signal limits the language model’s capacity to respond to non-verbal,
embodied, or purely environmental cues. This limit constrains how
fully atmospheric context can be represented within the current
pipeline.
Additionally, reliance on API-based LLM services introduces la-
tency, network dependency, and occasional unpredictability, which
can disrupt timely environmental response. The latency occasion-
ally may signify the asymmetry and indirectness of the mist output
cued by AI agents in terms of user experience. Such delays limit
the design feasibility of continuous or tightly coupled interaction
and require the interaction to tolerate delays, partial failures, or
inconsistent interpretation. One potential direction for mitigation
is to pre-train and deploy a smaller onboard model capable of light-
weight contextual interpretation. Although such a model would be
more limited in expressive range and reasoning capacity, it could
reduce latency and network dependence while preserving the in-
terpretive framing central to LLMMM.
Taken together, these challenges suggest that environmental
modalities, such as mist, demand different designs, evaluations, and
expectation-setting strategies than visual or auditory outputs when
integrated into AI-driven systems.
Our current presentation does not evaluate users’ responses to
or interpretations of mist behavior, nor does it attempt to assess
alignment between system intent and user perception. As an ex-
ploratory system, LLMMM prioritizes design space investigation
over user studies or systematic qualitative data gathering.
6 Future Works
LLMMM’s flexibility in AI agents’ personality design and prompt
engineering enables further exploration in different physical form
factor design to contrast or complement specific mist behavior.
Meanwhile, the flexible small board with power solution will allow
wide implementation in ranges of physical configurations, including
tabletop objects, wearable or body-adjacent devices, and spatial or
architectural installations. Exploring different form factors allows
mist to take on distinct interaction roles—ranging from intimate and
personal to shared and environmental—while maintaining the same
underlying control architecture. The exploration in physical form
may also help user interpret and understand personality design.
The LLMMM system can be combined with more sensory input
and output modules. Within the LLMMM system, MistMaker retains
available microcontroller pins and an I2C Qwiic connector, enabling
further extension through low-power sensors and output devices,
such as lighting elements, temperature modules, capacitive sensing,
etc. Our design in modularity allows multimodal LLM interaction
to be enriched beyond audio input and mist output, supporting
additional environmental sensing or actuation while preserving the
system’s lightweight and portable design.
Finally, future work should investigate methods for evaluating
interpretive environmental interaction without relying on conven-
tional notions of task performance or correctness. Under the frame-
work of MistMaker and LLMMM, we are particularly interested in
approaches that capture experiential qualities such as ambiguity,
atmosphere, and peripheral awareness. This may involve qualita-
tive studies, long-term deployments, or design-oriented evaluation
frameworks better suited to environmental and atmospheric media.
