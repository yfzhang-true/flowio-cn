Le, James Laudon, Richard Ho,  Roger Carpenter & Jeff Dean In 2020, we introduced a deep reinforcement learning method capable  of generating superhuman chip layouts1. T oday, we give this method  a name: AlphaChip. AlphaChip was one of the first RL methods deployed to solve a  real-world engineering problem, and its publication triggered an  explosion of work on AI for chip design2–16. Nevertheless, as described  in Sutton’s The Bitter Lesson17, there is often reluctance to accept the  application of machine learning to new areas, and ultimately this has  led to some confusion around our work, which we address below.   At the end of this addendum, we provide examples of high-impact  deployments for the interested reader. Pre-training Unlike prior approaches, AlphaChip is a learning-based method, mean- ing that it becomes better and faster as it solves more instances of the  chip placement problem. This pre-training significantly improves its  speed, reliability, and placement quality, as discussed in the original  Nature article and in a follow-up study at ISPD 2022 18. Incidentally,  pre-training also gives rise to the impressive capabilities of large  language models like Gemini 19 and ChatGPT 20 (the “P” stands for  “pre-trained”). Since publication, we have open-sourced a software repository21 to  fully reproduce the methods described in our paper. External research- ers can use this repository to pre-train on a variety of chip blocks and  then apply the pre-trained model to new blocks, as was done in our  original paper. As part of this addendum, we are also releasing a model  checkpoint pre-trained on 20 TPU blocks22. For best results, however,  we continue to recommend that developers pre-train on their own  in-distribution blocks 18, and provide a tutorial on how to perform  pre-training with our open-source repository23. Obviously, not performing any pre-training at all circumvents the  learning aspects of our method by removing its ability to learn from  prior experience. Training and computational resources As an RL agent (or any machine learning model) trains, its loss typically  decreases, and eventually plateaus, representing “convergence” — the  model has learned what it can about the task it is performing. Training  to convergence is standard practice in machine learning, and failing to  do so is likely to harm performance24. Performance of our method scales with computational resources  applied, a property which we explored further in our ISPD 2022  paper18. As described in our Nature paper, we used 16 workers, each of  which consisted of 1 GPU and 32 RL environments sharing 10 CPUs via  multi-processing, when fine-tuning on a particular block. Using fewer  computational resources is likely to harm performance, or require run- ning for considerably longer to achieve the same (or worse) performance. Initial placement Prior to running the methods evaluated in our Nature paper, an  approximate initial placement from physical synthesis, the previ - ous step of the chip design process25, was used to resolve imbalances  in the sizes of standard cell clusters from hMETIS 26. The RL agent  does not have access to this initial placement and does not place   standard cells. Even so, we ran an ablation study which eliminated any use of initial  placement whatsoever, and observed no degradation in performance  (see Table 1). Specifically, we skipped the cluster rebalancing step and  instead reduced hMETIS’s cluster imbalance parameter to its lowest  setting (UBfactor = 1), which causes hMETIS to generate more balanced  clusters27. This ancillary preprocessing step has been documented and  open-sourced since 10 June 202221 but appears to be unnecessary. Benchmarks In our Nature paper, we report results on TPU blocks with a sub-10 nm  technology node size. This technology node size is typical of modern  chips, but many academic papers report results on older technology  node sizes, such as 45 nm or 12 nm. Chips with older technology node  sizes differ substantially from a physical design perspective (e.g., at  sub-10 nm, multiple patterning is typically used 28, causing routing  congestion issues to emerge at lower density). Therefore, for older  technology node sizes, AlphaChip may benefit from adjustment to its  reward function. We welcome contributions from the community to  develop better-correlated cost functions for older technology node  sizes. Looking forward: AI methods transform the entire  chip design process Since its publication in Nature, AlphaChip has been used in three addi- tional generations of Google’s flagship AI accelerator, the T ensor Pro- cessing Unit (TPU). These chips have been manufactured and deployed  in datacentres all over the world. The gap between the performance of  AlphaChip and human experts has grown with each successive genera- tion of TPU, going from 10 RL-placed blocks and 3.2% wirelength reduc- tion vs. human experts in TPU v5e, to 15 blocks with 4.5% reduction in  TPU v5p, to 25 blocks with 6.2% reduction in Trillium30. AlphaChip has  also generated superhuman chip layouts for blocks used in datacentre  CPUs (Axion) and other unannounced chips across Alphabet. Other  organizations have adopted our approach and built on it. For example,  MediaT ek, a leading global chipmaker, extended AlphaChip to acceler- ate development of their most advanced chips, while improving power,  performance, and area31. AlphaChip is just the beginning. We envision a future in which AI  methods automate the entire chip design process, dramatically accel- erating the design cycle and unlocking new frontiers in performance  through superhuman algorithms and end-to-end co-optimization of  hardware, software, and machine learning models32.
