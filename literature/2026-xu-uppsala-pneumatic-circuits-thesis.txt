thesis advances the field of soft robotics by developing a unified framework for pneumatic
circuits that enable multiplexing, logic processing, sensing, and control. Motivated by the
growing demand for compliant, lightweight, and intelligent systems in wearable and human–
robot interaction contexts, the work addresses longstanding challenges in scaling soft robotic
architectures by integrating multifunctional pneumatic valves. By leveraging the pneumatic
mechanisms—ranging from miniaturized actuator matrices and high-gain valves to sensor–
valve reflex loops and reconfigurable modular assemblies—the research demonstrates how
pneumatic hardware can serve as actuating, sensing, and computing units.
The thesis introduces several key contributions. First, a multiplexed pneumatic actuator
matrix enables   actuators to be controlled using only   signals, offering a scalable and
space-efficient solution for high-density soft robotic interfaces. Second, programmable and
reconfigurable pneumatic valves—including hot-pluggable pinch valves, normally open and
normally closed architectures, and multifunctional logic-enabled devices—facilitate Boolean
operations such as AND, OR, NAND, and NOR without physical rewiring. These valves achieve
high pressure gain, rapid switching, and seamless integration into existing pneumatic lines.
Third, the dissertation establishes electronics-free sensorimotor pathways by coupling pressure-
sensitive pouches with pneumatic logic, enabling autonomous grasping, haptic feedback, and
object classification. Finally, a hook-and-loop modular soft robotic framework enables rapid,
reversible assembly of diverse robotic systems, supporting rapid prototyping and cross-material
integration.
Collectively, this work positions pneumatic circuits as foundational building blocks for
next-generation soft robotic systems capable of embodied intelligence. By unifying actuation,
sensing, logic, and modularity within compliant pneumatic architectures, the dissertation
outlines a pathway toward scalable, adaptive, and electronics-free soft robotics suitable for
wearable devices, autonomous manipulation, and distributed fluidic computation.

Chapter 1: Introduction 
Robotics and wearables have attracted significant  attention in both research 
and society for decades. Their applications span diverse fields, including man-
ufacturing, rehabilitations, and healthcare . For example, robot -assisted gait 
training (RAGT) uses robotic devices with body weight support for high -in-
tensity, repetitive, task-specific practice [1,2]. In the industrial manufacturing 
section, these technologies enhance the productivity of 3D printing manufac-
turing through the use of six-axis robotic systems [3]. Traditionally, such sys-
tems have been constructed primarily from rigid electronic and electrome-
chanical components. However, rigidity poses significant challenges in appli-
cations involving fragile or force-sensitive objects, as well as interactions with 
the human body, where comfort and gentle, compliant contact are required.  
For example, in the food industry, soft grippers are employed to handle deli-
cate products without damage (Figure 1A). These grippers are typically pneu-
matically actuated and fabricated from silicone rubber rather than rigid metal 
to provide complia nce and gentle force  control. Another example is robotic 
exoskeletons, designed to enhance work performance, reduce physical strain, 
or assist patients in rehabilitation (Figure 1B). While rigidity ensures struc-
tural support, it often compromises comfort and adaptability. However, to go 
one step further, there is a growing demand for systems that incorporate soft 
materials, offering compliance, lightness, and user comfort.

tions. Inspired by biological organisms such as octopuses, which exhibit ex-

sors, and control systems.  Various actuation principles have been explored, 
including electrostatic, hydraulic, pneumatic, and tendon-driven mechanisms.

Despite recent advancements, several challenges persist in the development 
and deployment of soft robotic systems . First, achieving effective coverage 
over large anatomical regions, such as the full human body, remains a signif-
icant hurdle. For instance, the distance from fingertip to foot can exceed two 
meters, imposing stringent constraints on sensing density, actuation routing, 
and control architecture, and overall system integration . Second, the design -
to-prototype cycle is often time-consuming. This is evident in low-fidelity, 
modular hardware platforms that are essential for rapid prototyping, user train-
ing, and iterative design exploration. The need to quickly evaluate diverse de-
sign hypotheses places additional pressure on fabrication pipelines. Third, ap-
plications requiring high spatial resolution, such as haptic or virtual reality 
(VR) gloves, necessitate the use of large arrays of individually controlled ac-
tuators to approximate human tactile acuity [20]. Scaling to this level introduces 
substantial complexity in fabrication, assembly, routing, and control, and de-
mands new strategies for actuator miniaturization and addressing. Fourth, 


 
 13 
although soft robotic systems increasingly leverage compliant materials, their 
electronic control system often still depends on rigid and bulky electrome-
chanical components. These rigid elements often constrain overall flexibility  
and limit conformability to the human body. Finally, miniaturization remains 
a critical requirement across a spectrum of wearable soft robotic devices. Fin-
gertip-mounted systems must remain extremely compact to avoid obstructing 
fine motoric tasks, and control units—particularly pneumatic valves—should 
ideally be miniaturized to maintain portability and reduce the physical foot-
print of the system. In addition, integrating pneumatic valves into existing 
pneumatic systems often requires cutting and reconnecting tubing, which in-
creases labour effort and elevates the risk of leakage, increasing costs and ul-
timately compromising reliability and long-term usability.  
Miniaturized pneumatic technology for soft robotics and wearables serves 
as the foundation for this thesis, owing to several key advantages. (1) Softness 
and Conformability. Soft pneumatic systems can be fabricated from compli-
ant materials such as polymers or fabrics, enabling natural conformity to body 
contours and deformation. This property is essential for wearable applications 
where comfort and adaptability are critical.  (2) High Force Output . Pneu-
matic actuators can generate substantial forces, sufficient to provide haptic 
sensations or assist in physical movements. This capability is particularly im-
portant in rehabilitation and assistive technologies. (3) Integration of Pneu-
matic Circuits and Logic. Miniaturized multifunctional pneumatic valves al-
low pneumatic systems to operate not only as actuators but also as computa-
tion-capable platforms.  As illustrated in Figure 3A and 3B , logic-enabled 
pneumatic valves have been applied in wearable soft robotics. By incorporat-
ing pneumatic logic elements, these systems can perform operations such as 
NOT, NAND, AND, or NOR, enabling distributed control within the soft sub-
strate itself. Furthermore, pneumatic multiplexing makes it possible to control 
arrays of actuators using a reduced number of input signals, thereby simplify-
ing system architecture while maintaining rich output functionality.  (4) En-
ergy Harvesting Potential.  Conventional pneumatic systems rely on air 
pumps, which introduce noise and exhibit low energy conversion efficiency. 
Alternatives such as compressed air cartridges offer quieter operation. Addi-
tionally, integrating energy harvesting mechanisms 
[6] into the system can im-
prove energy efficiency to over 20%, outperforming electromagnetic alterna-
tives. Moreover, m iniaturization plays a critical role in enabling compact, 
lightweight, and high -performance designs. Advances in micromachining, 
high-resolution additive manufacturing (e.g., DLP and SLA 3D printing), and 
soft lithography have made it possible to fabricate pneumatic components with 
sub-millimeter and millimeter-scale air channels, and thin-walled membranes.

These techniques allow precise control of channel geometry, reducing dead 
volume and improving actuation speed.

Chapter 2: Soft Pneumatic Systems 
Soft pneumatic systems play an important role in the advancement of soft ro-
botics and wearables. Their inherent compliance allows for safe and natural 
interaction with humans. At the same time, their ability to generate substantial 
force makes them well suited for powering motion and actuation in autono-
mous soft robots. In this work, we focus on two dimensions of this technology: 
the development of pneumatic circuits and the design of modular soft robotic 
systems.
 
Pneumatic Circuits 
Electronic circuits are deeply embedded in modern technology, delivering the 
convenience, speed, and computational power that underpin everyday devices. 
In parallel, pneumatic circuits are emerging as a promising alternative within 
soft robotics. These systems may combine multiplexing methods, high -gain 
mechanisms, logical operations, and electronics-free sensing and autonomous 
control—collectively offering a pathway toward fully autonomous soft robots 
powered and controlled entirely by air.  As presented in “CMOS-Inspired 
Complementary Fluidic Circuits for Soft Robots” 
[31], pneumatic circuits pos-
sess strong potential for enabling autonomous robotic s without conventional 
electronics. When soft pneumatic valves serve as core components, these cir-
cuits can realize three foundational capabilities: sensing, control, and actua-
tion. Together, these functions emulate the core principles of biological sen-
sorimotor systems, which integrate sensing, decision -making, and actuation  
[32]. Paper III demonstrates this concept by showcasing an entirely pneumatic 
sensorimotor loop composed of a processing unit (a NC valve), a sensing ele-
ment (a pressure-sensitive pouch), and a wearable haptic actuator. 
Pneumatic circuits can be effectively analyzed and designed using analo-
gies to electrical circuits, which provide an intuitive and well-established con-
ceptual framework. Table 1 summarizes the correspondence between funda-
mental pneumatic and electrical parameters.

To illustrate this analogy, it is helpful to introduce the RC circuit model, 
shown in Figure 4. An RC circuit is a first-order dynamical system governed 
by a first-order differential equation. It consists of a resistor (R) and a capaci-
tor (C) connected in series and serves as a fundamental representation of how 
energy is stored and dissipated in a system. The charging and discharging be-
havior of the capacitor closely parallels the inflation and deflation dynamics 
of pneumatic chambers in soft robotic actuators. During charging, the capaci-
tor gradually accumulates electrical energy, analogous to the progressive

pressurization of a pneumatic actuator. Conversely, during discharging, the 
capacitor releases stored energy, mirroring the controlled depressurization ob-
served in pneumatic networks.

constant τ, which indicates the rate at which the capacitor charges or dis-
charges. A smaller time constant indicates a faster response, allowing the sys-
tem to reach its steady state quickly, whereas a larger time constant results in 
a slower response, delaying the approach to steady state. This parallel enables 
engineers to predict transient behavior, tune actuation speed, and design pneu-
matic systems with targeted dynamic performance—without relying solely on

In electrical systems, the resistance R and capacitance C together determine 
the rate at which voltage changes over time. A similar relationship exists in 
pneumatic systems: the flow resistance within the channels and the pneumatic 
capacitance—arising from the actuator’s elasticity and its capacity for volume 
change as well as the compressibility of the air—govern the speed of inflation 
and deflation. Higher flow resistance slows the intake or release of air, while 
greater compliance increases the volume of air required to reach a desired 
pressure level. Consequently, the pneumatic time constant plays a critical role 
in shaping the dynamic response of soft actuators, much like the electrical time 
constant does in RC circuits.

Multiplexing 
Sometimes, soft robotic systems, and especially wearable haptic devices, re-
quire a large number of actuators to achieve high -resolution tactile feedback 
[10]. Consequently, conventional pneumatic implementations typically involve 
numerous valves and air channels, which can lead to complex and resource-in-
tensive control architecture. Multiplexing technology provides a potential so-
lution to this challenge by substantially reducing the number of control signals. 
In essence, multiplexing consolidates multiple input signals into a single 
transmission stream (Figure 5A), while demultiplexing restores the original 
signals at the output. These techniques, widely used in telecommunications 
and electronics for managing multiple data streams over shared channels, have 
recently been adapted for microfluidic and pneumatic systems. By leveraging 
multiplexing, soft pneumatic systems can achieve scalable and efficient con-
trol, paving the way for more compact designs.  
A seminal example is the work by Quake’s research group  
[23], published 
in Science (2000), which demonstrated pneumatic -based multiplexed valve 
control for large-scale microfluidic integration. Their system operated 1,024 
fluidic flow channels using only 20 control lines, leveraging a logarithmic 
scaling principle: 2 log2 n, where n represents the number of control channels. 
This breakthrough inspired extensive research into multiplexing for microflu-
idic systems [35]. It advances the designs where 19 fluidic channels were con-
trolled using only four signals by manipulating actuator size and pressure pa-
rameters, achieving superior efficiency in signal -to-channel ratios. Building 
on this concept, R. Wood et al. proposed a fluidic demultiplexer for large ar-
rays of soft actuators [24], introducing parallel structures and logic elements 
such as NOT gates to halve the control signal requirements. 
Paper I introduces a novel multiplexing strategy based on a crossed matrix 
configuration (Figure 5B). Unlike the parallel structures which are predomi-
nantly fabricated using soft lithography, the proposed actuator matrix employs 
a 3D sacrificial microchannel technique. This approach enables the integration 
of an additional patterned plastic film within the matrix to constrain downward 
expansion and thereby enhance upward actuation. In this context, “upward” 
refers to the side opposite the plastic film. The film is perforated with holes to 
improve compatibility and bonding with the soft actuator matrix, ensuring 
structural integrity and functional performance. Detailed fabrication proce-
dures are provided in the Materials and Methods section. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 5B, the crossed matrix consists of four rows and four columns, respec-
tively, forming 16 intersection points controlled by only eight signals (4 × 2). 
This design significantly reduces the number of control inputs compared to 
traditional one valve t o one output systems. Paper I relies on a cumulative

“Soft” Pneumatic Valves 
Unlike conventional rigid pneumatic valves that are typically engineered for 
a single control role, “soft” pneumatic valves leverage material compliance to 
achieve multifunctionality and versatility. These systems encompass a wide 
range of capabilities, including fundamental pneumatic valve  operation [39], 
physically responsive soft valve [40], high-gain amplifier [41], logical functions 
[21,22,49,31,42–48], analog control [31,47,50,51], random-access memory [52], pneumatic 
computing [53], autonomous robots [54], and electronics-free sensing. Beyond 
these functionalities, soft pneumatic valves are characterized by their inherent 
softness, which aims to minimize structural rigidity and enhance compliance. 
Design Concept 
Papers II, III, and IV introduce two distinct pinch-valve designs. Paper II 
presents a hot-plugging pneumatic valve, whereas Papers III and IV describe 
a multifunctional pneumatic valve architecture. These designs adopt a modu-
lar approach by decoupling the control unit from the operational unit, thereby 
enabling independent regulation of control pressure and supply pressure and 
facilitating integration with diverse fluidic source. In this work, c ontrol units 
are fabricated from elastomeric materials, while operational units employ thin 
silicone tubing. For the closing mechanism, sharp ridge was strategically in-
corporated to achieve low control pressure requirements while maintaining 
high supply pressure capacity within the operation tubing. This design choice 
optimizes actuation efficiency and enhances overall system performance. 
High-Gain Mechanism 
The concept of high gain , traditionally associated with electronics as an am-
plification factor, is less frequently explored in pneumatic systems, where gain 
is defined as the ratio between output pressure (Po) and supply pressure (Ps).  
In this context, the design  presented in Paper II leverages a sharp -ridge 
mechanism (Figure 6A) to pinch the tube to close it, then achieve mechanical 
amplification, thereby enabling high gain.  Specially, a relatively low input 
pressure can effectively control a higher output pressure, transforming a basic 
pneumatic valve into a pneumatic amplifier. This functionality is particularly 
advantageous in scenarios where direct control of high-pressure systems poses 
challenges related to sealing integrity, material constraints, and operational 
reliability. By employing low -pressure actuation for high -pressure compo-
nents, the system simplifies handling and enhances durability.  As shown in 
Figure 6B, the gain can be adjusted by modifying the distance between the 
two control units , allowing it to reach values of eight . Consequently, this

approach opens new avenues for developing efficient, responsive, and scala-
ble pneumatic architectures.

Hot-Plugging Function 
In electronics, hot -plugging—often colloquially referred to as “plug -and-
play” —denotes the capability to add or remove components from an operat-
ing system without shutting down or rebooting. Typical examples include 
USB peripherals (e.g., keyboards) that can be connected to a computer while 
powered and actively running. By analogy, the hot-plugging functionality in-
troduced in Paper II extends this concept to pneumatic systems, enabling the 
insertion and removal of valve elements without cutting, and re-routing. 
The motivation of this concept is that soft pneumatic systems increasingly 
rely on continuous pneumatic supply lines that are sensitive to leakage. Tra-
ditional integration of new components (e.g., valves) often requires breaking 
and re-making connections, which can lead to process interruptions. 
A hot- plugging pneumatic valve mitigates these drawbacks by enabling 
real-time, non -invasive integration and reconfiguration, thereby improving 
maintainability, system uptime, and experimental throughput. 
Design Concept and Architecture 
The hot-plugging valve designed in Paper II is capable of hot-plugging, and 
its control units and operational units are hydraulically/pneumatically isolated. 
This decoupled architecture yields several advantages: (i) Independent control 
and supply pressures . The control pressure acts solely on the elastomeric 
mechanism (e.g., a membrane or sharp-ridge indenter), whereas the supply 
pressure remains confined to the operational tubing. (ii) Modular integration. 
The valve can be positioned at any point along the tubing and engaged/disen-
gaged without severing or reconnecting lines. (iii) Compatibility with multiple 
fluid sources. Because control and supply are independent, diverse sources 
(compressed air, inert gases, or bio -compatible fluids) can be used without 
cross-contamination. (iv) Closing mechanism. A sharp-ridge geometry is em-
ployed to include a localized pinch, achieving full occlusion at comparatively 
low control pressures while tolerating high supply pressures within the tubing.  
Logical Functions 
Logic gates—NOT, AND, OR, XOR, NAND, NOR, XNOR  (Figure 7A)—
provide a set of operations needed for digital computation and decision-mak-
ing. While traditionally implemented in electronics, logic circuits are not ex-
clusive to electrical domains: fluidic and pneumatic analogs can realize equiv-
alent logical behavior using pressure (or flow) as the signal variable.

Reconfigurable vs Programmable Pneumatic Logic 
Most existing implementations rely on re configurable valves, where distinct 
logic functions are obtained by physically altering input connections (re-wir-
ing). While simple and robust, configurability limits adaptability, as each 
functional change requires a mechanical reconfiguration.  In contrast, a pro-
grammable pneumatic logic gate supports dynamic reconfiguration of its truth 
table by varying control pressures without changing physical connections. 
Programmability enhances responsiveness, reduces mechanical overhead, and 
enables multifunctional components.

Sensing and Control 
Pneumatic circuits can be endowed with sensing capabilities by exploiting the 
mechanics and fluid dy namics of embedded channels and compliant struc-
tures, thereby avoiding reliance on electrically sensitive materials [26]. Papers 
III and IV open complementary pathways in soft robotic systems: (i) Sensing 
using pneumatic valves. A soft pneumatic valve can operate as a threshold or 
analog sensor, producing a measurable (open/closed or partially throttled)  in 
response to pressure inputs. (ii) Electronics-free sensing and processing. Be-
yond single-function sensing, pneumatic components can be combined to re-
alize more complex behavior such as signal conditioning, processing, and re-
flex-like actuation. This configuration constitutes a pneumatic analog to bio-
logical sensorimotor reflex arc  and enables fully soft computation without 
electronics (see Figure 11). (iii) Electronics-free autonomous sensing and con-
trol, enabling a reflex -like response to external stimuli . The integrated pres-
sure-sensitive pouches and pneumatic valve form a fully passive sensor-valve 
system that autonomously adjusts internal pressure based on objects, enabling 
electronics-free grasping and force regulation. This pneumatic feedback loop 
allows the gripper to sense, decide, and modulate its behavior without any 
external electronics. (iv) Object classification. By linking pressure- sensitive 
pouches to the valve system, the system generates object- specific pressure-
position signatures that can be classified.  
Somatic Reflex Arc 
The somatic reflex arc is a biological pathway comprising three key elements: 
a sensory receptor, an integrating center, and an effector (Figure 11A). It ena-
bles the body to respond rapidly and automatically to external stimuli without 
requiring conscious thought. For example, if you touch an extremely hot sur-
face, thermal receptors in your skin immediately detect the harmful stimulus 
and transmit a signal to the spinal cord, where the integrating center processes 
the information almost instantaneously. Instead of waiting for the brain to in-
terpret the signal, the spinal cord directly activates motor neurons, triggering 
your arm muscles to contract and pull your hand away. In Paper III, a pneu-
matic analog of this structure can be formulated as follows: (i) Pneumatic sen-
sor. A deformable pneumatic channel or cavity serves as the receptor by trans-
ducing mechanical stimuli, such as contact, or external load, into a pressure 
signal. As the structure deforms, the internal pressure changes, providing a 
direct pneumatic representation of the mechanical input. A pressure-sensitive 
pouch is considered as the pneumatic sensor. (ii) Integrating center. A pneu-
matic valve functions as the integrating center, processing the incoming signal 
and applying thresholding, amplification, or logic -based decision rules. 

 
 33 
Depending on its configuration, the valve can mimic simple reflex-level com-
putations. (iii) Actuator. The actuator can be realized by a soft pneumatic ac-
tuator—such as a bending module, bellows chamber, or inflatable gripper fin-
ger. This actuator responds directly to the processed pneumatic signal, execut-
ing the corresponding mechanical action. Unlike conscious, voluntary de ci-
sion-making processes that require complex computation and high -level 
control, the reflex arc produces rapid, protective, or stabilizing responses. Its 
pneumatic counterpart similarly reduces the computational burden on central-
ized controllers by enablin g local, materials -level decision -making, fully 
without electronics (Figure 11B). This embedded, decentralized mode of op-
eration highlights the potential of pneumatic systems to support autonomy and 
embodied intelligence in soft robotics.

Chapter 3: Materials and Methods 
Fabrication 
Soft robotics encompasses a wide range of fabrication techniques, such as 
photolithography and PDMS casting using 3D -printed molds. In this work, 
however, we adopt a fabrication strategy that prioritizes the use of sacrificial 
materials and layered molds to construct monolithic structures composed en-
tirely of a single material, as used in Papers I, III, IV.  
The incorporation of sacrificial materials offers several advantages. Most 
notably, it facilitates rapid prototyping and ensures that the final structure is 
monolithic, minimizing the need for adhesives to seal individual components 
of the main body. This is particularly critical in soft robotics, where airtight-
ness and mechanical integrity are essential. Traditionally, silicone adhesives 
such as Elastosil A07 have been employed for sealing  or attaching compo-
nents, a process that typically requires several hours to cure at room tempera-
ture. By eliminating this step, the overall fabrication process becomes signif-
icantly more efficient.  Furthermore, the layered mold technique provides a 
straightforward and versatile approach for achieving complex geometries, in-
cluding the integration of internal features such as intricate channel networks 
or functional elements.

Chapter 4: Discussion and Future Respective 
This PhD thesis in vestigates soft robotic systems, with a particular focus on 
pneumatic technologies, encompassing both pneumatic circuit architectures 
and modular soft robotic platforms. Analogous to the role of electronic circuits 
in the digital domain, pneumatic circuits hold significant potential to perform 
multiplexed control, reduce the number of required control signals, and enable 
actuation, sensing, and decision-making directly through fluidic mechanisms. 
By leveraging valve designs and circuit configurations, these systems can em-
bed computation and control within compliant materials, thereby reducing re-
liance on rigid electronics. In parallel, soft modular robotic concepts are intro-
duced to simplify design iteration, reconfiguration, and system-level integra-
tion. Modular pneumatic structures allow rapid assembly and adaptation of 
soft robots without complex fabrication steps, lowering the barrier to proto-
typing and enabling wider applicability across diverse tasks. Together, these 
innovations aim to ease the implementation of soft robotic systems by reduc-
ing hardware complexity, minimizing electronic dependency in environments 
where electronics may be undesirable, and broadening the functionality achiev-
able through fully pneumatic means. As a result, this thesis advances soft pneu-
matic robotics toward practical deployment in applications such as wearable 
soft robotics, autonomous soft robots, and embodied sensorimotor pneumatic 
systems.
 
 
Paper I introduces a multiplexed pneumatic control strategy that significantly 
reduces the number of input lines required to address large arrays of soft pneu-
matic actuators. The method employs a three-dimensional matrix architecture 
composed of intersecting row and column channels. When a specific row and 
column are simultaneously pressurized, their cumulative effect generates a lo-
calized actuation point at the intersection. This mechanism enables an output 
scaling of n
2 actuators using only 2n control signals, representing a substan-
tial improvement in control efficiency for high -density soft robotic systems. 
By leveraging cumulative actuation at matrix cross -points, this technique 

 
 42 
allows soft actuator arrays to be constructed with far fewer pneumatic connec-
tions, reducing both hardware complexity and fabrication effort. 
Paper II-IV together introduce two generations of pneumatic valves that ex-
pand the functional capabilities of soft pneumatic circuits. Paper II presents 
a hot-plugging pneumatic valve designed for seamless integration into exist-
ing pneumatic networks without cutting or reconnecting tubing. This architec-
ture enables rapid reconfiguration, supports high pressure gain, and provides 
programmable logic behavior by adjusting control pressures. Papers III and 
IV build upon this concept by developing a multifunctional pneumatic valve 
platform capable of both reconfigurability and programmability. These valves 
are available in two configurations —normally open and normally closed —
allowing them to execute Boolean logic operations such as AND, OR, NAND, 
and NOR. Beyond logic processing, the multifunctional valves can also serve 
as sensing and control elements, enabling fully pneumatic sensorimotor re-
sponses and autonomous behaviors.
 
 
Paper V introduces a straightforward approach for constructing modular soft 
robots using hook-and-loop tapes as the primary connection mechanism. This 
method provides a reversible, lightweight, and compliant fastening strategy 
that avoids the rigidity, complexity, or irreversibility associated with mechan-
ical, magnetic, or adhesive connectors. By enabling rapid assembly and disas-
sembly of soft robotic modules without tools or curing steps, the tape interface 
greatly accelerates prototyping and supports fast reconfiguration of system 
morphology and functionality. Moreover, the method is compatible with di-
verse soft materials, such as silicone rubbers, textiles, and flexible plastics, 
allowing heterogeneous components to be combined into grippers, locomotion 
systems, or task-specific end-effectors within seconds. Through these capabil-
ities, it demonstrates that hook -and-loop modularity offers a practical and 
scalable platform for building adaptable soft robots, lowering design barriers, 
and facilitating iterative exploration in soft robotic research and applications. 
 
Taken together, these five papers form a comprehensive and interconnected 
body of work that advances soft pneumatic robotics across multiple dimen-
sions—scalable control architectures, embedded fluidic computation, elec-
tronics-free sensing and decision-making, and rapidly reconfigurable modular 
design. By integrating multiplexed actuation strategies, programmable and re-
configurable pneumatic logic, multifunctional sensing –control valves, and 
versatile hook -and-loop modular assembly, the thesis establishes a unified 

 
 43 
foundation for soft robots capable of adaptive intelligence. Collectively, the 
contributions demonstrate a coherent progression toward soft pneumatic ro-
bots that are scalable, embodied, and capable of operating with reduced elec-
tronic dependency—unlocking new possibilities for wearable devices, auton-
omous manipulation, and distributed fluidic computation.

Soft robots are a new generation of machines made from flexible, stretchable 
materials instead of rigid metal. Their softness makes them safer for interact-
ing with humans and better suited for handling delicate objects. However, 
even though the bodies of these robots are soft, their control systems are usu-
ally not—most still depend on traditional electronics, wires, and rigid valves.  
This thesis explores an alternative path: using only pneumatics to help soft 
robots sense, think, and move. By replacing electronic components with smart 
air-based mechanisms, soft robots can become lighter, safer, more adaptable, 
and capable of functioning in environments where electronics may fa il, such 
as underwater, near strong magnetic fields, or in wearable devices that must 
conform comfortably to the human body. 
A major challenge in soft robotics is how to control many small inflatable 
components, known as actuators, without needing a large and complicated 
network of tubing and valves. The first part of this work introduces a clever 
solution inspired by computer keyboards: a 3D pneumatic matrix that uses a 
small number of input signals to control a much larger number of outputs. This 
technique, called multiplexing, allows a grid of soft actuators to be controlled 
efficiently, making it possible to build high-resolution haptic displays, wearable 
sleeves, and other devices that would otherwise require too much hardware. 
The next part of the thesis focuses on developing new kinds of pneumatic 
valves—the essential components that control how air flows inside a soft robot. 
Traditional valves are rigid, here, two innovations are introduced. The first is 
a hot-plugging valve that can be attached or removed from a pneumatic system 
instantly, without cutting tubes. The second is a set of multifunctional valves 
that can be not only normally open but also normally closed. Both valves per-
form logic—simple forms of decision-making, similar to the way a computer 
processes information. By adjusting air pressure, these valves can switch be-
tween different logical behaviors, and because they are soft, they fit naturally 
into compliant robotic systems. 
Using these newly developed valves, the thesis demonstrates that soft ro-
bots can perform a range of sensing and interaction tasks. One example in-
volves human users interacting with a robot to grasp fruits gently and securely 
to preventing drops or damage using only pneumatic feedback. Another 

 
 47 
example showcases an electronics-free soft gripper that detects an object’s 
size through deformation of pressure-sensitive pouches and automatically ad-
justs its pressure to apply an appropriate gripping force. A further demonstra-
tion shows that a robot can "feel" and identify different objects by measuring 
unique pressure patterns produced when the gripper closes , achieving nearly 
perfect accuracy. 
The final part of the thesis introduces a practical method for building mod-
ular soft robots using everyday hook -and-loop tape (similar to Velcro). This 
approach allows soft robotic components—like bending actuators, sensors, or 
support structures—to be attached and rearranged in secon ds. With this sys-
tem, the same set of components can transform into a soft hand, a gripper, or 
even a climbing robot, enabling rapid experimentation and reducing the need 
for complex fabrication. 
Together, the contributions of this thesis show how air -powered mecha-
nisms can simplify, enhance, and expand what soft robots can do. By reducing 
dependence on electronics and embracing the natural advantages of soft ma-
terials, the work opens the door to soft robots that are more adaptive, more 
wearable, and more capable of operating safely alongside humans. These in-
novations bring soft robotics closer to the vision of machines that move, react, 
and cooperate with the world as naturally as living organisms.
